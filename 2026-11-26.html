Here is the fuller read on demand, supply and the sub-7% question.

Logan's setup. Mohtashami called Thanksgiving week "crazy": a stock rally, then a selloff, new tariffs, hot inflation, low jobless claims, strong GDP and then surprisingly firm housing data. Rates jumped from under 7% in September to 7.61% in early October. He says that shock hit demand hard, but rates have not gone higher since and have eased a bit. Purchase applications. MBA's purchase index jumped 10% for the week ending Nov. 20, the strongest pace since September, even with the average 30-year contract rate at 7.38%, a five-week high. MBA's Joel Kan said rates fell during the week, which likely sparked the jump. Peter Boockvar warned that holiday seasonal adjustments distort these numbers, so the next two weeks matter more. New homes. Logan said October new home sales beat estimates so much that his first instinct was not to believe it. He expects a downward revision. His bigger point: new home sales have been stuck between about 570,000 and 730,000 a year for a decade outside the COVID burst, because builders sell with rate buydowns that create a sub-6% market. Existing homes do not get that help, which is why resale volume is so weak. He also highlighted that builders have been cutting completed unsold inventory all year, a needed step before housing starts can rise.

The sub-7% line. He has written for weeks that the 10-year must be at 4.85% or lower for sub-7% rates, and that a small improvement in spreads would finish the job. Wednesday's close was 4.847%. But sellers face more competition than at any time since 2019 in many markets. That means more room to negotiate price and seller credits. For buyers who can qualify today, the combination of more choice and slightly lower rates is better than it was in October. For sellers, pricing right on day one matters more than it has in years.

3) ResiClub: foreclosures hit 61,900 in Q3, almost back to 2019's 64,560; 30-day mortgage delinquencies 4.0%, above pre-pandemic

So-what: Distress is normalizing, not spiking. FHA borrowers and lower-income ZIP codes are where the stress shows first, which matters for first-time buyer files.

1-minute read

Lance Lambert's ResiClub letter Wednesday charted the New York Fed's Q3 Household Debt and Credit Report. U.S. foreclosures were 61,900 in Q3 2026, up from 54,760 a year earlier and 41,520 in Q3 2024. Q3 2019 was 64,560.

The share of mortgage balances 90+ days late rose to 1.1%, from 0.8% a year ago, and is now above 2019's 1.0%. The share of mortgages moving into 30+ days late hit 4.0%, up from 3.6%, and above 2019's 3.4%.

Lambert's read: early delinquencies lead serious ones, and serious ones lead foreclosures. If nothing else changes, foreclosures likely tick up gradually over the next 12 months. A recession would change that path.

FHA loans have seen a notable spike in delinquencies over two years. They are about 12% of mortgage debt and concentrated in the South, where most new homes get built.

Plain English: this is a return to normal, not 2008. But FHA stress is real and worth watching.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is the fuller view of where mortgage distress stands.

The foreclosure trend. ResiClub listed Q3 foreclosures from the New York Fed going back to 2014. They were 112,780 in Q3 2014 and fell steadily to about 64,000 in 2018 and 2019. Then the pandemic moratorium and forbearance pushed them down to 16,000 in Q3 2020 and 9,600 in Q3 2021. Since then they have climbed every year: 28,420 in 2022, 36,040 in 2023, 41,520 in 2024, 54,760 in 2025 and 61,900 in 2026. That is about 4% below Q3 2019.

Why foreclosures stayed so low for so long. Lambert points to two things: government protections during COVID, and the Pandemic Housing Boom that pushed prices and equity to records. With equity, a struggling owner can sell instead of losing the home. That cushion is thinner now in markets where prices have slipped.

The pipeline. Before foreclosures rise, 90+ day delinquencies rise. Before that, 30+ day delinquencies rise. The 90+ share of mortgage balances was 0.4% in 2022, 0.5% in 2023, 0.7% in 2024, 0.8% in 2025 and 1.1% now. The share moving into 30+ days late was 2.1% in 2022, then 2.8%, 3.6%, 3.6% and now 4.0%, above any Q3 from 2016 through 2019. Lambert notes that credit card and auto 90+ delinquencies have surged to levels seen in the Great Financial Crisis, while mortgages only recently passed 2019.

Student loans. Delinquencies on student loans surged after reporting to credit bureaus resumed in 2025. The New York Fed found superprime borrowers with past-due student loans saw scores drop an average of 171 points in mid-2025. Lambert says that may have pushed some would-be buyers out of the market in 2026. Treasury has also resumed wage garnishment and tax refund seizures.

FHA and geography. FHA mortgages have seen a notable spike in delinquencies over the past two years. The New York Fed found the rise in delinquencies is greatest in lower-income ZIP codes. FHA is only about 12% of outstanding mortgage debt, and it is concentrated in the South.

The forecast. Based on delinquency data alone, Lambert expects foreclosures to tick up a little over the next 12 months and to gradually approach 2019 levels. A recession would speed that up.

How to read the numbers. These are national household counts from credit bureau data, so they lag a bit and blur local differences. A market with falling prices and heavy FHA lending will feel this sooner than a high-equity coastal market. ResiClub tracks metro-level delinquency in its paid terminal, which is the better tool for local calls.

Why it matters for you. For lending, the takeaway is not panic. It is underwriting care on stretched FHA files, and attention to borrowers whose credit took a student loan hit. For buyers, a slow rise in foreclosures adds a bit more inventory in some Southern and Florida markets. For content, "foreclosures back to 2019 levels" is a fair headline, as long as it says 2019 was a normal year, not a crisis.

4) Fannie and Freddie serious delinquency rates edged up in October: Freddie 0.62%, Fannie 0.63%, both up from a year ago

So-what: Conventional loans are performing near pre-pandemic norms. The stress is in FHA and multifamily, not in GSE single-family.

1-minute read

Bill McBride at CalculatedRisk posted the GSEs' October numbers. Freddie Mac's single-family serious delinquency rate was 0.62%, up from 0.61% in September and 0.56% in October 2025. That is slightly above Freddie's pre-pandemic level of 0.60%.

Fannie Mae's rate was 0.63%, up from 0.62% in September and 0.54% a year ago. That is still below Fannie's pre-pandemic low of 0.65%.

Serious delinquency means three or more payments behind or in foreclosure. Loans in forbearance count as delinquent here, though they are not reported to credit bureaus.

For scale, Fannie's rate peaked at 5.59% in February 2010 and 3.32% in August 2020. Freddie peaked at 4.20% and 3.17%.

McBride's summary: single-family delinquency is low but slowly rising, while multifamily delinquency is very high.

That keeps conventional credit risk low even as rates stay high.

Plain English: the typical conventional borrower is paying fine. The slow creep fits the ResiClub picture of normalizing, not breaking.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is more context on the GSE numbers and how they fit with this week's other credit data.

The October readings. Freddie Mac's single-family serious delinquency rate was 0.62% in October, up one basis point from September and up six from a year earlier. Fannie Mae's was 0.63%, also up one from September and up nine from a year earlier. These are loans three or more monthly payments past due or in foreclosure.

Against history. Before the pandemic, Freddie's rate was about 0.60% and Fannie's low was 0.65%. Freddie is now slightly above its pre-pandemic level, and Fannie is slightly below. Both are a small fraction of their crisis peaks: 4.20% for Freddie and 5.59% for Fannie in February 2010. During COVID forbearance they spiked to 3.17% and 3.32% in August 2020.

Multifamily. McBride's subhead said multifamily delinquencies remain very high. The detail is behind his paywall, so the exact October figures are not included here. The trend matters for apartment lenders and for rents in markets with heavy new supply.

How it fits with other data. ResiClub's NY Fed charts show the share of all mortgages moving into 30+ days late at 4.0%, above 2019, and FHA delinquencies rising fastest. MBA's Q3 survey last week showed total delinquency at 4.42% and FHA foreclosure starts at the highest since early 2017. Put together: GSE borrowers, who tend to have higher scores and more equity, are holding up. FHA borrowers are where stress shows.

Why it rises slowly. Many GSE borrowers locked very low rates in 2020 and 2021 and have large equity cushions. Even if someone hits trouble, they can usually sell or refinance their way out. That keeps serious delinquency and foreclosure low even as early delinquencies tick up.

What could change it. A rise in unemployment is the main risk. GSE serious delinquency tends to move slowly until job losses hit, then it moves fast. Weekly jobless claims at 189K say that is not happening yet. Another watch point is markets where prices are falling and recent buyers have thin equity, such as parts of Florida and Texas. Those are the first places a missed payment can turn into a forced sale. For now, the national numbers say the system is healthy, with a slow drift back toward pre-pandemic norms rather than a break.

Why it matters for you. For pricing, low GSE delinquency supports investor appetite for agency MBS, one less reason for spreads to widen. For underwriting, it is a reminder that layered-risk conventional files are still the cleanest credit in the system. For clients, it is a reassuring data point: the market is not seeing a wave of forced sales in the conventional space, so price weakness is coming from affordability and supply, not distress.

5) Cotality: non-QM share doubled in two years and 2026 volume will top 2025; 65% of stretched buyers switch financing, DSCR gaining ground

So-what: Self-employed and investor borrowers are a permanent market, not a niche. Brokers who can place bank-statement and DSCR loans win files that agency lenders turn away.

1-minute read

HousingWire covered a Cotality webinar on non-QM lending. Cotality found 69% of buyers first cut lifestyle spending to afford a home. When that is not enough, 65% change how they finance.

Chief economist Selma Hepp said non-QM is becoming a permanent part of the market because the borrowers it serves are permanent. She cited about 11.9 million independent contractors, 7.4% of employment, and 9 million multiple-job holders.

Cotality's Patrick Kiser said non-QM is on pace to beat 2025 volume this year and could grow again in 2027. Cotality says non-QM market share doubled in two years, fueled by low-doc and interest-only structures. Consumer loans are still the biggest slice, but DSCR investor loans are gaining.

Hepp cautioned that non-QM is not a simple affordability fix. Its value is matching underwriting to real repayment ability. Rising insurance, taxes and a softer job market are the risks.

Plain English: if your client earns well but their tax return does not show it, there is a growing, mainstream path to a loan.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is the fuller picture on non-QM demand and what lenders are seeing.

How buyers adapt. Cotality's research on homebuyer behavior found a clear sequence. First, 69% of buyers cut everyday and lifestyle spending to make ownership work. When that runs out, 65% pivot to changing their financing. That second step is where non-QM comes in, along with ARMs, buydowns and other structures.

Who the borrowers are. Hepp pointed to business owners, independent contractors, commission-based workers, real estate investors and households with several income sources. She cited about 11.9 million independent contractors, or 7.4% of employment, and 9 million people holding more than one job, about 5.5% of the employed. Many can repay a mortgage but have trouble documenting income through standard agency underwriting, often because tax write-offs shrink their reported income.

Hepp's caution. She said alternative underwriting should not be sold as an affordability solution. What it can do is align underwriting with the borrower's actual financial capacity. That is a useful line for client conversations: non-QM usually costs more in rate, and its purpose is to qualify the right borrower, not to stretch an unqualified one.

Volume and mix. Cotality's Patrick Kiser said nonagency lending has grown mainly because of non-QM, which is on pace to exceed 2025 volume by year-end and could grow again in 2027. Cotality's webinar notes say non-QM market share doubled in just two years, driven by low-doc, no-doc and interest-only structures. Consumer non-QM, such as bank-statement loans, is still the biggest share. DSCR loans, which qualify investors on the property's rent instead of personal income, are gaining. Other coverage of the webinar put DSCR at about 35% of non-QM production in October, though that figure is not in the HousingWire story.

Investor risks. Hepp flagged rising insurance premiums, property taxes and operating costs, which can squeeze rental cash flow and DSCR ratios. A softer labor market could also hit self-employed and variable-income borrowers.

What a loan officer sees. Holly Niemeier, a senior loan officer at NEXA Lending who works mainly with investors, told HousingWire she is busier than ever this month. Prices have dropped some, and "the rates aren't really affecting the investors. They're still buying." Her clients range from first-timers to investors buying up to 20 properties at once. Nearly all her investor business is DSCR, single-property or portfolio. Common paths are buy, renovate and refinance into DSCR, or buy turnkey rentals with tenants in place. She sees investors favoring lower-cost urban markets like St. Louis and Detroit.

Why it matters for you. This lines up with the Newrez survey earlier this week, where most originators said small-business owners do not know non-QM could qualify them. For BuyWise, that is a content and referral opening: self-employed buyers in California and across the 36 states the team serves. A clear explainer on bank-statement and DSCR loans, with honest talk about pricing, fits the show and the pipeline.

So-what: Jumbo pricing swings harder than conforming right now. With the 2027 conforming limit at $855,400, some high-balance California buyers move out of jumbo entirely in January.

HousingWire's Mortgage Rates Center on Tuesday had the 30-year conforming average at 7.53%, down 5 bps in a week. FHA 30-year fell 7 bps to 7.30%. The 30-year jumbo dropped 25 bps to 7.62%.

MBA said affordability is about 8% weaker than a year ago, though it improved slightly from September to October.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is the fuller detail on this week's rate moves by loan type.

The numbers. HousingWire's Mortgage Rates Center, published Tuesday, showed 30-year conforming loans averaging 7.53%, down 5 bps from a week earlier. FHA 30-year loans dropped 7 bps to 7.30%. Jumbo 30-year loans fell 25 bps to 7.62%, by far the biggest weekly move. HousingWire called it the seesaw pattern that has defined the second half of 2026.

Why jumbo moved more. Jumbo loans are priced by banks and private investors, not the agency MBS market. Their rates can react faster to shifts in bank funding costs and appetite for high-balance loans. That makes them more volatile in both directions. A 25 bp drop in one week can reverse just as quickly.

How it compares. These are survey averages, not daily lock rates. Wednesday's bond rally came after this snapshot. MND's daily index, which tracks top-tier conventional scenarios, was 7.26% Wednesday. MBA's weekly contract rate was 7.38% for the week ending Nov. 20. Each survey uses its own borrower assumptions, so trend is more useful than level.

Demand and affordability. The story cited MBA's prior week of applications, down 5%, with purchase apps 18% below a year ago. MBA president Bob Broeksmit said the direction of rates in the coming weeks will be key for momentum into 2027. MBA's affordability data showed payments about 8% less affordable than a year ago, slightly better than September. The most recent week's MBA data, released Wednesday, was better: purchase apps up 10%.

The Fed angle. Dan Ribler of Longbridge Financial said October's jobs report should have sent rates higher: payrolls rose 165,000 and unemployment held at 4.1%. He noted unemployment has been under 5% every month since September 2021. His bigger point was inflation: above 2% for 57 months straight. His analogy: if a kid breaks curfew 57 nights in a row and the parent keeps saying punishment is coming, eventually the kid stops believing it. Markets are now pricing that the Fed follows through in December.

The California angle. FHFA set the 2027 baseline conforming limit at $855,400 and the high-cost ceiling at $1,283,100. In high-cost counties like Orange and Los Angeles, loans up to the county limit get agency pricing starting with deliveries in January. That pulls some borrowers out of jumbo and into high-balance conforming.

Watch the gap. Jumbo at 7.62% versus conforming at 7.53% in this survey is a narrow spread, about 9 bps. When the gap is that small, a borrower just over the conforming limit is not paying much of a penalty, and shopping several jumbo investors can still pay off because pricing varies widely from one bank to the next.

Why it matters for you. For jumbo clients, compare jumbo against high-balance conforming quotes, especially for January closings. For everyone, the week's lesson is that rates are moving in quarter-point swings. Locking at a local low is worth more than guessing the next move.

10) Judge upholds New York City's first-ever rent freeze on one- and two-year stabilized leases, covering more than a million apartments

So-what: A legal green light for a mayor-driven rent freeze will embolden similar pushes in other cities. That matters for small landlords and anyone buying rentals in rent-regulated markets.

1-minute read

On Thanksgiving eve, Manhattan Judge Brendan Lantry dismissed landlords' challenge to the Rent Guidelines Board's June decision to freeze rents. It is the city's first freeze on both one- and two-year leases. It took effect Oct. 1 and covers more than a million apartments.

Owners argued Mayor Zohran Mamdani stacked the board after promising a freeze. Lantry rejected that, saying the board's 42-page statement showed it weighed every required factor. He wrote that "elections have consequences."

Plain English: rent control just survived a major court test in the biggest rental market in the country.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is more on the ruling and why the industry was watching.

The decision. Judge Brendan Lantry dismissed the landlords' challenge on Wednesday. The Rent Guidelines Board voted in late June to freeze rents on one- and two-year renewals for rent-stabilized apartments. The board had frozen one-year leases before, in 2015, 2016 and 2020, but never both terms at once. The freeze started Oct. 1 while the case was pending and covers leases on more than a million apartments.

The landlords' case. Owners of rent-stabilized buildings argued that Mayor Mamdani filled the board with loyalists after promising a freeze, making the result predetermined. They said the board followed a campaign promise instead of data on rising landlord costs. The suit was filed in late July in Staten Island and moved to Manhattan in August at the city's request.

The legal standard. Lantry wrote that the board acts in a quasi-legislative role, so its orders stand if they have a rational basis, and courts may not substitute their own judgment. He found the board's 42-page explanatory statement covered each factor the city code requires, including the industry's economic condition and the cost of living. Appointees who share the mayor's views did not show collusion.

The criticism. Lantry did not let the mayor off easily. He wrote that campaign promises to freeze rent were reckless and irresponsible, because the mayor has no authority to set stabilized rents; only the board does. He called an email in which the board's chair shared a draft statement with a mayoral aide before the vote troubling, saying it cast a dark shadow over the board's independence. Without more, it was not enough to overturn the decision under Article 78, the state law for reviewing agency actions.

The broader push. The ruling follows an appeals court decision three weeks ago that revived the city's pied-a-terre tax on second homes valued at $5 million or more. Mamdani posted that the city has billed those owners more than $500 million.

What to watch next. The board sets stabilized rents again each year, so the next vote is the next test. Landlord groups have said rising costs outpace frozen rents; the mayor's side says tenants needed relief. The ruling settles the legal question for this year's freeze, not the economic debate.

Why it matters for you. For California, rent caps already exist under state law, and local measures keep coming up. A clear court win for a freeze in New York gives advocates elsewhere a model. For investor clients, especially DSCR borrowers, rent regulation risk belongs in the underwriting conversation: a property's future rent growth may not be the landlord's call. For the show, it is a useful example of how local politics can change the math on buying a rental.
