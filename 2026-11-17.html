2) Builder confidence falls to 25, the lowest since April 2012; 46% of builders cut prices and 68% offer incentives

Wed show

So-what: New construction is a buyer's market. Builder incentives and price cuts are now the best deal in many areas, and our buyers should shop new homes alongside resale.

1-minute read

The NAHB/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index dropped four points to 25 in November. Economists expected no change at 29. It is the lowest reading since April 2012, when housing was just climbing out of the foreclosure crisis. A year ago it was 38. Anything under 50 means more builders see conditions as poor than good.

All three parts fell. Current sales dropped six points to 28. Sales expectations for the next six months fell two points to 30. Buyer traffic fell to 16, its lowest in 15 years outside the start of the pandemic.

Builders are responding with deals. 46% cut prices in November, the highest share since Covid, and the average cut held at 6% for an eighth straight month. 68% offered sales incentives, also a post-Covid high. NAHB blamed mortgage rates above 7%, high energy and material costs, and labor shortages tied to immigration enforcement. The West's three-month average fell to 23, among the weakest regions. In plain terms: builders want to move homes, and rate buydowns plus price cuts are on the table.

Expand 3-minute read

This is the clearest sign yet that the new-home side of the market has hit a wall, and it matters for how we advise buyers.

The headline index fell to 25 from 29. Reuters' poll had expected 29. This is the lowest since April 2012. All three components fell: current single-family sales -6 to 28, next-six-month expectations -2 to 30, and prospective buyer traffic -2 to 16. Investinglive pointed out that current sales have fallen 11 points since August, so the slide has picked up speed through the fall. Buyer traffic at 16 is the weakest outside of spring 2020 in 15 years.

NAHB Chairman Bill Owens said stepped-up immigration enforcement is keeping legal workers off job sites, deepening labor shortages and driving up costs. He added that higher fuel prices are pushing subcontractors to raise rates sharply, and with fewer buyers around, more builders are scaling back construction. Chief Economist Robert Dietz pointed to tariffs on materials, worker shortages, regulation, and mortgage rates above 7% holding buyers back.

The pricing data is the part to use with clients. 46% of builders cut prices in November, the highest share in the post-Covid period. The average price cut stayed at 6% for the eighth straight month. 68% offered incentives, a post-Covid high, and that share has been at or above 60% for 20 months in a row. Incentives usually mean rate buydowns, closing cost help, or upgrades.

Regionally, on a three-month average, NAHB had the Northeast at 34, the Midwest 35, the South 27 and the West 23. The West and South are where new supply is heaviest, which lines up with today's ResiClub research on overbuilt growth markets.

Context: Reuters noted the 30-year fixed is up about 120 basis points since the U.S. and Israel struck Iran in February. NAR's October existing-home sales were the lowest in more than 16 years. Builders are also carrying an overhang of unsold new homes. Boockvar's take was blunt: "Nothing that we don't already know for this most challenged part of the US economy."

A few more numbers from the NAHB release give useful color. The current-sales index at 28 compares with 34 in October, and expectations at 30 compare with 32. Investinglive noted that expectations holding above current sales means builders still hope for some improvement, but at 30 that hope is faint. Reuters added that Freddie Mac's 30-year average hit 7.40% in mid-October, a three-year high, before easing to 7.18%, and that tariffs on imported materials are adding to costs. Taken together, the builder survey and last week's existing-home sales tell the same story: demand at today's rates is thin on both sides of the market, and sellers who want to move are paying for it with price cuts and concessions.

For the show and for clients: a builder offering a big buydown can beat a resale deal on payment, even at a similar price. Buyers should compare the full package, including what the builder's lender requires, and we can run side-by-side numbers. The flip side is that weak builder demand means fewer new starts in 2027, which keeps long-run supply tight.

3) Lawler: current-coupon MBS yields jumped 40 bp last week and spreads widened 9 to 11 bp, back near the year's highs

Wed show

So-what: Last week's rate jump was not just Treasuries. Mortgage spreads widened too, so even if the 10-year calms down, mortgage rates need spreads to tighten again to fall.

1-minute read

Housing economist Tom Lawler wrote on CalculatedRisk that the current-coupon MBS yield, the yield on the mortgage bonds that set rate sheets, rose 40 basis points last week. He tied it to the hotter-than-expected October CPI and hawkish comments from many Fed officials. That jump erased almost all of the drop in yields from Oct. 14 to Nov. 5.

The spread between current-coupon MBS and Treasuries widened 9 to 11 basis points last week. That more than reversed the prior week's tightening and left spreads around the year's highs. Lawler's headline: this year's rise in rates is the "real deal."

There is a measurement wrinkle. HousingWire's Logan Mohtashami tracks the spread between the 30-year mortgage rate and the 10-year, and his chart showed it narrowing to 2.13% from 2.27%. The long-run normal is 1.60% to 1.80%. Different spread measures, different weeks, but the shared message is that spreads are still wide by history. In plain terms: there is room for mortgage rates to fall if bond investors calm down, but last week moved the wrong way.

Expand 3-minute read

Two smart trackers looked at mortgage spreads this week, and it is worth knowing why their numbers differ before using them on air.

Tom Lawler's note on CalculatedRisk focuses on the current-coupon MBS yield, which is the yield on the agency mortgage bonds lenders sell loans into. That is the most direct driver of rate sheets. He says it rose 40 basis points last week, driven by the hot October CPI report and hawkish comments from "quite a few FOMC members." That move reversed almost all of the surprising decline from Oct. 14, the day the hot September CPI came out, through Nov. 5. He also says the spread between current-coupon MBS and Treasuries widened 9 to 11 basis points, more than undoing the narrowing of the week before, and leaving spreads near the highs for the year.

HousingWire's Logan Mohtashami uses a different measure: the gap between a 30-year mortgage rate and the 10-year Treasury. His chart of the day showed that gap at 2.13%, down from 2.27% a week earlier, against a historical range of 1.60% to 1.80%. His measure moved the other way because it compares the consumer mortgage rate to the 10-year, and the 10-year itself rose a lot. Lawler's measure looks at the bond market's own pricing.

The common ground: spreads are wide compared to history. A spread around 2.1% means a 30-year rate near 7.2% to 7.3% with a 10-year around 5.15%. If spreads went back to the normal 1.70% range, the same 10-year would support rates closer to 6.9%. That is real payment relief without any help from the Fed.

Why spreads are wide now: MBS investors demand extra yield when rates are volatile, because volatility makes prepayment timing hard to predict. Last week's swings, from a near-sub-7% rally to a 7.50% Mortgage News Daily average in three days, are exactly the kind of volatility that keeps spreads wide.

CalculatedRisk's mid-November overview adds context. Realtor.com active listings were 1,183,500 in October, up 7.4% from a year ago and only 6.6% below pre-pandemic levels, the narrowest gap since the pandemic. NAR's October sales were a new cycle low at 3.75 million, with 5.1 months of supply.

One more data point for context: CalculatedRisk's overview notes months of supply for existing homes is at its highest level in more than a decade, and median prices are up only 0.4% from a year ago. With supply rising and prices flat, wider spreads are the piece keeping rates from following any relief in Treasuries. Lawler's framing that this year's rate rise is "the real deal" is a caution against telling clients a quick drop is coming. If spreads normalize, that would be good news, but nothing in last week's data suggests it is happening yet.

On the show: "rates rose because the 10-year rose" is only half the story. The other half is spreads. Calmer markets, even with a high 10-year, can still bring rates down.

4) BofA: 173 lenders now pull VantageScore 4.0, but only about 7% to 8% of conventional loans are priced with it, and Rocket and UWM do 95% of that

So-what: VS4 can price borrowers better, about 43 points higher on average when chosen, but most lenders are not set up for it yet. Ask our wholesale partners if they can price off VS4.

1-minute read

A Bank of America Global Research report using Recursion data shows lenders are pulling VantageScore 4.0 far more than they are using it. In October, 148 sellers delivered agency MBS loans that had a VS4 score pulled. In the first 10 days of November that rose to 173.

Actual pricing on VS4 is much smaller. Only eight lenders sold VS4-priced loans in September, 22 in October and 37 so far in November. In October, 20% of conventional issuance reported a VS4 score, and 39% of those used it for pricing. That is about 8% of all conventional loans. November so far is about 7%.

Rocket and UWM drove 95% of the loans priced with VS4 in October. When VS4 was the pricing score and both scores were reported, VS4 averaged about 43 points higher than FICO. BofA blames the gap on systems: pulling a second score is easy, but building it into pricing engines takes work. In plain terms: for a borrower whose FICO sits just under a pricing break, a lender that can price on VS4 may save them real money.

Expand 3-minute read

This is the first hard look at how the VantageScore 4.0 rollout is going in the actual loan pipeline, and it is a broker opportunity.

Since October, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac require agency MBS pools to disclose both Classic FICO and VS4 when both are pulled, and to say which one set the price. Bank of America's analysts used that new disclosure, through Recursion data, to see who is doing what.

Pulling VS4 is spreading fast. 148 sellers delivered loans with a VS4 pull in October, rising to 173 in the first 10 days of November. Pricing with it is not. Lenders selling VS4-priced loans went from eight in September to 22 in October to 37 in November so far.

In October, 20% of conventional issuance had a VS4 score reported. 18% had both scores and only 2% had VS4 alone. Of the loans with a VS4 score, 39% used it for pricing, or about 8% of all conventional issuance. In early November, 21% reported VS4 and 32% of those used it to price, about 7% of total.

Two lenders dominate. Rocket and UWM together were 65% of all loans with a VS4 score pulled in October, and 95% of loans actually priced with VS4. Among other lenders able to pull VS4, only 13% of production had a VS4 score reported and only 1% priced with it.

The borrower profile is telling. VS4-priced loans tend to have higher balances, fewer first-time buyers, and more cash-out and rate-and-term refis. When VS4 was the pricing score and both were present, the VS4 score averaged about 43 points above FICO. When FICO was chosen, the two were about the same. That is the "best of two" effect at work: lenders pick whichever score helps the borrower more. BofA found that among October loans with both scores, 24% had FICO below 700 and 21% had VS4 below 700, but only 17% had a pricing score below 700.

BofA's explanation for the lag: "Pulling and reporting an additional score is relatively straightforward, while actually incorporating VS4 into loan pricing likely requires more extensive changes to lenders' pricing systems and operational infrastructure."

Why the timing matters: the Fannie and Freddie disclosure rule only started in October, so this is the first month anyone outside the lenders could see these numbers. Expect the gap between pulling and pricing to keep narrowing as pricing engines catch up. BofA also noted that the best-of-two approach is already reshaping published score distributions, which MBS investors will watch closely. If more lenders begin pricing on VS4, borrowers with thin or nontraditional credit files, including some self-employed clients, could see the biggest gains. That makes this a topic worth tracking month by month, not a one-time headline.

For BuyWise: since UWM is one of the two lenders actually pricing on VS4, a borrower sitting in the 660 to 700 band on FICO could land a better LLPA tier through a VS4-priced channel. It is worth running both scores on borderline files and asking each wholesale partner where they stand.

5) FHA's insurance fund capital ratio is 10.29%, five times the 2% floor for the 12th straight year; MBA and CHLA push for lower premiums

Wed show

So-what: There is a strong case for an FHA premium cut, but HUD sounds "comfortable" and is signaling no change for now. Do not promise clients a cut.

1-minute read

FHA's 2026 Annual Report to Congress shows the Mutual Mortgage Insurance Fund's capital ratio at 10.29% as of Sept. 30. That is down from 11.47% a year ago, but still more than five times the 2% legal minimum. It is the 12th straight year above the floor.

The fund's economic net worth slipped $6.3 billion to $182.6 billion. Total capital resources rose $9.3 billion to $148.9 billion. Both forward loans and reverse mortgages (HECMs) are above 2% on their own.

That strength is fueling calls to cut the annual mortgage insurance premium (MIP). MBA CEO Bob Broeksmit said FHA "should modestly reduce annual mortgage insurance premiums," paired with safeguards against layered risk. The Community Home Lenders of America wants FHA to end life-of-loan premiums, a 2013 stopgap. But Ginnie Mae President Joseph Gormley told HousingWire the administration is "comfortable" with capital levels, signaling no immediate premium change. In plain terms: an MIP cut would lower FHA payments, but it is a lobbying push, not a plan.

Expand 3-minute read

The FHA fund report is out, and it gives the industry its best argument in years for cheaper FHA insurance.

As of Sept. 30, the Mutual Mortgage Insurance Fund's capital reserve ratio was 10.29%, down from 11.47% in 2025. The statutory minimum is 2%. This is the 12th straight year above it. Both the single-family forward program and the HECM reverse program have each stayed above 2% for six years.

The dollar numbers moved in different directions. Economic net worth fell $6.3 billion to $182.6 billion, reflecting softer modeled future performance. Total capital resources rose $9.3 billion to $148.9 billion, meaning FHA's actual cushion keeps growing.

HUD Secretary Scott Turner wrote that HUD has worked to "streamline and deregulate FHA Single Family programs" while fighting fraud and waste. Joseph Gormley, assistant secretary for housing and Ginnie Mae president, said about 150 "burdensome and unnecessary" policies were eliminated, alternative credit scoring models were put in place, and appraisal technology submissions were updated. HUD also plans to roll out a partial claim reform called a Reinstatement Advance Payment in fiscal 2027.

On premiums, Gormley recently told HousingWire the administration is "comfortable" with the fund's capital levels, which reads as no immediate changes to FHA premiums or pricing.

Industry groups want more. MBA's Broeksmit said the results make room for "targeted changes that improve affordability" and that FHA should modestly cut the annual MIP for moderate- and lower-income buyers, with safeguards against stacking multiple risk factors on one loan. CHLA said life-of-loan premiums were meant as a temporary fix after 2008 and it is "long past time to stop overcharging homeowners."

What a cut could mean: the annual MIP on most FHA purchase loans is 0.55%. Each 0.10 point cut saves about $33 a month on a $400,000 loan. Ending life-of-loan MIP would matter even more for long-term owners, since it would let premiums drop off once enough equity is built.

Timing is worth watching. FHA has cut annual premiums twice in the last decade, in 2015 and in 2023, both times after strong fund reports. MBA's suggestion to pair any cut with tighter rules on layered risk is a sign the industry expects HUD to want guardrails. The Reinstatement Advance Payment change planned for fiscal 2027 is separate, but it affects how FHA handles borrowers who fall behind, which matters for servicing and loss mitigation. For now, the safe message to clients is that FHA remains a strong option for lower down payments and lower credit scores, with today's premium as the baseline when comparing it to conventional loans with private mortgage insurance. If a cut does come, it would make FHA even more competitive against conventional for buyers with modest down payments.

For the show: a good topic, framed honestly. The fund is flush, the industry is asking, and HUD is not yet committing.

6) NAMB launches campaign against Senate bill that would nearly triple the VA IRRRL funding fee to 1.42% and make it permanent

Wed show

So-what: On a $400,000 VA streamline, the fee would go from $2,000 to $5,680. That could push some veterans past the 36-month recoupment test.

1-minute read

The National Association of Mortgage Brokers is urging mortgage pros to call their senators and oppose Section 104 of S. 4744. That is the Senate version of the Take Care of America's Veterans Act, which passed the House by voice vote on Veterans Day. The Senate bill is on the calendar and eligible for a vote.

Section 104 would raise the VA Interest Rate Reduction Refinance Loan (IRRRL) funding fee from 0.5% to 1.42%. The VA assumption fee would double from 0.5% to 1%. Both increases would become permanent, dropping a 10-year sunset.

NAMB President Michael Farrell said a $400,000 IRRRL fee would jump from $2,000 to $5,680. NAMB estimates $4 billion in added costs for veterans, about $3,780 each. It also warns the higher fee could make some refinances fail VA's 36-month recoupment rule. NAMB supports the benefit itself and backs the standalone Major Richard Star Act, which passed the House on Nov. 12, as a way to fund it without the fee. In plain terms: veterans who might refi when rates fall would pay more to do it.

Expand 3-minute read

This is a direct hit on one of the best refinance tools veterans have, and brokers have a reason to speak up.

Background: the House passed H.R. 9237, the Take Care of America's Veterans Act, by voice vote on Veterans Day. The Senate's version is S. 4744. It is on the Senate calendar and can come up for debate and a vote. The bill adds benefits for combat-injured veterans, and Section 104 pays for part of it by raising VA loan fees.

The fee changes: the IRRRL funding fee goes from 0.5% to 1.42%. The assumption fee goes from 0.5% to 1%. And both become permanent, eliminating a 10-year sunset.

NAMB President Michael Farrell put it this way to HousingWire: veterans "didn't take or raise their hand to receive a bill to pay for benefits for their fallen or disabled brothers and sisters." He said that is the job of taxpayers through appropriations. On a $400,000 average loan, the IRRRL fee goes from $2,000 to $5,680.

NAMB's numbers: about $4 billion in added costs for veteran borrowers, or an average of $3,780 per veteran. The bigger practical issue is recoupment. VA requires the borrower to recoup the costs of an IRRRL within 36 months through the payment savings. A bigger fee means bigger costs to recoup, so some refinances that work today would fail the test.

NAMB's alternative is the bipartisan Major Richard Star Act (H.R. 2102 / S. 1032), introduced by Rep. Gus Bilirakis and Sen. Richard Blumenthal. It would let about 54,000 combat-injured military retirees get both full retirement pay and VA disability pay. The House voted 245-175 on Veterans Day to discharge it and passed it on Nov. 12.

A quick example shows the recoupment problem. Say a veteran with a $400,000 balance can lower the rate enough to save $150 a month. At today's 0.5% fee, the $2,000 fee is recouped in about 13 months, well inside 36. At 1.42%, the $5,680 fee alone takes almost 38 months to recoup, before counting title, appraisal or other closing costs. Under VA's rules, a refinance like that may not qualify unless the savings are bigger. So the fee change does more than raise costs. It narrows who can use the IRRRL at all, right when many veterans are waiting for rates to come down.

Why it matters for BuyWise: with rates near 7.2% to 7.5%, many veterans who bought in 2023 to 2025 are prime IRRRL candidates whenever rates fall. A fee near 1.42% shrinks the savings and could disqualify some of them. Assumptions also matter more as buyers hunt for low-rate VA loans to take over. If Josh wants to weigh in, NAMB's campaign page has the contact tools.

7) Ackman says Trump can still release Fannie and Freddie by executive action this term; skeptics point to a $328 billion capital gap

So-what: Do not expect a GSE exit to change mortgage pricing any time soon. The bigger near-term risk is policy changes from FHFA, not a relisting.

1-minute read

In a shareholder letter, Pershing Square's Bill Ackman and CIO Ryan Israel argue Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac can exit their 18-year conservatorship and relist on the NYSE before 2029 using executive authority alone, even after Democrats won both chambers in the midterms. Their road map: a new Treasury-FHFA deal on Treasury's senior preferred stock, Treasury exercising its warrants for 79.9% of the common, then FHFA approval to relist. They say the GSEs have more than $200 billion of equity and trade at about 1.5 times earnings.

The other side, reported by Commercial Observer and CRE Daily: Fannie needs about $187 billion in capital and Freddie about $141 billion. Treasury's $192 billion senior preferred stake does not count, leaving a shortfall near $328 billion. A $30 billion IPO cannot close that. Former Freddie CEO Donald Layton calls it "the trap." Mizuho cut its odds of a deal by 2028, and both stocks are down roughly 63% to 65% from their highs. In plain terms: privatization looks stalled, and Pershing has about $800 million riding on it.

Expand 3-minute read

Two very different reads on the Fannie and Freddie exit landed this week.

Pershing Square's case: In a letter to Pershing Square Holdings shareholders signed in early November, Bill Ackman and Ryan Israel wrote that "a relisting and release from conservatorship can be accomplished by the President using his executive authority without any involvement of Congress." They argue the administration has a political reason to act before 2029 rather than hand control of the GSEs to a future administration. They also cite Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent as indicating the administration will focus on the GSEs' ownership in 2027 and 2028.

Their step-by-step plan: a new agreement between Treasury and FHFA that repays or restructures Treasury's senior preferred stock, then Treasury exercises warrants for 79.9% of each company's common stock, then FHFA approves relisting on the NYSE as private companies. They say the GSEs have more than $200 billion of shareholder equity, average credit losses of only 0.05% a year over the past decade, and capital that could top $375 billion in five years. They note the shares rose about eight times from Trump's election through last year, but have fallen more than 60% this year. Pershing holds about 99 million Fannie shares (about $422.5 million) and 95 million Freddie shares (about $386.9 million).

The skeptics' case: Commercial Observer, summarized by CRE Daily and Yahoo Finance, says the push has lost momentum. Fannie's capital requirement is about $187 billion and Freddie's about $141 billion. Treasury's $192 billion senior preferred position does not count toward regulatory capital, so the shortfall is about $328 billion. Former Freddie Mac CEO Donald Layton calls this "the trap": even a strong $30 billion IPO would leave the government in control for years, which he called "a poison pill to investors." Mizuho cut its odds of a deal by 2028 by about 12 points, and as of Nov. 11 Fannie traded near $4.43 and Freddie near $3.98.

There is also a rate concern. Experts quoted in the coverage warn that removing the implied federal backstop could push mortgage rates higher, which is a hard sell during an affordability push. NYU's Sam Chandan expects small administrative tweaks, not a full exit.

One more angle for brokers. Any GSE change that raises guarantee fees or adds risk-based pricing would hit conforming borrowers first, and that is where most first-time buyers sit. Ackman's plan says it would maintain affordability, but the details of how Treasury's stake gets resolved would decide that. With the stocks down more than 60% this year and the administration quiet, the market is not betting on fast action.

Bottom line for originators: the guarantee and pricing framework is likely to stay as is through 2027. Watch FHFA policy moves on fees, credit scores and loan limits. Those will affect borrowers far sooner than any relisting.

10) ResiClub: the bigger the boom, the bigger the bust. Overvaluation, building and migration in 2022 predicted which metros cooled most

So-what: Austin is down about 27% from its 2022 peak while Syracuse is up about 33%. Boise ranked top five on both building and migration, which matters for the Crane Creek lot decision.

1-minute read

Lance Lambert went back to Q2 2022, the peak of the pandemic boom, and asked which data available then best predicted which metros would cool. Three factors stood out: overvaluation versus local incomes, homebuilding per capita, and inbound domestic migration. ResiClub combined them into a new ResiClub Housing Cyclical Index.

Overvaluation was strongest. Austin was about 45% overvalued in Q2 2022 and is now down about 27% from peak. Cape Coral was about 51% overvalued and is down about 19%. North Port was about 45% overvalued and is down about 17%. On the other end, Syracuse was only about 11% overvalued and is up about 33%; Milwaukee, about 8% overvalued, is up about 23%.

Homebuilding from 2019 to 2021 explained about 45% of the variation in price changes since the peak. Austin led at 53.1 permits per 1,000 residents, and Boise ranked fifth at 40.9. Boise was also fourth for net domestic migration in mid-2020 to mid-2021, at 32.4 per 1,000. California's big metros were among the biggest net losers then. In plain terms: froth plus heavy building plus migrants equals bigger corrections.

Expand 3-minute read

Lance Lambert's latest ResiClub PRO piece is a useful framework for explaining why some markets fell and others did not.

The question: using only data available in Q2 2022, when the pandemic housing boom peaked, what would have best flagged downside risk? ResiClub's answer is cyclical buildup, measured three ways.

Overvaluation: how far home prices had pulled ahead of what local incomes supported. The most stretched markets at the peak are many of the ones that have fallen the most. Austin was about 45% overvalued in Q2 2022 and is down about 27% from its 2022 peak. Cape Coral-Fort Myers was about 51% overvalued and is down about 19%. North Port, Florida, was about 45% overvalued and is down about 17%. Markets with little froth held up. Syracuse was about 11% overvalued and is up about 33% since its 2022 peak. Milwaukee was about 8% overvalued and is up about 23%.

Homebuilding: single-family and multifamily permits per 1,000 residents from 2019 to 2021. That alone explained about 45% of the variation in price changes since the peak (R-squared of 0.45). The top five were Austin (53.1), Cape Coral (41.9), Lakeland (41.4), North Port (41.1) and Boise (40.9). The lowest included Syracuse (3.4), Hartford (3.4), Providence (3.8) and Oxnard-Thousand Oaks-Ventura (4.9). When the boom ended, builders cut prices and bought down rates, which pulled buyers away from resale homes and added to resale inventory.

Migration: net domestic migration per 1,000 residents from mid-2020 to mid-2021. This was a weaker signal alone (R-squared of 0.12) but it helps the combined index. The top included North Port (37.0), Lakeland (35.9), Cape Coral (35.7) and Boise (32.4). The biggest losers were San Jose (-29.2), San Francisco (-29.0), New York (-18.5), Los Angeles (-17.6) and San Diego (-12.4).

Lambert's point is that a big positive demand shock in a growth market sets up a bigger negative one later. Migration slows just when prices are most stretched, and the new supply builders started at the top arrives at the same time.

One caveat Lambert notes: this is a look back, not a forecast. The cooling window is still going, and the index is a way to see which markets built up the most risk. It does not say prices must fall further. But it does give a clear reason why the national median can be flat while some metros are down double digits and others are still rising.

For the show, this is a calm, data-based answer to "is the market going to crash?" It is a local question. Coastal California saw out-migration and low building, which is part of why prices here held up. For Josh's Boise lots, Boise screened as high-risk on two of three factors, so local comps and builder incentives deserve a close look before committing.
