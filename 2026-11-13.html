This is the week the Fed's message turned clearly toward more hikes, and the last moderate voice joined in.

Williams. Speaking at a UC Berkeley Alumni Salon in New York on Thursday, John Williams said further increases are likely as the Fed works to bring inflation back to 2% "in a timely manner and on a sustained basis." He added, "We still have work to do to get monetary policy to the right place." He also said the Fed targets the rate of inflation, not the price level. In plain terms, the Fed is not going to try to push prices back down to where they were. Higher living costs are here to stay; the goal is just to slow future increases.

Why his words carry weight. Ben Emons wrote Thursday that Williams has been the Fed's key moderating voice. His speeches since September have taken about 35 basis points off rate-hike expectations, and his October interview helped pull the 10-year from 5.36% back below 5% in a single session. Emons also notes the Fed board is debating whether Williams should step down by June 2027 under an age rule. If he leaves early, the balance tilts more hawkish. Emons says the forward curve now points to more increases that could keep the 10-year structurally above 5%.

Goolsbee. On CNBC Friday, Goolsbee said, "We've been above 2% for almost six years... lately we're not making any progress, and if anything, it's going the wrong way." His key point was breadth. Inflation is showing up in categories that are not tied to oil or tariffs. That is the kind of evidence that makes the Fed stop "looking through" an energy shock.

The data behind it. Tuesday's CPI rose 0.4%, core 0.3%. Mauldin's Over My Shoulder summary of Wolf Richter's analysis notes services CPI ran at a 3.6% annualized pace for a third straight month above 3.5%, and core goods rose 0.39%, the biggest monthly gain since May 2023. What it means for your book. Fed hikes move prime, so HELOCs and many second-lien products reprice right away. Short-reset ARMs also look less attractive when the short end is climbing. Fixed rates are tied more to the 10-year, which is already pricing a lot of this in. For clients choosing between a HELOC and a cash-out refi, the math may shift as prime moves up. It is a good time to rerun those comparisons.

Expand 3-minute read

HousingWire published a long analysis Thursday arguing builders have to plan 2027 without knowing if the market has bottomed. The numbers explain why.

Resale. NAR reported October existing-home sales fell 5.5% to a 3.75 million annual pace, down 8.8% from a year ago and the weakest reading since August 2010. Inventory rose to 1.59 million, up 4.6% from a year ago. Months of supply reached 5.1, up from 4.4 a year earlier and the highest since November 2015. The median price rose just 0.4% to $416,700. Bill McBride at CalculatedRisk called it a new cycle low and noted that while inventory is still below 2019, sales have fallen even more, which is what pushes months of supply up. In October 2019, supply was 3.9 months.

New homes. NAHB data cited by HousingWire shows September new-home sales at a 676,000 annual pace, 5.3% below a year ago, with 8.4 months of supply. The NAHB/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index fell to 29 in October, the lowest since June 2012. 43% of builders reported cutting prices and 65% offered sales incentives.

Demand, not just rates. The Fed's latest Senior Loan Officer survey showed weaker demand across all residential mortgage categories in the third quarter, with standards mostly unchanged. Construction and land loans saw weaker demand and tighter standards. Cotality chief economist Selma Hepp pointed to stacked costs: high payments, tariff-driven building costs, insurance, property taxes and utilities. "The key question is not simply whether mortgage rates decline, but whether they fall enough to meaningfully improve purchasing power," she said. HousingWire adds that a household can qualify and still decide buying does not make sense, and that temporary buydowns do not fix worries about jobs or home values.

The three-way fight. During the pandemic, lock-in kept resale listings scarce and high rents pushed people toward buying, so builders had the field. Now builders compete with other builders, a growing pool of resale listings and better rental options, including concessions in new apartments and professionally managed single-family rentals. A resale seller can wait. A builder pays land, construction, financing and overhead costs every month until homes sell. That pushes builders to adjust effective prices sooner, which can then reset what resale sellers expect.

The West. NAR said the West had the biggest sales drop in October, down 9.7% for the month. That is the region where BuyWise does most of its business, and where price-to-income ratios leave the least room for higher rates. Expect more price cuts and longer days on market in Southern California through the winter.

What it means for you. For purchase clients, this is real negotiating room. Builders with standing inventory are motivated to offer rate buydowns, closing cost help and upgrades. Compare a builder's permanent buydown against a price cut, since a lower price helps the appraisal and future equity, while a buydown helps the payment. For resale, 5.1 months of supply nationally means more buyer leverage than at any point in a decade, and the West led October's sales drop.

Expand 3-minute read

This is the biggest escalation yet in the fight over private and office-exclusive listings, and much of it is in California.

The lawsuits. On Thursday evening, Compass International Holdings filed 15 suits against 15 MLSs. Each claims the MLS fines and punishes agents who publicly market an "office exclusive" listing at the seller's request without submitting it to the MLS. The defendants are bridgeMLS, Bay East MLS, Contra Costa MLS, MLSListings, The MLS-CLAW, San Diego MLS, Canopy MLS, Hive MLS, Doorify MLS, Arizona Regional MLS, REcolorado, NorthstarMLS, the Houston Association of Realtors, Stellar MLS and OneKey MLS. The claims largely track the suit Compass filed earlier against California Regional MLS.

Compass's argument. It says MLS rules that punish agents for following a client's lawful instructions keep sellers from choosing strategies like privacy-sensitive or phased exposure campaigns. It also says MLS boards are made up of Compass competitors who are dictating how it can compete. A spokesperson told HousingWire: "Only the law and the homeowner should tell agents how to market a home, and the MLS is neither the law nor the homeowner." Compass points out that seven of the top 25 MLSs, covering about 340,000 agents in 12 states, have allowed public marketing of office exclusives for years.

How we got here. Compass sent letters to MLSs in early September demanding they stop enforcing these rules. At the Council of MLSs conference in late September, CEO Robert Reffkin threatened to sue any MLS that refused. In response, CRMLS, ARMLS, OneKey, MLSListings, Bridge, Canopy, REcolorado, Hive and Doorify filed two lawsuits against Compass in federal court in the Southern District of New York, asking for a ruling that they can keep enforcing their cooperation rules without breaking antitrust law. Compass has also barred its employees from Realtor association leadership roles, and its brokerage brand announced a policy requiring its agents to cooperate with other brokers, including on Private Exclusive and Coming Soon listings, unless a client says otherwise in writing.

Why it matters to a lender. More listings outside the MLS means buyers relying on portals and MLS alerts may see fewer homes. It can also mean less price transparency, which matters for appraisals and comps. For California buyers working with smaller brokerages, it could mean missing homes that only a big firm's agents see first.

What happens next. The MLS defendants had not responded to HousingWire's request for comment. With suits now running in both directions, in New York federal court and wherever Compass filed, the courts will likely decide whether MLS cooperation rules are a fair standard or an antitrust problem. That could take a long time, and policies could shift while it plays out.

What to do. Ask your top agent partners how they are handling office exclusives and pocket listings. For pre-approved buyers, encourage them to work with an agent who has a plan to surface off-MLS inventory. And watch this as content: "Are you seeing every home for sale?" is a question your audience cares about.

8) California cities get warning letters before housing votes: CalHDF sends about 100 a year, and fines can reach $50,000 per unit

So-what: State law now makes it hard for cities to block or shrink housing that meets zoning. More approvals should mean more for-sale townhomes over time.

1-minute read

HousingWire reports pro-housing nonprofit California Housing Defense Fund (CalHDF) now sends warning letters to cities before they vote on projects, citing the Housing Accountability Act, the Density Bonus Law and last year's CEQA reforms.

Example: a 135-townhome Warmington project in Rancho Cucamonga, with seven very-low-income units, cleared the planning commission Tuesday after a CalHDF letter. The same day the group sent letters to Santa Clarita, Whittier, Escondido and Twentynine Palms. It sends about 100 a year.

The stakes are real. Under SB 1037, willful violations can cost cities up to $50,000 per unit. La Cañada Flintridge paid CalHDF about $1.26 million in fees and costs after losing a builder's remedy case.

Newsom's 2025 reforms (AB 130 and SB 131) exempt qualifying infill housing from environmental review. Neighbor lawsuits now focus on whether a project qualifies for the exemption.

For your market: more Southern California townhome projects should get through. That means more entry-level for-sale supply for first-time buyers.

California housing fights are increasingly settled before the vote, and this is good news for future supply.

How it works. California Housing Defense Fund sends letters to cities ahead of key votes. They cite the Housing Accountability Act, which limits when cities can deny or shrink projects that meet zoning, the state Density Bonus Law and the 2025 CEQA reforms. The letters warn that rejecting or reducing a project would require written findings of a specific health or safety hazard. They also create a record the group can use in court if a city ignores the law. James Lloyd, its director of planning and investigations, told HousingWire the group sends about 100 letters a year.

The Rancho Cucamonga example. On Tuesday, a 135-townhome project from Warmington Residential cleared the Rancho Cucamonga Planning Commission on staff's recommendation. Seven units are for very-low-income households. Neighbors had raised traffic, wall height and privacy concerns, and staff added conditions on construction parking and frosted windows. But staff's report tracked CalHDF's points: the project qualified for density bonus waivers and was exempt from CEQA review. CalHDF sent at least four other letters the same day, to Santa Clarita, Whittier, Escondido and Twentynine Palms, on projects of 17 to 146 units and one ADU ordinance.

The legal backdrop. Gov. Newsom signed AB 130 and SB 131 in 2025, exempting qualifying infill housing from environmental review and narrowing review for projects that nearly qualify. Lawmakers also tightened the Housing Accountability Act. Under SB 1037, penalties can reach $50,000 per unit for willful violations. La Cañada Flintridge refused to process a builder's remedy project, lost in court in March 2024, dropped its appeal rather than post a $14 million bond, and paid CalHDF $1,262,500 in fees and costs.

Not over yet. Neighbor suits have shifted to arguing whether a project qualifies for a CEQA exemption at all. In Pacifica, neighbors won a trial court ruling against an eight-townhome project and nearly $1.3 million in fees, though an appeals court vacated the fee award in December 2025 and sent it back.

Why lenders should care now. A project like Rancho Cucamonga's typically takes a few years to deliver homes, but approval is the step that used to fail most often. As cities learn that denials can cost $50,000 a unit plus legal fees, the pipeline should get more predictable. That gives builders more confidence to commit capital, even in a slow market.

What it means for you. Projects like Rancho Cucamonga's add entry-level, for-sale townhomes in Inland Empire markets where first-time buyers can still qualify. Over a few years, more approvals mean more new-construction inventory, more builder financing incentives and more opportunities for your first-time buyer clients. It also makes a good California-specific story for the show about why more townhomes are coming.

Expand 3-minute read

This is a real estate story, but it points to where AI rules for mortgage are likely heading too.

What happened. The American Real Estate Association launched an AI Task Force focused on passing "licensee-in-the-loop" laws in every state. Co-founder Jason Haber said the push was inspired by a HousingWire op-ed by Dmitriy Kruglyak, a software engineer turned broker, who argued that agentic AI could put consumers at risk by letting unlicensed systems negotiate contracts and guide transactions. Kruglyak now chairs the task force, which will meet for the first time in the coming weeks and plans white papers, policy analysis and advocacy.

What the laws would require. Haber: "If you have an act performed by an automated system on behalf of the consumer for a real estate transaction, it has to be supervised by a responsible licensee that's identifiable to the consumer with a name and license." Before any offer or counteroffer goes out, a licensee reviews it, and the AI is backed by that licensee's fiduciary duty to the client.

Why they care. Haber said, "AI has no fiduciary, it holds no license, it has no errors and omission insurance and when something goes wrong, there is no real estate board to file an ethics complaint with." His example: a consumer has no idea how an AI was trained, so an AI tuned to benefit sellers could convince a buyer to offer more. He said buying or selling solely with AI is still rare, but he expects more AI agent platforms in the next year and wants rules in place before consumers get hurt.

Timeline. There are no federal real estate licensing laws, so ARA is going state by state, using its state chairs and a federal lobbyist to find sponsors. Haber called getting even one bill through "Herculean" but expects bills introduced in some states as early as the end of this year or January. He called it "the defining issue for our industry in 2027."

The mortgage parallel. Lenders are already adding AI to processing, document review and borrower chat. If states adopt licensee-in-the-loop rules for agents, regulators could ask similar questions of mortgage AI: who is accountable when an AI gives a borrower bad guidance on a program or a rate? Building a clear review step now is cheap insurance.

What it means for BuyWise. Mortgage lending already has a licensing structure through the NMLS and state regulators, so a similar "human in the loop" idea could show up in mortgage rules too. Practical steps: keep a named, licensed LO responsible for every client-facing recommendation, even when AI drafts the email, summary or scenario. Keep records of who reviewed what. And be ready for show questions: can an AI be my loan officer? The honest answer is that AI can speed up the work, but a licensed person is still accountable.
