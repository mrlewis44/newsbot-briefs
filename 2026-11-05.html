2) Layoffs stay low: jobless claims fall to 189,000 and October job cuts are the lowest for the month since 2023

So-what: Low layoffs keep the Fed leaning toward a hike, which limits how far rates can fall before Friday's jobs report.

1-minute read

Employers are still not letting many people go, and that keeps pressure on rates.

New jobless claims fell 6,000 to 189,000 in the week ending Oct. 31. Forecasts were 196,000. The prior week was revised to 195,000. The four-week average fell to 194,750.

There is one soft spot. Continuing claims, which count people still collecting benefits, rose 23,000 to 1.718 million. That can mean people are taking longer to find new jobs.

Challenger, Gray & Christmas reported 43,994 announced job cuts in October. That is up 2% from September but down 71% from October 2025. It is the lowest October total since 2023. Andy Challenger called it "a low-fire environment." Employers are holding onto workers and adding mostly short-term holiday help.

The BLS also reported that worker productivity rose 3.6% in the third quarter, per its release headline.

Why it matters for borrowers: low layoffs give the Fed less reason to back off its rate hike plans. That makes Friday's October jobs report more important. A strong number could push rates back up. A weak one could extend this week's rally.

Expand 3-minute read

This morning's labor data says the same thing it has said for months: companies are not hiring fast, but they are not firing much either.

Jobless claims. The Labor Department said initial claims for unemployment benefits fell 6,000 to 189,000 in the week ending Oct. 31. Economists expected 196,000. The prior week was revised up slightly to 195,000 from 194,000. The four-week average, which smooths out weekly noise, fell 2,250 to 194,750. These are very low numbers by historical standards. Initial claims are the best weekly read on layoffs, so a low number means few people are losing jobs.

The soft spot. Continuing claims rose 23,000 to 1,718,000 in the week ending Oct. 24. That counts people who are still on unemployment after their first week. The four-week average rose only 1,000 to 1,709,500, so the weekly jump is bigger than the trend. The insured unemployment rate held at 1.1%. investingLive's Greg Michalowski said the rise in continuing claims "tempers that signal because it can point to people taking longer to find work." In plain English: the people who do lose jobs may be having a harder time landing new ones.

Challenger job cuts. Challenger, Gray & Christmas said U.S. employers announced 43,994 job cuts in October. That is up 2% from 43,281 in September, but down 71% from 153,074 in October 2025. It is the lowest October total since 2023 and below the 2015 to 2025 October average of 56,348. Through October, announced cuts total 617,189, down 44% from the same period last year.

Where the cuts are. Food companies led October with 9,677 cuts. Technology announced 5,576, its lowest month of the year. But tech still leads 2026 with 171,501 cuts, up 21% from last year. Challenger also noted that Microsoft offered voluntary exit packages to 60,000 employees in October. Those are not counted in the total yet. Andy Challenger said such offers "tend to signal more layoffs to come."

The quote that sums it up. "This is a low-fire environment," Challenger said. "Employers are holding on to the workers they have heading into the holidays, and the hiring they are announcing is largely short-term, seasonal work."

Productivity. The Bureau of Labor Statistics release headline said nonfarm business productivity rose 3.6% in the third quarter. Higher productivity means companies get more output per worker. Over time, that can help hold down inflation, because pay can rise without prices rising as fast. We could not open the full BLS release by build time, so we are not reporting the labor cost details.

Why this matters for rates. Bonds took this data in stride. MBS Live had UMBS 6.0 down just 0.07 shortly after the release, and CNBC showed the 10-year lower by 6:57 AM PT. But the bigger picture is that a job market with few layoffs gives the Fed room to keep raising rates to fight inflation. investingLive noted the drop in claims gives the Fed less reason to ease.

What is next. The official October jobs report comes Friday morning. That is the big one for mortgage rates this week. Wednesday's ADP report showed 86,000 private jobs added.

For clients: today's data did not change the rate picture much. Friday could. Borrowers close to a decision should not count on another big rally before the jobs report is out.

Expand 3-minute read

The election changed who holds the gavels in Congress. It did not change who runs the agencies that write most mortgage rules.

What happened:

Democrats captured both the House and the Senate on Tuesday night. President Trump retains the White House and the leadership at agencies like FHFA, HUD and the CFPB. Any housing bill now needs both Trump's support and enough Republican votes to get through the Senate.

What the industry wants:

Isaac Boltansky, head of public policy at Pennymac, said voters made affordability a central concern. He said the ROAD to Housing Act "laid a solid foundation" and called for a ROAD to Housing 2.0 package. He pointed to two ideas. One is the capital gains tax lock-in effect. Plainly, some long-time owners do not sell because the tax bill on their gain would be large, and that keeps listings off the market. The other is the Neighborhood Homes Investment Act, which is aimed at building and rehabbing homes in distressed areas.

The Mortgage Bankers Association released a policy road map in October. Its proposals include easing tri-merge credit report requirements, aligning loan-level price adjustments, and lowering the annual FHA mortgage insurance premium. Tri-merge means pulling all three credit bureau reports on every file. Fewer required reports could lower credit costs. LLPAs are the fees Fannie and Freddie add based on credit score and down payment. A lower FHA annual premium lowers the monthly payment for every FHA borrower.

The American Land Title Association said consumer protection must stay in the conversation, warning that "fraud is unaffordable."

What could slow things down:

Tim Rood of Impact Capitol said Democratic majorities will control the agendas for the House Financial Services and Senate Banking committees starting in January. They can investigate agencies, shape spending talks and withhold Senate confirmation of nominees. But agency leadership "does not change automatically with Congress." He expects more hearings, tougher confirmations and a more contested calendar, "not an overnight rewrite of the rules."

One example is Brian Johnson's nomination to lead the CFPB. An attorney told HousingWire the confirmation is now at risk as Democrats may seek more representation across financial agencies. The same attorney said changes to the loan originator compensation rule, one of the industry's top priorities, remain unresolved.

Rates:

Realtor.com's Jake Krimmel said the effect on rates is unclear. He pointed to the Iran war as a key variable. A resolution that improves oil supply could ease inflation and "potentially provide relief for mortgage rates." Boltansky expects another debt ceiling fight in 2027, which matters because the bond market sets mortgage rates.

What it means for us:

1. No quick changes. Plan on current FHA premiums, current LLPAs and current credit report rules for now.

2. Watch the FHA premium. If it gets cut, it is a direct payment drop for buyers in California and in every state we serve. That would be worth a show segment and a client email.

3. Comp rule. Any change to loan officer compensation rules affects how brokers price and compete. Nothing is moving yet.

4. Do not promise policy relief to clients. Run the numbers on today's terms and treat any cut as a bonus.

7) Florida voters pass Amendment 3 with 62%: the homestead exemption rises from $50,000 to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028, but buyers must be Florida residents by Dec. 31, 2026 for the full break

So-what: Clients buying a primary home in Florida have a hard year-end residency deadline that can change their property tax bill for years.

1-minute read

Florida just passed a big property tax break for homeowners, and the deadline is close.

Amendment 3 needed 60% and won about 62% in unofficial returns. It replaces Florida's $50,000 homestead exemption with $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028. The school board levy is excluded.

Only primary residences qualify. Second homes and investment properties get nothing. Renters, who live in about a third of Florida homes, get no relief.

Timing is the key detail. Homeowners must establish Florida residency by Dec. 31, 2026 to get the full benefit. Later arrivals get the $50,000 exemption for four years before qualifying.

The cost: state analysts project local governments lose nearly $5 billion in 2027-28. Sheriffs, firefighters and city groups opposed it, warning of service cuts and new fees.

Context: Florida home values fell 3.7% over the past year, but assessments keep rising toward market value under the Save Our Homes rule.

The so-what: a California client moving to Florida should know the Dec. 31 residency date. A lower tax bill also lowers the monthly payment used to qualify. Confirm local tax details before quoting.

Expand 3-minute read

This is a state ballot result with a direct effect on buyer payments, so it is worth knowing the details.

What passed:

Florida's Amendment 3 expands the homestead exemption. A homestead exemption reduces the taxable value of a primary home. Right now it is $50,000. Under the amendment, it rises to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028. The school board levy is excluded, so school taxes are not cut by the larger exemption.

The measure needed 60% to pass. Unofficial returns showed about 62%. HousingWire said it had been given little chance of winning, but cost of living concerns carried it.

Who benefits:

Only primary residences qualify. Second-home and investment property owners get nothing from the higher exemption. Renters, who occupy about a third of Florida's housing units, get no relief.

The amendment also cuts the annual assessment cap on non-homestead commercial property from 10% to 5%, starting Jan. 1, 2027.

The deadline:

Homeowners must establish Florida residency by Dec. 31, 2026 to get the full benefit. People who arrive later get the $50,000 exemption for four years before they qualify for the larger one. For anyone already planning a move, that is a meaningful date.

Why voters wanted it:

Florida home prices rose more than 50% between 2020 and 2022. Prices have since stalled, and the average home value fell 3.7% over the past year. But assessments have not followed prices down. Under the Save Our Homes recapture rule, assessed values keep rising until they meet market value, with the annual increase capped at 3% or inflation, whichever is less. City tax bills statewide rose about 50% on average over several years.

The other side:

The legislature's economic research office estimated local governments would lose nearly $5 billion in 2027-28. The Florida Sheriffs Association, police and firefighter groups, the Florida League of Cities and the Florida Association of Counties opposed it. They warned of fewer deputies, slower 911 response and new fees. A land use attorney said cities may now favor projects that bring jobs and tax revenue over ones that need heavy public services.

What it means for us:

1. Payment math. Property tax is part of the monthly payment used to qualify a borrower. A larger exemption lowers that number, which can help debt-to-income ratios for Florida primary home buyers. Use the actual county numbers, not a guess.

2. California movers. Many Californians look at Florida. If they plan to make it their primary home, the Dec. 31, 2026 residency date matters. They should talk to a tax professional about what establishing residency requires.

3. Not for investors. DSCR and second home buyers get no benefit, so their cost estimates stay the same.

4. Wider trend. Property tax relief is a hot ballot issue. It also shows up in the national policy debate after the midterms. We can help buyers in Florida and in every state we serve sort out what local rules mean for their payment.
