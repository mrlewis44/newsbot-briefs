Oil has become one of the main drivers of mortgage rates this fall, so it is worth understanding today's swings.

Monday's drop. Brent crude futures settled at $104.77 a barrel on Monday, down $8.66, or 7.63%, per CNBC. The day before, the brief noted a 6.4% drop in the morning. The selloff deepened through the day as markets priced in a more diplomatic path. President Trump raised doubts about resuming strikes and said a deal with Tehran could happen. Tehran said contacts with the U.S. were continuing after talks with Qatari mediators produced altered proposals but no breakthrough.

The overnight strike. The Wall Street Journal then reported that the U.S. had struck Iran's Qeshm Island, citing U.S. officials. Some described the strikes as defensive, to keep Iran from attacking commercial ships in the Strait of Hormuz. Oil rose in early Asian trading. Brent was up 0.33% to $105.12, and U.S. West Texas Intermediate crude was up 0.89% to $97.32. One analyst told CNBC the report "added a degree of risk premium back into the oil price." A risk premium is the extra price traders pay because supply might be disrupted.

The morning reversal. By U.S. morning, oil was falling again. CNBC's bond market story said December WTI was down less than 1% to about $95, and Brent was more than 1% lower at about $103. MBS Live's morning note said Iranian officials floated concessions in exchange for sanctions relief, and headlines said Washington approved a draft memorandum of understanding. Higher oil feeds into gasoline, shipping, and airline costs, which keeps inflation higher. Higher inflation keeps the Fed leaning toward hikes and keeps long-term bond yields up. Mortgage rates follow those yields. CNBC also noted Brent ended October at its highest level in six months on fears the conflict could escalate after today's elections.

How bonds reacted. Interestingly, MBS Live said bonds were "little-moved" by Monday's huge drop in oil. Bonds had already rallied in recent weeks, and supply worries from Treasury borrowing were a counterweight. This morning, the Iran headlines helped bonds recover from overnight weakness, before the strong job openings report pushed yields back up.

So what. For clients asking "why did rates move," oil is a big part of the answer this fall. We cannot predict war news, so the practical advice is the same: have a lock plan and do not wait on headlines you cannot control.

3) Treasury will borrow $618 billion this quarter and $800 billion next; full refunding plan lands Wednesday

So-what: More government borrowing means more bonds to sell, which can keep long-term rates, and mortgage rates, higher.

1-minute read

The Treasury told markets Monday how much it plans to borrow, and the number was a bit higher than expected under the surface.

For October through December, Treasury expects to borrow $618 billion from private investors, assuming it ends December with $850 billion in cash. That is $10 billion lower than its August estimate. But Treasury said that is mostly because it started the quarter with more cash. Without that cash cushion, borrowing is $24 billion higher than planned.

For January through March 2027, Treasury expects to borrow $800 billion, assuming a $1 trillion cash balance at the end of March.

MBS Live flagged the $24 billion surprise as one reason bonds did not rally more on Monday's oil drop.

The next step comes Wednesday at 5:30 AM PT. Treasury will say how it splits this borrowing between short-term bills and longer notes and bonds.

So what: when Treasury sells more long-term debt, investors want higher yields to buy it. Mortgage rates track the 10-year, so the mix announced Wednesday matters for rate sheets.

Expand 3-minute read

This is a quieter story than oil or jobs, but it is one of the forces that keeps mortgage rates from falling faster.

What Treasury announced. On Monday, Nov. 2, the Treasury Department released its estimates for "privately-held net marketable borrowing." That is the amount of new debt the government needs to sell to investors, not counting bonds held by the Federal Reserve.

For October through December 2026, Treasury expects to borrow $618 billion. That assumes the government ends December with $850 billion in its cash account. The estimate is $10 billion lower than the figure Treasury gave in August.

The catch. Treasury explained the lower number is mainly because it started the quarter with more cash than it assumed. It ended September with $984 billion, not the $950 billion it planned. If you take that cash cushion out, Treasury said this quarter's borrowing is $24 billion higher than it announced in August. In plain terms, the government is spending more than expected, and the extra cash simply hides it for now.

Next quarter. For January through March 2027, Treasury expects to borrow $800 billion, assuming a cash balance of $1 trillion at the end of March.

Looking back. For July through September, Treasury borrowed $742 billion, versus its August estimate of $739 billion. The small difference came from holding more cash at quarter end.

How the market took it. MBS Live's Monday recap pointed out the $24 billion surprise as a reason bonds were "little-moved" even as oil and tech stocks fell sharply. Normally those drops would help bonds. More supply of Treasury debt works the other way, because the government has to find buyers, and buyers want higher yields to take on more.

Why mortgage rates care. Mortgage rates move with the 10-year Treasury yield. If the government sells more long-term debt, it can push those yields up even when inflation is cooling. That is part of why rates remain in the 7% range despite a softer housing market. WSJ also reports that federal debt has hit 100% of GDP, which keeps this topic in the headlines.

What to watch Wednesday. Treasury releases its full Quarterly Refunding details at 8:30 AM Eastern, which is 5:30 AM PT, on Wednesday, Nov. 4. That will show how it splits borrowing between short-term bills and longer-term notes and bonds. More bills and fewer long bonds tends to be friendlier for mortgage rates. A bigger increase in long-term auctions tends to be less friendly. Wednesday also brings ISM services and ADP payroll data, so it could be a busy morning for rates.

So what. When clients ask why rates are not dropping faster, government borrowing is one honest answer. It is not a forecast. It is one of several forces, with oil and jobs, that decides where rate sheets land each day.

4) Election Day in California: four statewide measures touch housing, from an $11.25 billion housing bond to new-home buyer loans

Wed show

So-what: Results could change down payment help, home building rules, and local tax votes in California. Results start tonight.

1-minute read

California votes today, and several statewide measures affect housing. This is not about candidates. It is about what changes for buyers and builders.

Prop 1 lets the state sell $11.25 billion in housing bonds. That includes $7.2 billion for affordable rental housing, $1.1 billion for homeownership programs such as first-time buyer help, and $1.25 billion for veterans home loans. The state Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO) says it could help up to 40,000 households become owners.

Prop 37 lets CalHFA sell up to $25 billion in bonds for a "middle-class homeownership loan." It could cover up to 17% of the price of a newly built home. Buyers put down at least 3%, and income is capped at two times the area's typical income.

Prop 45 sets faster deadlines for environmental reviews, permits, and court challenges on certain "essential" projects.

Prop 43 would require a two-thirds vote for new or extended local special taxes starting in 2027.

So what: Props 1 and 37 could add new down payment tools in California. Watch results tonight and the details after.

Expand 3-minute read

California voters decide several statewide measures today. The brief skips the candidate races. Here are the measures with a direct housing or rate angle, using the state Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO) analyses.

Prop 1: $11.25 billion housing bond. This lets the state borrow $11.25 billion by selling general obligation bonds. That breaks into $10 billion for state housing programs, repaid from the General Fund, and $1.25 billion for the CalVet home loan program, repaid by veterans' loan payments. Of the main bond, $7.2 billion goes to build or renovate affordable rental housing, usually through low-interest loans to developers who reserve units for low-income households for 55 years. Another $1.1 billion supports homeownership for low- and moderate-income households. That includes first-time buyer help through loans that usually do not have to be repaid until the home is sold or paid off. The LAO estimates the money could help up to 40,000 households, including about 2,100 veterans, with homeownership. The last statewide affordable housing bond passed in 2024 at $2 billion, and the last veterans home loan bond passed in 2018 at $1 billion.

Prop 37: middle-class homeownership loans. This lets CalHFA, the state housing finance agency, sell up to $25 billion in revenue bonds. The money would fund a loan covering up to 17% of the purchase price. The buyer must put down at least 3%. Income is capped at two times the area's typical income. The buyer must be the first purchaser of a newly built home. Builders can opt in under a "qualified builder" path that comes with labor standards and different construction defect rules. The LAO says there is no direct cost to the state. In plain English: it is a large shared down payment tool, but only for new construction.

Prop 45: faster reviews for "essential" projects. Under CEQA, the state's environmental review law, agencies must study a project's impacts, and opponents can sue. Prop 45 creates optional new procedures for eligible projects. It tightens time limits for completeness checks, environmental reports, permits, and court cases. For example, if an agency misses a 365-business-day limit for finishing a full environmental report, the applicant can request a hearing and potentially sue. Agencies would also have to list everything required up front, instead of asking for more information repeatedly.

Prop 43: higher bar for local special taxes. Starting Jan. 1, 2027, any new, increased, or extended local special tax proposed by voters would need a two-thirds vote. Recent court rulings had allowed some of these to pass with a simple majority. The LAO says local tax revenue could be lower in the future, though the amount is unknown. Some local housing programs and transfer taxes have been funded this way, so this matters for future local measures.

What to watch. Polls close at 8 PM PT. California counts slowly, so close measures may take days or weeks.

So what for the show. Two of these measures, Props 1 and 37, could create new down payment tools for California buyers. If they pass, the details will take time to roll out, and the program rules will matter. It is also a good reminder that down payment help exists in many states, and the team can walk buyers through what is available where they live.

Two price reports landed Monday, and both point the same way: home price growth has nearly stalled.

AEI Housing Center. AEI's Ed Pinto shared September data showing national home prices up 0.8% from a year earlier. That is down from 1.4% in August. Adjusted for inflation, prices fell 2.8%. For context, the year-over-year gain was 1.8% in September 2025 and 4.9% in September 2019, before the pandemic. AEI splits homes into price tiers. The high-priced tier rose 3.6%. Freddie Mac's index. Calculated Risk's Bill McBride reported the Freddie Mac House Price Index rose 0.15% in September from August, after seasonal adjustment. 7) New appraisal format UAD 3.6 is live, and appraisers are pushing back on a rule that lets lenders share report data and photos

Wed show

So-what: Appraisals look different starting this week, and some appraisers are leaving GSE work. That can mean slower turn times.

1-minute read

The biggest change to appraisals in years started Monday.

As of Nov. 2, every new appraisal sent to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac must use UAD 3.6, the redesigned appraisal data format. The value approach does not change. How the report is ordered, filled out, delivered, and revised does. Many fields are now dropdowns, which makes reports easier for computers to read.

There is a catch. Fannie and Freddie gave a one-time exception to lenders that are not ready. Those lenders can keep sending old-format UAD 2.6 reports through May 19, 2027. So your wholesale lender's switch date may not be Nov. 2.

HousingWire reports pushback over "Certification 25." It lets lenders, the GSEs, investors, and data firms store, copy, analyze, and share appraisal data, including interior photos, without the appraiser's consent. One Virginia appraiser has stopped GSE work over it. Others raise MLS photo copyright concerns.

So what: check each lender's UAD 3.6 date, expect some appraisal bumps, and tell sellers their interior photos may live on in data systems.

Expand 3-minute read

Appraisals changed this week, and it matters for every loan going to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac.

What changed. Starting Monday, Nov. 2, every new appraisal report submitted to the Uniform Collateral Data Portal for a GSE loan must use UAD 3.6. That is the redesigned Uniform Appraisal Dataset. The old forms, like the familiar 1004, are replaced by one dynamic report. Many fields are now structured, with dropdown choices instead of free text. The goal is cleaner data that computers can read and compare.

What did not change. As a National Association of Mortgage Brokers piece in MBA NewsLink put it, how a home is valued does not change. How the appraisal is ordered, delivered, and revised does.

The exception. On Sept. 30, Fannie and Freddie announced a one-time exception for lenders that could not finish their UAD 3.6 work in time. Lenders that get it can keep submitting new UAD 2.6 reports through May 19, 2027. They can resubmit earlier UAD 2.6 reports through late June 2027. The GSEs say the exception will not be extended. For brokers, that means each wholesale lender may switch on a different date.

The appraiser fight. HousingWire reports that "Certification 25" in the new report is causing pushback. It says lenders, the GSEs, secondary market investors, and data services can store, copy, analyze, and distribute report data, including interior photos, without the appraiser's consent. One Virginia appraiser, Mehbod, told HousingWire he has stopped doing GSE work over it. Florida appraiser Frank Gregoire said it is similar to the old Certification 21, but raised concerns about MLS photo copyright. Sacramento appraiser Ryan Lundquist also weighed in on how appraisers are responding.

Why it matters for originators. If some appraisers step back from GSE work, turn times and fees could rise in some markets. New formats also mean new kinds of revision requests while everyone learns the system. Lenders using UAD 3.6 may see different underwriting conditions tied to the structured fields.

Why it matters for consumers. Interior photos of a borrower's or seller's home become part of a data set that can be shared more widely. Most people never think about where appraisal photos go. Sellers who care about privacy may ask questions, and agents should be ready.

What brokers should do now. Confirm each lender's UAD 3.6 start date. Ask whether they took the exception. Check how they handle appraisal revisions and whether their appraisal management company panels are ready. Build in a little extra time on appraisals over the next few weeks.

So what for the show. This is a behind-the-scenes change that buyers will feel only if something slows down. It is a good chance to explain what an appraisal is, why the lender orders it, and why a short delay is not a red flag.

eLEND cited FICO data showing the average score slipped to 714, and NAR data showing first-time buyers were just 21% of buyers.

Many lenders add their own "overlays," which are rules stricter than what FHA, Fannie Mae, or Freddie Mac require. A common one is a minimum credit score. eLEND is loosening that overlay for the rest of 2026.

The program. eLEND, also known as American Financial Resources, announced CreditEdge on Nov. 2. It is a limited-time incentive. Under it, FHA cash-out refinances can go as low as a 500 credit score. Fannie and Freddie conventional loans can go below 620. When private mortgage insurance is required on a conventional loan, the minimum is 600.

More people are trying to earn on both sides of a home purchase, as agent and as loan officer. A new analysis gives a rough count.

The numbers. Real estate data firm RETR matched names in real estate agent license records with loan officer records in the NMLS system. HousingWire reported it found 3,729 matches nationwide. California had 1,494, or 40% of the total. Florida had 503, Texas 349, and Arizona 298. Together, those four states account for about 71% of matches.

The big caveat. These are name matches. RETR did not verify that every match is the same person. Common names can create false matches, and some people use different name forms in different systems. So treat the count as an estimate, not an exact figure.

Why California stands out. California's 1,494 matches are almost three times Florida's 503. That fits what loan officers see on the ground: in many California markets, it is common to meet agents who also originate loans, or loan officers who also hold a real estate license. The analysis does not say how many of these people are actively doing both jobs today.

How one practitioner does it. HousingWire profiled Shawn McNeil of HomeFlo and Mpire. He said he splits his time about 50/50 between real estate and lending. He said he discloses his dual role 100% of the time, so clients know he is acting in both capacities.

The compliance side. Dual roles bring real legal risk if done wrong. RESPA, the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act, bans kickbacks and unearned referral fees for settlement services such as mortgages. Affiliated business arrangements need specific disclosures. Federal loan officer compensation rules limit how loan officers can be paid and prevent pay based on loan terms. Some lenders and brokerages do not allow their people to hold both roles at all. State rules on disclosure vary too.

Questions to ask a dual-licensed partner. Who is the lender of record on the loan? How is the person paid on the loan side, and is that pay set by a standard comp plan? Is the dual role disclosed in writing at the start, before the buyer chooses a lender? Is the buyer told they can shop other lenders? Clean answers protect the client and the business.

What it means for consumers. Some buyers like one point of contact. Others worry about conflicts of interest, since the same person benefits from the deal closing on both the home and the loan. Clear disclosure and the buyer's right to shop for a lender are key protections.
