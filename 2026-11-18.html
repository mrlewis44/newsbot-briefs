2) Fed's Waller says the Fed may need to "shock people" with a half-point hike in December; markets price a quarter point plus more in January

Wed show

So-what: The debate is no longer whether the Fed hikes, but how fast. Faster hikes are bad for the short end and keep mortgage rates near 7.5% for now.

1-minute read

Fed Governor Christopher Waller told CNBC this morning that a half-point hike at the Dec. 8-9 meeting is probably necessary. "You might need to, like, shock people," he said. "I'm very, very concerned about inflation. It's kind of headed in the wrong direction, and it's spreading out."

Waller said the Fed should first take back last year's three quarter-point cuts, which would put the target range at 4% to 4.25%, and may need to go further. He would accept a quarter point in December only if the Fed clearly signals another hike in January, per MarketWatch. Chairman Kevin Warsh has so far resisted that kind of forward guidance.

Other officials disagree on pace. St. Louis Fed's Alberto Musalem floated up to four hikes by spring on Tuesday, while New York Fed's John Williams said "I don't think the situation calls for dramatic action." Futures fully price a quarter-point hike in December and another move in January, per CNBC. The October minutes come out at 11:00 AM PT.

Plain terms: hike talk lifts short-term rates first. Mortgage rates follow the 10-year, which has been calmer.

Expand 3-minute read

Waller's interview matters because he has been a reliable signal of where the Fed's center is heading. Last week he said a half-point hike would be on the table if core inflation hit 0.3% for the month, and it did. Today he went further.

His main concern is psychology. "People are starting to think 3% may be the norm," Waller said, per MarketWatch. "Businesses are starting to pass along these price increases to customers. That's the kind of psychology I think we need to start breaking." Reuters reported he noted that much of the faster inflation is now in services, which are "largely beyond the influence of high oil costs and other temporary factors." That is a hawkish point: it means the Fed cannot wait for oil to come down and expect inflation to follow.

On the path, Waller said the Fed needs to reverse the three 2025 cuts. The Fed raised rates once in September, its first hike in more than three years, then held in October at 3.75% to 4%. Three more quarter-point moves would mean 4.5% to 4.75%. MarketWatch quoted him saying "we may have to go above three... plus maybe some more" to get inflation coming down. He offered a compromise: quarter-point moves in December and January would be OK if the Fed clearly says January is coming. If not, "you have to go 50 in December."

There is a real split. Musalem's Tuesday speech pointed to as many as four hikes before spring 2027. Cleveland's Beth Hammack and Dallas's Lorie Logan have backed gradual tightening. Williams said the current approach of collecting data and debating each meeting "is working well." Separately, WSJ reports Governor Lisa Cook has refused to take part in a White House hearing, citing a biased panel, which keeps a Fed independence story in the background.

Market pricing: CNBC reports futures fully price a quarter-point December hike, another move in January and at least one more before the end of 2027. HousingWire cited CME's FedWatch at more than 93% for December, up from 76% a month ago. The 2-year rose more than 4 basis points to about 4.83% right after Waller spoke.

Peter Boockvar pushed back hard. He called a 50-basis-point "shock" hike "a tone deaf response" to what is happening in credit markets and with AI and data center stocks, which have fallen sharply this month. He said he could understand another 25 but not 50, given "a changed economic and market environment over the past month."

Why it matters for mortgages: the Fed sets short-term rates, not the 30-year. But a faster hiking path keeps inflation fears and bond selling alive, and it keeps mortgage spreads from tightening. For clients, the honest message is that the Fed is still leaning tighter, so near-term relief has to come from weaker data or a market scare, not the Fed.

3) Pending home sales fall 4.7% to a record low, down 16.7% from a year ago; NAR's Yun says 2026 sales could be the lowest since the mid-1990s

Wed show

So-what: Fewer signed contracts in October means fewer closings in November and December. Expect a slow year-end and more motivated sellers.

1-minute read

NAR's Pending Home Sales Index fell 4.7% in October, more than double the 2% decline forecast, to the lowest level in the index's 25-year history. It was down 16.7% from a year ago, and all four regions fell, with the sharpest drops in the South and West, per CNBC.

"Two consecutive months of record-low pending contracts point to very sluggish fourth-quarter home sales closings, and annual existing-home sales could be the lowest since the mid-1990s," said NAR chief economist Lawrence Yun.

Pending sales count signed contracts, so they lead closed sales by a month or two. CNBC noted the drop came even though rates eased in October: Mortgage News Daily's 30-year average started the month at 7.54% and ended at 7.27%, before jumping again last week.

Realtor.com's October report backs this up: contract signings down 17.0% year over year, the lowest October on record. Cancellations were 7.8%, about normal. In plain terms, buyers are not backing out of deals. They are just not starting them.

Expand 3-minute read

This is the clearest sign yet of how hard higher rates have hit buyer demand this fall.

The September index was already a record low: it fell 7.3% to 66.0, per HousingWire's report at the time. October fell another 4.7%. That is two record lows in a row. Forecasters expected only a 2% drop, likely because rates eased during October. CNBC's Diana Olick pointed out that Mortgage News Daily's 30-year average started October at 7.54% and ended at 7.27%. That should have helped, but it did not, at least not enough.

Yun's statement is the headline quote for the show: annual existing-home sales could be the lowest since the mid-1990s. For context, NAR reported last Thursday that October existing-home sales fell to 3.75 million at an annual rate, the lowest since August 2010, with a median price of $416,700, up 0.4%, and 5.1 months of supply. Pending sales feed future closings, so the November and December existing-home sales reports should be weak too.

Realtor.com's October data gives more detail. Contract signings were down 17.0% year over year and 8.1% from September, the steepest annual drop since March 2023 and the lowest October on record. The stock of homes in pending status was down 10.5% year over year. Realtor.com estimated that the change in average mortgage rates alone has cut buyer purchasing power by 6.5% since August and more than 12% since February. The cancellation rate was 7.8%, up only slightly from 7.5%. Their conclusion: the problem is homes failing to go under contract, not deals falling apart.

Redfin's earlier October report showed the same pattern, with pending sales down 3.6% month over month to the lowest since 2013 outside of April 2020, and the biggest year-over-year drops in Seattle and San Diego, both over 20%. Redfin also reported its national median sale price fell 0.4% year over year to $388,634, the first decline since May 2023.

What it means for BuyWise clients: buyers who can qualify today face less competition than at any time in years. Redfin's Chen Zhao put it simply: "It's a buyer's market, but not many people can afford to buy." Her advice to buyers was to treat list price as a starting point and negotiate for closing costs, repairs and rate buydowns. That is a good show segment: the rate is high, but the deal terms are the best in years.

A bit more context on the October drop. CNBC said regional declines were sharpest in the South, usually the busiest region for sales, and in the West, the most expensive. Redfin's October report said closed sales fell 2.1% to the lowest since December 2023, and that total homes for sale rose to the highest since early 2020. New listings, though, fell 1% to the lowest in more than two years, so would-be sellers are pulling back as well. That mix, with fewer buyers, fewer new sellers and more stale listings, is the classic setup for price cuts on homes that have been sitting.

For the business: purchase pipelines into year-end will be thin. Expect more seller concessions and more requests for temporary buydowns funded by sellers.

4) October housing starts jump 9.9% to 1.309 million on apartments, but single-family permits drop 8.7% and total permits hit the lowest since May 2020

Wed show

So-what: The headline beat is mostly apartments. Falling permits mean builders are pulling back, which points to less new supply later.

1-minute read

Census reported October housing starts at a 1.309 million annual rate, up 9.9% from September's revised 1.191 million and well above the 1.245 million forecast. Starts were up 2.8% from a year ago.

Most of the jump was apartments. Single-family starts rose 4.0% to 864,000 but were down 3.2% from a year ago. Buildings with five or more units hit 433,000, and CalculatedRisk said multi-family starts were up 17.1% year over year.

Permits are the worry. Total permits fell 7.4% to 1.295 million, below the 1.377 million forecast and 8.7% below a year ago. Single-family permits fell 8.7% to 772,000 and were down 12.9% from October 2025, the lowest since May 2020, per CNBC.

investingLive called the starts number "a rare win in US housing data" but said to treat it as a one-off given falling permits. Realtor.com's Joel Berner said builders are "slowing down activity at the top of their funnel." Plain terms: fewer new homes are coming, but builders still need to sell what they have, so incentives stay big.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is how to read the construction numbers.

Starts measure homes where construction began. Permits come first and are a better guide to future activity. October's starts beat, but CalculatedRisk noted starts for the prior two months were revised down, and year-to-date total starts are down 1.1% from 2025.

Single-family is the part that matters most for buyers. Starts rose to 864,000 from 831,000 in September. Peter Boockvar put that in context: 864,000 compares with 875,000 in July and 902,000 in June. Multi-family, which swings a lot month to month, was 445,000 by his count versus 360,000 in September and a peak of 643,000 in November 2022.

Permits fell sharply. Total permits were 1.295 million, down 7.4% from September's 1.398 million. Single-family permits fell to 772,000 from 846,000. CNBC said single-family permits were down 12.9% from a year ago and at the lowest level since May 2020, when the pandemic shut things down. Five-plus unit permits were 475,000.

Why are builders pulling back? Tuesday's NAHB index explains it. Builder confidence fell to 25, the lowest since April 2012. 46% of builders cut prices in November and 68% used incentives, both post-Covid highs, and buyer traffic fell to 16. Builders cited higher rates, tariffs on materials, labor shortages tied to immigration enforcement and higher fuel costs. HousingWire also reported LGI Homes, which sells mostly to entry-level buyers, had a 53.8% cancellation rate last quarter, versus 33.6% a year earlier.

The incentive angle is important for brokers. Realtor.com reported the average new-home buyer had a starting mortgage rate of 5.01% in the third quarter, versus 6.15% for existing-home buyers. On a $400,000 home with 10% down, that is about $258 a month. Century Communities said incentives averaged about 13% of the sale price in Q3 and will rise another 200 to 250 basis points in Q4.

Some regional and builder detail rounds this out. HousingWire noted PulteGroup's traffic fell 14% year over year in the third quarter, with first-time buyers making up most of the missing traffic, and CEO Ryan Marshall said move-up buyers "need to sell their existing home, and that is tough." The median new-home price in September was about $400,000, down nearly 4% from a year ago, and Meritage's average closing price fell 4% to $365,000. Meritage CFO Hilla Sferruzza said incentives will stay elevated "absent a sustained market-driven catalyst that provides meaningful mortgage rate relief." Several builders, including D.R. Horton, gave wider-than-usual 2027 guidance ranges because visibility is poor.

For the show: new construction is where the deals are right now, because builders are paying to buy down rates. For BuyWise, that means comparing a builder's in-house rate offer against what we can do with the builder's incentive applied to our loan. The longer-term story is less hopeful. Fewer permits now means less new supply in 2027 and 2028, which can keep prices firm once rates come down.

5) MBA: applications fall 2.3% as the 30-year contract rate jumps to 7.35%; purchase apps down 18% from a year ago, but FHA refis rise 7%

Wed show

So-what: The October rate dip had been pulling buyers back. Last week's jump stopped that. FHA pricing is the one bright spot for refis.

1-minute read

MBA's weekly survey for the week ending Nov. 13 showed total applications down 2.3%. The average 30-year conforming contract rate rose to 7.35% from 7.21%, with points up to 0.79 from 0.75, the highest in three weeks.

Purchase applications fell 3% for the week and were 18% below a year ago. Refinance applications fell 2% and were 54% lower than a year ago. MBA's Joel Kan said "renewed affordability challenges from elevated mortgage rates have pushed prospective homebuyers to the sidelines."

One exception: FHA refinances rose almost 7%. Kan said the FHA rate was roughly 30 basis points below the conforming rate, which "supported refinance opportunities for some homeowners."

Other rate gauges agree. HousingWire's Mortgage Rates Center had the 30-year conforming average at 7.58% Tuesday, up 23 basis points in a week, with jumbo at 7.87% and FHA at 7.37%. Mortgage News Daily was about 7.50% to start the week. Plain terms: buyers are back to about 7.5% after briefly seeing low 7s.

Expand 3-minute read

This is the first weekly demand reading after last week's rate jump, and it shows the hit.

CNBC's Diana Olick noted that mortgage demand had been improving for about a month as rates fell, and that streak "came to an abrupt end last week." MBA's survey showed the composite index down 2.3% seasonally adjusted and 5% unadjusted. The 30-year conforming rate rose 14 basis points to 7.35% for loans of $832,750 or less with 20% down.

Kan's explanation: "Treasury yields rose last week amid data showing inflation accelerated in October and Fed officials acknowledging that additional rate hikes may be needed in the coming months." Purchase applications were down 18% from a year ago. The refinance index was 54% below a year ago, and CalculatedRisk noted the refi index is historically very low.

The FHA detail is useful. FHA refis rose almost 7% while overall refis fell. With FHA running about 30 basis points below conforming, some FHA borrowers with recent higher-rate loans have a small window. This lines up with the strong FHA fund report and talk of a premium cut that we covered Tuesday.

HousingWire's rate tracker is higher than MBA's because it measures a different set of loans. On Tuesday it showed 30-year conforming at 7.58%, jumbo at 7.87% and FHA at 7.37%. HousingWire also quoted Kyle Bass of Refi.com on preparation: "A shadow application, with income documents, statements and credit in order, lets a homeowner ask what their current mortgage could do for them the day rates cooperate. Waiting for a better number only works if you are ready when it arrives."

Logan Mohtashami's weekly tracker gives another view. Inventory dropped to 895,988 as seasonal declines set in, while new listings were 53,162 versus 56,569 a year ago. He said weekly pending sales had stabilized for four weeks, and that price cuts rose about 1 point year over year once rates moved above 7%. He also noted his -0.62% national price forecast for 2026 may be hard to hit since most indexes show 1% to 2% growth.

For the show, a HousingWire column made a simple point worth borrowing: on a $300,000, 30-year loan, going from 7% to 7.5% adds about $102 a month. That is real money, but much smaller than the $731 jump from 3% to 7%. The Fed does not set the 30-year rate, and a later Fed hike does not change a fixed loan once it closes. For prepared buyers, price, repairs and concessions can matter as much as a half point.

Lock behavior is shifting too. When rates whipsaw like this, more borrowers ask about floating, and more ask about ARMs. Optimal Blue showed ARMs at 17% of October locks, and MBA's ARM share was close to 10% back in September when the 30-year first crossed 7%. For borrowers who expect to move or refinance within five to seven years, a 5/1 or 7/1 ARM can be worth pricing side by side with the 30-year fixed, with a clear explanation of how the caps work.

Also new: the HousingWire column on rates noted Freddie Mac's series hit a record low of 2.65% in early January 2021 and a high of 18.63% in October 1981. Those extremes are useful perspective, but they do not make today's housing cheap. Home prices, incomes, insurance and taxes all matter. The column's bottom line was to judge the whole transaction, including price and terms, not just the rate.

Practical step: build a ready-to-go refi list now, especially FHA borrowers from 2025 and 2026 at 7.5% or higher.

6) ResiClub: 76 of the 300 largest metros now have falling home prices year over year, up from 54 in August; national prices up just 0.7%

Wed show

So-what: Softening is picking up again with rates above 7%. The national number hides big local splits, so local inventory is what to watch.

1-minute read

Lance Lambert's ResiClub analysis of the Zillow Home Value Index shows national home prices up just 0.7% from October 2025 to October 2026. That is down from 1.0% in September and 1.3% in August.

The count of falling markets is rising again. 76 of the 300 largest metros (25%) had year-over-year declines in October, up from 66 in September and 54 in August. Among the 50 largest metros, 21 (42%) are negative, so declines lean toward bigger markets.

The split is wide. Hartford, CT prices are 28.0% above their 2022 peak, while Austin, TX is 28.8% below. Weak spots are mostly in the Sun Belt and Mountain West, including parts of Texas, Florida, Washington and Colorado, where inventory is well above 2019. Gains remain in parts of the Northeast and Midwest, where inventory is still tight.

Lambert says the big question is how much more softening comes now that the 30-year rate is back above 7%, and he recommends watching local active inventory and months of supply. Plain terms: most metros are still up a little, but after inflation, prices are falling in real terms almost everywhere.

Expand 3-minute read

This is the latest in Lambert's monthly tracker and the trend has turned the wrong way for sellers.

After a period when the number of falling markets shrank, it is growing again. The count of the 300 largest metros with year-over-year price declines went from 54 (18%) in August to 66 (22%) in September to 76 (25%) in October. Lambert says the national market is softer than these counts suggest because declines skew toward larger metros. Among the 50 largest, 21 are negative.

Where prices are falling: many of the soft markets are places that ran up most during the pandemic boom, such as Tampa and Austin, where prices outran local incomes. Once pandemic migration slowed and rates rose in 2022, those markets had to rely on local incomes. The Sun Belt also had a big wave of new construction, and builders cutting prices or offering incentives pulled buyers away from resale homes, which pushed resale inventory higher.

Where prices are rising: pockets of the Northeast and Midwest where active inventory is still well below 2019. Lambert stresses that 224 of the 300 metros still show nominal gains, but most are below income and consumer price growth, which economists call real price declines.

The gap between strong and weak markets is unusually wide in direction, he says, even if the range is historically normal. The longer some markets keep rising while others keep falling, the wider that gap grows. Hartford at +28.0% versus its 2022 peak and Austin at -28.8% is the cleanest example.

Other inventory data supports the story. CalculatedRisk cited Realtor.com's October report: active listings of 1,183,500, up 7.4% year over year, the fourth straight month of faster growth. Inventory is now just 6.6% below typical pre-pandemic levels, the narrowest gap since the pandemic. Mohtashami's weekly data had inventory up 7.11% year over year. Bill McBride noted NAR months of supply is the highest in more than a decade.

For California and the other states BuyWise serves, the message is local. Ask clients about inventory and months of supply in their specific market, not national headlines. Redfin's October report showed San Francisco prices up 16.8%, fueled by AI wealth, while Seattle was down 5.1%. In softer markets, buyers have room to negotiate. In tight markets, waiting for a price drop may not pay off.

Lambert's historical chart covers the 50 largest metros back to 2000, with the national figure as a yellow line. He notes that when you weight by population, the market looks a bit weaker than the metro counts suggest. His advice is to watch local active inventory and months of supply over the next several months, because markets where inventory is well above 2019 levels are the ones most likely to see continued price pressure.

Show angle: "Is your market rising or falling?" is a strong segment, because the answer really depends on where you live.

7) MBA: mortgage lenders lost $30 per loan in Q3, the first production loss since late 2023, after earning $973 per loan in Q2

So-what: Lender margins are gone in the strongest season of the year. Expect cuts, tighter pricing and more consolidation into year-end.

1-minute read

MBA's Quarterly Mortgage Bankers Performance Report showed independent mortgage banks and bank mortgage subsidiaries lost an average of $30 per loan before taxes in the third quarter of 2026. That compares with a $973 profit in Q2, and it is the first production loss since Q4 2023.

"The results are particularly disappointing given that the third quarter has historically been the strongest quarter of the year," said MBA's Marina Walsh.

Production revenue fell to $11,585 per loan, while costs rose to $11,615. In basis points, lenders lost 4 bps versus a long-run average profit of 38 bps since 2008. Average volume fell to $632 million per company and 1,848 loans.

Servicing helped. Net servicing income more than doubled to $195 per loan serviced. Including servicing, 59% of companies were profitable, down from 85% in Q2. Without servicing, only 44% would have been. Walsh said staff cuts "may accelerate the rest of this year and in the first quarter of 2027." Plain terms: the retail lender side is under pressure, which can help brokers win talent and pricing comparisons.

Expand 3-minute read

These results show how fast the summer rate rise hit lender economics.

Walsh said rising rates over the summer lowered volume and raised costs. Average loan balances also fell for a second straight quarter, which knocked about $325 off revenue per loan. Origination fees are partly tied to loan size, so smaller loans hurt.

Staffing did not adjust. Walsh noted production employees per firm were flat from Q2 and higher than a year earlier, even as volume dropped. That is why she expects staff cuts and other cost moves to speed up into Q1 2027.

Purchase loans were 83% of first-mortgage dollar volume among the firms surveyed, versus an estimated 73% for the whole industry. That means the IMBs in the survey are already purchase-heavy, so there was little refi business to fall back on.

Servicing was the offset. Net servicing financial income rose to $195 per loan from $80, helped by slower prepayments and higher MSR values. When rates rise, people refinance less, so servicing rights are worth more. That is good for lenders that keep servicing, and it is why companies with big servicing books are holding up better. It also ties to Fitch's warnings this week about MSR-heavy balance sheets, covered next.

Compare to history: the long-run average production profit since Q3 2008 is 38 basis points. Q2 was 25 bps. Q3 was a 4 bp loss. For context, Q4 is usually weaker than Q3 seasonally, so Q4 2026 could be worse unless rates fall.

One more detail: production revenue per loan fell to $11,585 from $11,909, while expenses rose to $11,615 from $10,936. That swing in both directions is what turned a $973 profit into a loss in one quarter. Average production volume was $632 million per company, down from $689 million in Q2 and roughly flat with $634 million a year earlier. Loans per company fell to 1,848 from 1,958. The flat year-over-year volume with much higher costs is the real warning sign: lenders staffed for a recovery that the summer rate jump took away. If rates stay above 7% into the spring, more consolidation among IMBs is likely.

Walsh's comments also point to a timing problem. Q3 has historically been the strongest quarter, so a loss there leaves little cushion going into the seasonally slow winter. Firms that relied on servicing to stay in the black now depend on rates staying high to keep MSR values up, which is the opposite of what their origination teams need.

What it means for BuyWise: retail lenders under pressure tend to cut costs and sometimes sharpen pricing to keep volume, and they also lay off or lose loan officers. That can be a recruiting opportunity. On the wholesale side, lenders losing money may tighten margins in other ways or push volume incentives, so watch rate sheets and lender fees closely. Clients also benefit from shopping, since lenders that need volume may compete harder on price.

8) Fitch turns negative on Pennymac, Planet and Freedom outlooks; flags UWM leverage at 5.3x and MSR risk across nonbanks

Why it matters: in a higher-for-longer rate world, MSRs are worth more on paper, but they can lose value fast if rates drop and borrowers refinance. Companies with heavy MSR exposure and high debt are exposed both ways. For brokers, this is mostly background. It becomes relevant if a lender changes pricing, tightens overlays or exits a product to protect its balance sheet. Spreading volume across more than one strong wholesale partner is a sensible hedge.

A practical note for borrowers: a lender's credit rating does not affect a closed loan. Loans are sold or securitized, and servicing can transfer, but the note terms do not change. The risk shows up in the pipeline: product changes, slower turn times or sudden pricing shifts when a lender needs to protect liquidity. For a broker, keeping two or three strong wholesale partners approved and active is the simplest protection. It also helps to note how each lender's servicing retention and recapture programs work, since MSR-heavy lenders will push hard to keep and refinance your clients later.

9) CFPB sends a revised mortgage servicing rule to the White House; industry attorneys expect a much narrower loss-mitigation rule

So-what: Servicers would likely get fewer required steps when borrowers fall behind. Clients who struggle later may have fewer guaranteed protections.

1-minute read

HousingWire's LendingLife reported that the CFPB has sent a revised version of its 2024 rule, "Streamlining Mortgage Servicing for Borrowers Experiencing Payment Difficulties," to the Office of Management and Budget for review.

The rule would update loss-mitigation standards under Regulation X, the RESPA rules that cover how servicers handle borrowers who miss payments, including foreclosure timing and options like forbearance and loan modifications.

Industry attorneys told HousingWire they expect a "significantly narrower" rule than the original proposal. They cite President Trump's directive to modernize Dodd-Frank-era rules and broad pushback from servicers against open-ended requirements in the 2024 version.

OMB review is one of the last steps before a final rule is published, though no date has been set. Plain terms: this is about servicing, not origination, but it shapes what happens to your clients if they hit hard times after closing. It also lands as delinquency risk rises with higher rates and a softer job market.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is the background on why this rule matters and what might change.

The original 2024 proposal from the CFPB under prior leadership aimed to simplify the loss-mitigation process. The idea was to give servicers more flexibility to offer help faster, but it also added new borrower protections, including limits on foreclosure steps while a borrower is being reviewed for help and requirements for notices in more languages. Servicers objected to the cost and to requirements they saw as open-ended.

The revised version is now at OMB, the White House office that reviews major rules. HousingWire says industry attorneys expect the final rule to be narrower, with fewer mandatory steps and more servicer discretion. That fits the current CFPB's direction of rolling back or trimming Dodd-Frank-era requirements.

This also connects to items we covered recently. FHA's fund report noted HUD plans a Reinstatement Advance Payment change for fiscal 2027 that affects how FHA handles borrowers who fall behind. And MBA's Q3 report shows servicing income is now holding up lender profits. Servicers will want the lowest-cost loss-mitigation rules possible while delinquencies are still manageable.

What it means for brokers: very little changes at origination. But brokers often get the call when a past client falls behind. Knowing that federal servicing rules may become looser helps set expectations. Investor rules from Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, FHA and VA still set many of the actual options, so borrowers with agency loans will still have defined workout paths.

Watch for the final text. Key items include whether dual-tracking limits survive, how notice requirements change, and whether servicers get more room to offer streamlined modifications without a full application. The timing is uncertain, and a narrower rule could still face challenges from states or consumer groups, especially with Democrats taking both chambers of Congress after the midterms.

It is also worth watching how the new Congress responds. Democrats won both chambers in the midterms, according to HousingWire's reporting on GSE reform, which could mean more oversight hearings on consumer protection rollbacks, even if they cannot easily block agency rules. State regulators, including California's DFPI, have also stepped up their own servicing rules in past rollback periods. For a multi-state broker, that means servicing standards could look different depending on where the client lives.

One more connection: the House-passed veterans package would raise VA refinance and assumption costs, and NAMB has been lobbying on S.4744. Taken together with the servicing rule and the FHA premium debate, the regulatory picture for government lending is moving on several fronts at once. These are worth a short monthly check-in rather than daily attention, unless a final rule or vote lands.

For the show, this is probably too inside-baseball. It is more useful as a talking point with referral partners and as background if a client asks what happens if they lose income after buying.
