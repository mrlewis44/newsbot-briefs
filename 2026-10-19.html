Expand 3-minute read

Peter Boockvar's Monday Boock Report, titled "Why this really matters, I think," covers a lot of ground. The part with the most direct link to mortgage rates is who is buying U.S. government debt.

Start with the August Treasury International Capital report, known as TIC data. It tracks foreign holdings of U.S. Treasuries. Japan cut its holdings by $35.6 billion to $1.068 trillion. That is the fourth straight monthly decline, and the total is just off the lowest level since 2019. China cut its holdings by $27.3 billion to $591 billion. That is China's smallest position since August 2008, and Boockvar notes much of that money has been going into gold instead.

So who bought? Private foreign investors were the biggest foreign buyer in August. Boockvar's takeaway is that Asia's trade surpluses with the U.S. are "no longer being recycled back into US Treasuries." For years, countries that sold more to the U.S. than they bought would park those dollars in Treasuries. That steady, price-insensitive buying helped hold down long-term rates. It is fading.

Why does that matter for a mortgage? The 30-year mortgage rate tracks the 10-year Treasury yield, plus a spread. Central banks and governments tend to buy no matter the price. Private investors care about price, so they ask for a higher yield to take on more long-term debt. Less official demand means the long end has to work harder to find buyers. That puts a floor under the 10-year, which was already near 5.1% on Friday.

Europe added to the pressure Monday. France's 10-year government bond yield, the OAT, jumped 9 basis points to 4.90%. The French stock index fell 1.4%, and the euro weakened against the dollar. Boockvar ties the move to rioting and political turmoil. Global bond yields tend to move together, so a selloff in Europe can bleed into Treasuries.

Boockvar also returns to AI and credit, a topic Mauldin covered separately in today's brief. His new angle: cancelled IPOs at data-center firms Firmus and AirTrunk, and "now maybe Nscale," matter because data-center spending feeds a long supply chain, from chips and servers to steel, cement and power. He says "what moves stock prices is rate of change," so watch whether capex growth slows. His bottom line is that "growing financing costs and ever growing need for capital" put the speed of the AI buildout in question.

On inflation, he quotes M&T Bank: "we do not see evidence of energy shocks seeping into core inflation, we expect overall inflation to decelerate." His reply: "let's hope they are right." M&T described the job market as "low-hire, low-fire." It also said consumer loan growth came from recreational finance and HELOCs, and Truist said its loan growth came from consumer and residential mortgage lending.

What it means for you and your borrowers: there is no single buyer coming to rescue the bond market. Foreign central banks are stepping back, European yields are rising, and the AI financing story keeps adding risk. None of that guarantees higher rates. But it makes a quick drop back to the 6s less likely without a clear slowdown at home. For clients waiting on rates, a float plan needs a real reason, not hope.

This is the clearest picture yet of what 7% mortgage rates did to buyers in September, and it lands close to home.

Redfin's report, published Oct. 12 and featured in Monday's Mortgage Nuggets, says pending home sales fell 4% from August on a seasonally adjusted basis. That is the biggest monthly drop in more than three years. Contract signings are now at their lowest level since spring 2014, with the only exception being April 2020, when the pandemic froze the market. Redfin ties it directly to rates, which climbed steadily and passed 7% by the end of September. Its weekly average mortgage rate measure hit 7.28% at the end of September, the highest in three years. Mortgage Nuggets adds that the 30-year went from 6.66% in late August to 7.4% by Oct. 8.

Every one of the 50 largest metros saw pending sales fall from a year earlier. That last happened in September 2023, the last time rates stayed above 7%. The West Coast took the biggest hit. Seattle was down 24.4%. Los Angeles was down 16%, San Diego 15.8% and Anaheim 15.5%. Riverside was down 13.5%. Redfin says pricey Southern California markets were hit hard because higher rates priced out buyers who were on the fence.

Closed sales also fell 1.6% from August to their lowest level since late 2023.

Prices are losing steam. The national median sale price was $393,895, up 1.3% from a year ago, the smallest gain since February. Median asking prices fell 0.5% for the month. About 60.5% of homes sold below their original asking price. And 19.8% of sellers with active listings cut their price, with the share rising in 43 of the 50 biggest metros.

Supply is building. Total homes for sale rose 1.2% for the month to the highest level since March 2020, because sellers keep listing while buyers pull back.

The standout number is cancellations. Nationally, 14.5% of deals that went under contract in September fell through, the highest share on record outside March and April 2020. Locally, the cancellation rate was 14.3% in Anaheim, 16.1% in Los Angeles, 15.3% in San Diego and 17.4% in Riverside. Redfin says buyers walk when an inspection goes badly or the monthly payment is not what they expected.

NAR's survey of agents points the same way. Only 8% of respondents expect buyer traffic to rise over the next three months, down from 13% the prior month and the weakest since fall 2023. Zonda's Ali Wolf put it simply: "Don't think of this as a recovery."

For Emily and Jake, Redfin's Chen Zhao offers the upside. With less competition, buyers have room to negotiate on price, closing costs and repairs, and sellers sometimes pay for a rate buydown. Slower sales may also mean prices flatten or fall. For Josh, a 14.5% fall-through rate is a reminder that a strong pre-approval and a clear payment picture up front help keep deals together.

ResiClub's monthly home price report, sent Sunday, uses the Zillow Home Value Index, which tracks the typical home in the middle of the market. It shows the recent rate spike starting to bite into prices.

The metro count helps show how wide the weakness is. In September 2026, 17 of the 50 largest metros, or 34%, had prices below a year earlier. For comparison, it was 24 metros, or 48%, in September 2025. It was 7 in 2024 and 19 in 2023. From 2020 through 2022, it was zero.

Where is it weak? ResiClub says markets in Texas, Florida and Colorado, where inventory has risen above 2019 levels, are seeing mild corrections. In its regional tables, sent Friday, the weakest large Western metro was Las Vegas at down 2.7%. In the Southwest, Austin was down 3.9%. In the Southeast, Asheville, North Carolina was down 3.5%.

For the Wednesday show, the message for Emily and Jake is balanced. Prices are not crashing nationally. But when rates jump, prices soften, and the share of markets with falling prices is meaningful. That means more negotiating room, especially on homes that have been sitting.

The housing market is splitting in two, and the split favors the top end.

A Wall Street Journal story Monday, using First American data, says luxury prices are rising faster or falling slower than starter-home prices in 33 of the top 50 metros. The top third of the market has pushed national prices up "more than 4% over the past two years."

The national price numbers hide a split market. A Wall Street Journal story by Nicholas G. Miller, published Monday, says the top of the market is doing the heavy lifting while the bottom struggles. The headline also says the luxury market "runs on cash." The story did not give a cash share we could confirm, so we are not quoting one.

The top third of the market has pushed national prices up "more than 4% over the past two years." In other words, the average looks fine because expensive homes are holding up.

Tampa shows the same pattern, but harsher. Homes priced below $305,000 fell 4.1% in August from a year earlier. Homes priced above $471,833 rose 0.4%.

Builders have noticed. Toll Brothers, which builds high-end homes, saw its average selling price rise to $996,000 from $974,000. Lennar, a big entry-level and first-time-buyer builder, saw its average fall to $372,000 from $383,000. The Journal says entry-level builders are leaning on rate buydowns to keep sales moving. That is builder money used to lower the buyer's rate, at least for a while.

Bill McBride at Calculated Risk adds some national context from his mid-October housing review. Realtor.com median list prices have been down from a year earlier for 11 straight months, and September's was the lowest for that month since 2021. The Case-Shiller National index was up 1.9% from a year earlier in July. After inflation, Case-Shiller prices are 3.8% below the 2022 peak, and he says recent inflation will push real prices down further. NAR's months of supply hit 4.8, the highest in over a decade, and he calls a small national price decline over the winter "possible."

So why does this matter for a broker who mostly serves first-time and move-up buyers?

First, first-time buyers are shopping in the softest part of the market. Starter homes are where prices are flat to down and where sellers and builders are under the most pressure. That gives Emily & Jake more room to negotiate on price, credits and closing costs.

Second, builder buydowns are the main lever at the entry level. Comparing a builder's buydown offer against a price cut, and showing the payment both ways, is real value a broker can add. A permanent buydown, a temporary one and a price cut all land differently over five or ten years.

Third, move-up buyers face a mixed deal. Their current starter home may be selling into a weaker tier, while the home they want sits in a tier that is holding value. That squeezes the equity they can carry forward. Run the sale and the purchase together, not separately.

For Wednesday's show, the simple message is: the market is not one market. Prices at the top are holding. Prices at the bottom are giving way. If you are a first-time buyer, the weak spot is yours to use.

Expand 3-minute read

Two pieces of news from Friday, both reported by HousingWire, point the same way. Big players are planning for mortgage rates to stay high for a while.

Start with Fannie Mae. Its Economic and Strategic Research group cut its 2027 origination forecast 18%, to $1.86 trillion from $2.27 trillion. It sees 2026 at about $2 trillion. We covered Fannie's 7.5% rate forecast on October 15. The new news here is how much volume that rate path removes.

The refi cut is the big one. Fannie now expects about $430 billion in 2027 refinances, down from $802 billion in its prior forecast. It sees 2026 refis around $695 billion. Purchase volume holds flat at $1.4 trillion in 2027. Fannie also expects the Fed to keep raising rates. The next Fed meeting ends October 28.

The Mortgage Bankers Association is in the same place. MBA sees rates above 7% through 2027. It expects 2027 originations to fall 5% to $1.99 trillion, from $2.1 trillion in 2026. It also expects more consolidation among independent mortgage banks. BSI Financial CEO Gagan Sharma summed up the mood: "Rates have gotten higher than what anybody expected."

Now the bank side. First Horizon is selling about $4.5 billion of residential mortgages at roughly a 10% discount. The loans carry a weighted average rate of 3.85%, so they are worth much less than face value with rates above 7%. Only about $200 million of deposits go with the sale.

The cost is real. First Horizon takes a $473 million hit in the third quarter and posts a net loss of about $79 million. In return, it expects about a 4% boost to 2027 earnings per share and about 1.5 points added to its return on tangible equity. Its loan-to-deposit ratio drops to about 88%. CEO Bryan Jordan explained the decision: it "became clear that we are in a cycle of rising rates."

Why does a bank sale matter to a mortgage broker? Because it shows how a large lender is betting. Banks do not take a nearly half-billion-dollar loss to sell old loans if they think rates will drop soon. They do it when they want to free up money to lend at today's higher rates.

Here is what it means for a broker/owner planning 2027.

Mortgage Nuggets reported Monday that Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac repurchased 1,401 loans in the second quarter of 2026. Those loans carried $465.2 million in unpaid principal. That is up 19.5% from the first quarter.

A repurchase, or buyback, happens when Fannie or Freddie finds a defect in a loan they bought and makes the lender take it back. The lender then owns the risk, and often a loss. That is why lenders watch these numbers closely.

Fannie was the more aggressive of the two. It accounted for 58.2% of total repurchase dollars. More telling is how each agency resolved its defect demands. The most useful number is the age of the loans. Loans originated in the third and fourth quarters of 2025 made up 46.5% of total repurchase activity. That means loans roughly 6 to 12 months old are driving most of the buyback volume. These are not old files. They are recent loans.

Why should a broker care? In wholesale, the repurchase demand lands on the lender first. But lenders protect themselves.
