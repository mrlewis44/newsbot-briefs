4) Summit takeaway: street fight market funnels growth to brokers and nondelegated lenders

So-what: A tiny share of lenders take most production growth as LOs leave banks and IMBs for leaner broker and non-del shops.

1-minute read

At HousingWire's Mortgage Banking Summit in Dallas, Princeton Mortgage CEO Rich Weidel called today's market a street fight. Using RETR data across 2,070 lenders, he said half of lenders saw net production shrinkage last year, while only 57 companies (about 2.8%) grew more than $400 million over the past 12 months and captured roughly two-thirds of all production growth.

NFM Lending's Rick Roque said from Jan 1 through Sep 9 this year, 17,438 loan officers changed employers and moved about $6.8 billion in production. IMBs net lost 97 LOs and $1.8 billion. Banks net lost 230 LOs and $5 billion. Brokers net gained 327 LOs and $6.8 billion. Much of that, Roque said, is the nondelegated correspondent model: lender-like control with underwriting, closing, and funding costs parked at a larger partner.

So-what for a California broker shop: growth and talent are concentrating in lean broker and non-del platforms, not in high-cost bank and traditional IMB structures.

Expand 3-minute read

HousingWire's Oct 2 summit write-up is core wholesale and broker strategy news. Weidel's street fight framing matches what BuyWise feels in daily lock desks: volume is flat-to-hard, rates are elevated, and market share is a knife fight. The RETR slice he cited is stark. Across 2,070 lenders, half shrank net production last year. Only 57 firms grew by more than $400 million over twelve months. That 2.8% cohort took about two-thirds of all production growth.

Weidel sorts winners into capital winners and cost-to-produce winners. Capital players use balance sheet to pay upfront signing bonuses in an acqui-hire model that only works if producers stay. Cost winners attack per-loan expense so they can pay originators more, price better for consumers, or both. He also sees originators moving into the nondelegated channel, describing brokers becoming non-dels and mortgage bankers getting lean as green shoots with an entirely new cost structure that is attracting production.

Roque's LO migration numbers put flesh on that. From Jan 1 to Sep 9, 17,438 loan officers changed shops, shifting roughly $6.8 billion of production. Independent mortgage bankers: net loss of 97 LOs and $1.8 billion. Banks: net loss of 230 LOs and $5 billion. Brokers: net gain of 327 LOs and $6.8 billion. Roque ties a large share of that to nondelegated correspondent, where teams keep lender-like ops while a partner carries dedicated UW, closing, and funding capacity. He cited large NFM teams with corporate UW and funding, and non-del shops large enough that UWM assigns dedicated underwriters, closers, and funders.

Teraverde's James Deitch described a cost hierarchy: banks typically highest cost, then IMBs, then brokers. Roque sees that hierarchy lining up with where LOs and volume are migrating. For BuyWise and Arbor-bound ops, the implication is not to chase every signing bonus. It is to keep cost-to-produce tight, protect broker economics, and decide deliberately whether non-del capacity is a growth path or a distraction from the retail broker model that already fits Josh's channel.

Wednesday show translation for Emily and Jake still works even if this item is more industry than consumer: your LO's channel matters. Broker and non-del shops that win on cost can often show more competitive pricing and faster condition clearing than bloated platforms. Ask who underwrites and funds the loan, how overlays differ from the guideline, and whether the team has dedicated support at the investor when a file gets sticky.

Compliance and capital note: non-del still needs licensing, warehouse discipline, post-close QC, and clear investor overlays. Summit praise is about cost structure, not a free pass on repurchase risk. If BuyWise evaluates non-del partners, compare turn times, exception desks, and rate-sheet competitiveness on the same 30-year conventional and non-QM scenarios you sell today.

9) California Prop 37: up to $25B CalHFA down-payment seconds for new homes

Wed show

So-what: A possible fixed-rate state second for middle-income new-construction buyers, not a cheaper first mortgage.

1-minute read

LA Times' Oct 1 voter guide explains California Proposition 37: a CalHFA-run middle-income down payment loan program of up to $25 billion for buyers of newly built or never-sold homes. Funding would come from revenue bonds repaid by program borrowers, not general taxpayer dollars. Buyers need income at or below 200% of local median (about $216,200 for a family of four in Los Angeles County) and typically homes priced around $1 million to $1.5 million by county.

Borrowers would put at least 3% down, with the state able to lend up to 17% of price as a fixed-rate second. Interest on that second would likely run higher than conventional first-mortgage rates near 7%, because revenue bonds demand a higher return. Supporters include CAR and carpenters; opponents say it subsidizes expensive new construction without lowering prices.

Show so-what: if it passes, it is a second-lien tool for new construction, not a magic 3% rate on the first mortgage.

Expand 3-minute read

For a Huntington Beach broker, Prop 37 is on-topic California housing policy ahead of the November ballot. The LA Times guide says the measure creates a down payment assistance program totaling up to $25 billion, administered by the California Housing Finance Agency, which already runs homeownership programs. The state would sell bonds and repay investors from interest and loan payments inside the program rather than from taxpayer general funds. CalHFA would also set the second-mortgage rates borrowers pay.

Eligibility is middle-income by local standards, not only first-time buyers. Income caps at 200% of local median. The Times cites about $216,200 for a family of four in Los Angeles County, about $166,800 in Madera County, and up to about $375,800 in Santa Clara County, per California Budget and Policy Center figures. Homes must be newly constructed or never sold before, including some commercial-to-condo conversions. County price caps generally land between $1 million and $1.5 million. Minimum borrower down payment is 3%, with state help up to 17% of sales price via a fixed-rate second mortgage.

The Budget and Policy Center caveat matters for client expectations: the second-mortgage rate would likely be higher than prevailing conventional 30-year rates, which have hovered near 7%, because revenue bonds are riskier for investors. That means Prop 37 is a leverage and cash-to-close tool, not a below-market first-lien subsidy that magically recreates 2021 payments on a coastal purchase.

Politics in brief without horse-race filler: carpenters' groups, the California Association of Realtors, and the California Democratic Party support it. Building a Better California, backed in part by Google co-founder Sergey Brin, is a major donor arguing it expands middle-class ownership. Reform California and Assemblyman Carl DeMaio's orbit oppose it as a subsidy for high-priced construction that fails to cut housing costs. Former Senate Majority Leader Bob Hertzberg spearheaded the initiative after gathering petition signatures.

Origination angle if voters approve: learn CalHFA second guidelines early, model combined first-plus-second payment and CLTV, and confirm new-construction builders who will accept the lien in their purchase contracts. Pair with conventional, FHA, or non-QM firsts carefully so reserve, disclosure, and subordinate-financing rules stay clean. Existing-home shoppers are largely outside this program by design, which matters in coastal resale-heavy markets like Orange County.
