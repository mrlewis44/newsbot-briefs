3) Fed minutes: most officials see one more hike by year-end, no rate cut in the 2027 median

So-what: A Fed still leaning toward hikes keeps a floor under the 10-year, and the minutes say mortgage borrowers are already facing tighter credit.

1-minute read

The Fed's September minutes, released Wednesday at 11:00 AM PT, confirmed what markets expected. Most officials think another rate hike "would likely be appropriate by year end." They did not pick October or December.

Quick recap: on September 16 the Fed voted 12-0 to raise its benchmark rate by a quarter point to 3.75% to 4.00%. The median forecast has one more hike this year, to 4.00% to 4.25%, and no cut at all in 2027.

The WSJ digest headline put it simply: further increase this year, but no urgency for an October hike. Before the minutes, CME FedWatch showed about a 20% chance of an October hike and better than 84% odds of a hike by December.

Why it matters for mortgages: officials said mortgage borrowers and small businesses faced somewhat tighter credit, and that mortgage rates had risen a bit more than the 10-year yield. Rates on 30-year loans follow the 10-year, not the Fed directly. But a Fed still leaning toward hikes keeps a floor under yields. The next Fed meeting is October 27 to 28. Thursday's Waller comments added to the hawkish tone.

Expand 3-minute read

The minutes cover the Fed's September 15 to 16 meeting and were released Wednesday at 11:00 AM PT. Here is what is in them, using the Fed's release plus a summary from ETF.net and the WSJ digest headline.

The decision itself. The Committee voted 12-0 to raise the federal funds target range by a quarter point to 3.75% to 4.00%, effective September 17. The interest paid on bank reserves went to 3.90%, and the discount rate went to 4.00%. The statement said the economy is "expanding at a solid pace," inflation "remains elevated," and the Committee "will deliver price stability."

The forward guidance. The key line: "most participants assessed that another increase in the target range for the federal funds rate would likely be appropriate by year end." Officials stressed they come into each meeting with an open mind. Only two meetings remain this year: October 27 to 28 and December 8 to 9.

The dot plot. Of 18 officials, 12 had the rate one quarter point higher by year-end, four had two more hikes, and two had no change. The median sits at 4.1% for the end of 2026 and 4.1% again for the end of 2027. In plain terms, the typical Fed official does not see a rate cut next year.

Why inflation worries them. Participants said they had not seen enough recent progress. Staff tied part of the August inflation estimate to energy. Officials pointed to geopolitical events lifting crude oil and refined fuel, and to surging investment in artificial intelligence. Most saw inflation risks tilted upward, and some said that tilt had grown. Longer-term inflation expectations still looked consistent with 2%, which is exactly the anchor Waller warned about losing on Thursday.

There was a small piece of good news after the meeting. August PCE inflation, released September 30, came in at 3.4% overall and 3.0% core. Fed staff had estimated 3.8% and 3.4%. Part of that gap was due to methodology changes, so it is not a clean win.

The mortgage-specific detail. The minutes note that 2-year to 10-year Treasury yields had already risen about 0.35 percentage point by the meeting, and mortgage rates rose a little more than the 10-year. Larger companies and state and local governments could still borrow fairly easily. Mortgage borrowers and small businesses faced somewhat tighter credit. That is the Fed acknowledging that housing is taking more of the hit.

The jobs picture. Officials called the labor market stable and close to maximum employment. Since then, September payrolls came in at just 29,000 with unemployment at 4.2%, and July and August were revised down a combined 60,000. Thursday's jobless claims at 197,000 show layoffs remain low.

How markets took it. The WSJ digest headline read "Fed Minutes Signal Further Rate Increase This Year, but No Urgency for October Hike." Before the minutes, CME FedWatch had about a 20% chance of an October hike, down from about 51% a week earlier, and about 84.5% for December. The bond market had largely priced this already, which is why Wednesday afternoon stayed calm.

What to tell clients. The Fed sets short-term rates. Mortgage rates move with the 10-year yield, which reacts to inflation, deficits, and Fed expectations. A Fed that is still hiking makes a quick drop in mortgage rates unlikely. The October 27 to 28 meeting comes before the next PCE report on October 29 and the October jobs report on November 6, so the Fed will be deciding with older data.

One soft spot: Manheim used car prices fell 1.1% in September and 0.6% from a year ago, which helps the goods side of CPI.

His Wednesday flash on the 10-year auction was upbeat: 5.30%, a 2.77 bid-to-cover that matches the best since late 2014. Buyers show up at these yields.

So-what for borrowers: the Fed is leaning toward more hikes, and expectations are moving the wrong way.

Expand 3-minute read

The Waller whiplash. Boockvar thinks Waller is worth watching because he is a Governor and a good read on where rates go next. The problem is how much he has moved. On September 3, Waller told Reuters he wanted to "paraphrase John Lennon" and give disinflation a chance. In a speech that same day he said he would be inclined to support holding the fed funds rate if the next two weeks of data kept showing disinflation. A few weeks later he backed a rate increase. Boockvar's joke: disinflation got its chance for about two weeks.

What Waller said Thursday. "If the economic data continue to come in as expected, I anticipate additional hikes to support a timelier return of inflation to our 2% goal." He added that the hikes "do not need to come at consecutive meetings, but they should be in place in an acceptable period of time." He also said he worries that inflation, which will soon have run above the Fed's target for five and a half years, will push consumers, investors, and businesses to raise their expectations for future inflation.

Why the market shrugged a little. The words "flexible" and "consecutive" let traders spread the hikes out. Boockvar puts the odds of a hike in three weeks at only 20%, but 86% by December. For rate sheets, that means the next Fed meeting is not the main risk. The steady drip of hawkish talk and long-end selling is.

Inflation expectations are moving the wrong way. The NY Fed's September Survey of Consumer Expectations showed one-year inflation expectations up 0.3 point to 3.9%, the highest since May 2023. The three-year outlook rose one tenth to 3.3%. Households expect higher prices for gasoline, food, medical care, education, and rent. The labor answers were better: unemployment expectations, quit rates, and the odds of finding a new job all improved, and income expectations rose a touch. But the NY Fed said more households report a worse financial situation than a year ago and expect a worse one a year from now.

One piece of good inflation news. Manheim's September used vehicle index fell 1.1% from August and 0.6% from a year ago. Used cars feed the goods part of CPI. Manheim blamed record diesel prices, the Middle East conflict, and climbing interest rates. It also noted the first September in decades with no Atlantic hurricane, which removed the usual bump in replacement demand.

The auction that helped Wednesday. In a separate Wednesday post, Boockvar called the 10-year note auction solid. It sold at 5.30%, almost 2 basis points below where it traded just before. The bid-to-cover was 2.77, well above the one-year average of 2.51 and tied for the highest since late 2014. Direct and indirect buyers took 97.5%, the most he has seen. The 10-year backed off to 5.27% from 5.31% right after.

The oil and Europe backdrop. His Wednesday note quoted Saudi Aramco's CEO: the world entered the Iran conflict with about 10 billion barrels of inventory and is down to about 6 billion, most of it not practically available. He said it could take up to two years to normalize even if the Strait fully reopened. French bond yields keep hitting new highs, and US yields are rising with them.

The takeaway for your clients: strong demand at 5.3% is a floor of sorts, but hawkish Fed talk, rising inflation expectations, and thin oil supply keep pressure on the ceiling.

6) Property tax delinquency hits 5.2%, the highest since 2017, per Cotality's study of 8 million non-escrowed loans

So-what: More owners who pay their own taxes are falling behind, which is a fresh reason to push escrow accounts on new loans and to watch HOA and tax liens on refinance title work.

1-minute read

Cotality's 2026 Property Tax Delinquency Report says the national delinquency rate has climbed to 5.2% so far this year, up from 4.2% in 2025. That is the highest level since 2017. It is still under the long-term average of 5.4%, and Cotality says part of the jump is seasonal.

The study covers about 8 million mortgages where the owner pays property taxes directly instead of through an escrow account. It draws on roughly 15 million tax reporting events.

Cotality's Sean Graham said delinquency moved from the lowest level in its data to the highest since 2017 in a matter of months. Mississippi led in 2025 at 15.1%. Louisiana had the biggest jump, from 5.8% to 9.0%. Arizona improved from 9.2% to 7.2%.

One surprise: state unemployment rates had no meaningful link to delinquency. HOA liens and mortgages 90 or more days past due were better predictors.

Why it matters for you: borrowers who waive escrow are the group falling behind. When a client asks to waive escrow, this data is a good reason to talk them out of it. On refinances, expect more tax and HOA liens to show up on title.

Expand 3-minute read

HousingWire reported Wednesday afternoon on Cotality's 2026 Property Tax Delinquency Report. The headline number: the national property tax delinquency rate has reached 5.2% so far in 2026, up from 4.2% in 2025. That is the highest reading since 2017.

Some context keeps this from sounding worse than it is. The rate is still below Cotality's long-term average of 5.4%. Cotality also says part of the 2026 year-to-date figure reflects seasonal patterns, since tax due dates cluster at certain times of year. The speed of the change is what stands out. Sean Graham, senior vice president of servicing and payment solutions at Cotality, said delinquency went from the lowest level in the company's data to the highest since 2017 in a matter of months. He called property taxes one of the largest costs of homeownership and said a rise this size shows more households are finding those bills harder to manage.

What the study covers matters for brokers. Cotality only looked at mortgages where homeowners pay property taxes directly to their local taxing authority, meaning loans without an escrow account. That is about 8 million non-escrowed mortgages and roughly 15 million tax reporting events. Borrowers with escrow do not show up here because the servicer pays the bill.

The state numbers are wide and sticky. Mississippi had the highest rate in 2025 at 15.1%, nearly three times the national rate. It has ranked first in 13 of the 14 years since 2012. Kansas at 9.6%, New Jersey at 9.5%, Louisiana at 9.0% and Massachusetts at 8.9% round out the top five. The lowest rates were North Dakota at 1.4%, Wisconsin at 1.5%, Wyoming at 1.6%, Illinois at 2.1% and Minnesota at 2.3%. California was not named in either list in the article.

Louisiana had the sharpest increase, rising from 5.8% to 9.0%. Alabama went from 5.2% to 7.1% and Kansas from 8.1% to 9.6%. On the other side, New Mexico fell from 9.4% to 5.4%, Washington, D.C. from 9.5% to 6.0%, and Arizona from 9.2% to 7.2%.

Two findings are worth remembering. First, state unemployment rates had no meaningful relationship with property tax delinquency. Delinquency tracked more closely with HOA liens and with mortgages more than 90 days past due. That suggests the stress is about the total cost of owning a home, not just job loss. Second, how a state collects unpaid taxes matters. States that sell lien certificates to investors averaged a 6.1% delinquency rate, compared with 4.5% in states where counties sell the property itself. In lien states, owners get more time before they risk losing the home, and Graham said that extra time seems to lead to higher delinquency.

What this means for you. When a borrower asks to waive escrow to keep cash in hand, this report is a concrete reason to push back. The people falling behind are exactly the ones managing their own tax bills. Many lenders also charge a pricing hit for waiving escrow, so the borrower may not even save money.

On refinances and cash-out deals, expect title to turn up more unpaid tax and HOA liens, which have to be cleared at closing. Ask about tax status early so it does not delay a file. And for clients in high-tax states, include realistic tax and HOA numbers in the payment conversation up front. Insurance and taxes are rising faster than many buyers expect, and this data shows what happens when they are underestimated.

7) CalculatedRisk: asking rents may be near a bottom; Apartment List shows rents down 0.4% from a year ago

So-what: If rents stop falling, the rent-versus-buy math stops getting worse for buyers, but with 30-year rates above 7.5% renting still wins on monthly payment in most markets.

1-minute read

Bill McBride at CalculatedRisk posted his monthly rent review Thursday morning with a clear headline: there are emerging signs that asking rents are near a bottom.

The lead data point comes from Apartment List. The national median rent fell 0.1% in September from August, the first monthly drop since January, and is down 0.4% from a year ago. Apartment List says the monthly dip is normal for fall, when fewer people move and landlords offer small discounts to fill units.

The bigger story is supply. The apartment construction boom peaked in 2024 with more than 600,000 new units, the most in a single year since 1986. Deliveries have slowed since then. Apartment List says occupancy is now hitting a turning point at the same time as rent growth, after nearly four years of soft conditions.

Why it matters for you: for nearly four years, falling or flat rents have made renting look cheap next to buying at 7% plus. If rents bottom and start rising again in 2027, that gap narrows. That is a useful talking point with renters who are waiting, as long as you do not promise timing.

Expand 3-minute read

Bill McBride at CalculatedRisk posted his monthly rent and vacancy review on Thursday, October 8, at about 7:18 AM PT. His title sums up the message: emerging signs that asking rents are near a bottom. McBride pulls together several private rent surveys each month. Part of this post is open to the public and part is for paid subscribers. This summary covers the public portion.

Start with Apartment List. Its national rent report for September shows rents down 0.1% from August and down 0.4% from September 2025. That was the first monthly decline since January. Apartment List says this is expected at this time of year. Fewer renters move in the fall and winter, so property owners offer modest discounts to fill vacant units. In other words, the September dip by itself is seasonal, not a new leg down.

The more important part is the supply story. Apartment List says a historic surge in multifamily construction has kept the rental market soft for nearly four years. The boom peaked in 2024, when more than 600,000 new apartment units hit the market. That was the most new supply in a single year since 1986. Deliveries have slowed a lot since then, although they are still fairly strong by historic standards.

Even at the tail end of the boom, the market had trouble absorbing all those new units. Apartment List now says that is finally changing. Occupancy is hitting an inflection point at the same time as rent growth. That combination, fewer new units coming and more of the existing ones filling up, is what usually comes right before rents firm up.

McBride's post also covers Realtor.com data, noting a 37th straight month of year-over-year declines in rents. The details of that section sit behind his paywall, so they are not summarized here. The headline alone tells you the direction: rents across several surveys have been flat to lower for about three years.

Why does this matter for a mortgage broker? Rent is the main alternative to buying. Over the last few years, the case for waiting has been easy to make. Rents were flat or falling while mortgage rates climbed from the 6s toward the high 7s. A renter could sign a lease, wait, and lose very little. If rents bottom here and start rising next year, the cost of waiting starts to grow again.
