Scotsman Guide's write-up adds the numbers. MBA's average 30-year rate climbed from 6.8% to nearly 7.5% during September. That is a big payment jump in a single month for a buyer who was already house hunting.

Lance Lambert's ResiClub newsletter on Tuesday evening shows home price softening is spreading again, now that the 30-year rate is back above 7%.

Here are the numbers. Based on ResiClub's analysis of the Zillow Home Value Index, U.S. home prices were up 1.0% from September of last year. That is a slowdown from the 1.3% yearly gain in August. Monthly price changes were covered in Monday's brief. What is new today is the broader count of falling markets and the slower annual pace.

Among the nation's 300 largest housing markets, 66 posted a year-over-year price decline in September. That is 22% of markets. In August, the count was 54, or 18%. After a year in which that number was shrinking, it is rising again. Among just the 50 largest metros, 17, or 34%, are posting negative yearly price growth.

Location drives most of the difference. Prices are still rising in pockets where active inventory is well below pre-pandemic 2019 levels, such as parts of the Northeast and Midwest. Prices are correcting, slipping or flat in pockets of Texas, Florida, Washington and Colorado, where inventory is well above 2019 levels.

Lambert explains why the Sun Belt and Mountain West are softest. Many of those areas saw the biggest price jumps during the pandemic boom, with prices outrunning local incomes. Then migration slowed, rates rose in 2022, and builders added lots of new supply. Builders are often willing to cut prices or offer incentives, which pulls some buyers away from resale homes and pushes resale inventory higher.

The split between strong and weak markets is striking. Hartford, Connecticut is now 28.6% above its 2022 peak. Austin, Texas is 28.1% below its 2022 peak. ResiClub says the range itself is fairly normal historically, but the split in direction, with some markets rising and others falling while the national number sits near zero, is wider than usual.

There is a key point for buyers. Most of the 234 markets still posting gains are rising slower than incomes and consumer prices. Economists call that a real, or inflation-adjusted, price decline. So even where prices are up a little, homes are slowly becoming a bit more affordable relative to paychecks, before you factor in rates.

What to watch next: Lambert says the big question is how much more softening comes with rates back above 7% on top of already strained affordability. He recommends closely tracking local active inventory over the next several months. Rising inventory usually comes before price weakness.

For the show, this is a good way to frame the market for Emily and Jake. The national number is basically flat. Local markets are going different ways, so national headlines can mislead. In places with growing inventory, buyers have real leverage to negotiate. In tight markets, prices are still holding. A possible policy change landed overnight that could matter for a lot of existing borrowers.

Scotsman Guide reported Tuesday that it obtained a draft executive order titled "Decreasing Housing Costs for the American People." Multiple industry sources who have seen it said it could be issued by the White House as soon as Wednesday. The draft is subject to change and may not be issued at all. The White House and FHFA did not respond to requests for comment. Scotsman Guide also notes it is unclear whether all of the directives fit within current mortgage laws and agency authority.

Here is what the draft says, in five main areas.

First, government loans. It would direct USDA and the VA to lower mortgage rates on single-family loans using their own agency authorities. The draft does not spell out how much or how.

Second, mortgage insurance. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac would review their portfolios for borrowers eligible to have mortgage insurance cancelled proactively, within safety rules and contracts. Servicers could be required to notify borrowers promptly when their MI may be cancelled and to cut unnecessary paperwork and appraisal costs for cancellation. In plain terms, homeowners who have built equity could see PMI drop off without having to fight for it.

Third, refinance closing costs. FHFA Director Bill Pulte could greatly expand a title insurance waiver pilot that started in 2024. That would mean removing geographic limits, allowing borrowers with less than 20% equity, and certifying more title providers to increase competition.

Fourth, pricing and paperwork. The draft calls for reducing or eliminating loan-level price adjustments on eligible refinances. LLPAs are the risk-based fees that push rates up for lower credit scores or smaller down payments. It would also waive appraisals, income re-verification and other documents for limited cash-out refinances that lower the monthly payment, where Fannie and Freddie data support it. Lenders would have to pass the savings through and tell borrowers about them.

Fifth, supply. A task force co-chaired by Pulte and HUD Secretary Scott Turner, with Commerce, VA and USDA, would work to standardize permitting for manufactured and factory-built housing. It would also look at tying some federal grants to states and cities adopting those standards.

Context matters. Scotsman Guide notes rates are now above 7%, versus around 6% in February, and two-thirds of existing borrowers have rates under 5%. The draft follows Pulte's posts last weekend about an LLPA decision before year end and fewer credit reports, which we covered Tuesday. This is the first look at what that could include.

Voters care about this too. A Clever Real Estate survey of 1,000 Americans, cited by Mortgage Nuggets, found their top housing asks are lower property taxes at 60%, lower homeowners insurance at 53%, and lower mortgage rates at 53%. And 63% doubt the midterms will change much on housing.

For the show, keep it simple and careful. Most of this draft targets refinances and existing owners, not first-time buyers. Nothing is final. If it is signed, we will break down who qualifies.

Buyers usually budget for property taxes. Many do not budget enough for insurance.

A Bloomberg Intelligence analysis, reported in the LA Times on Tuesday, finds the average home insurance premium is now higher than the average property tax bill in two-thirds of U.S. counties.

Home insurance premiums, averaged by county, are up 45% in a decade after adjusting for inflation. Nebraska is up 79%.

On average, homeowners pay 14% more for insurance than for property tax at the county level. Add the weather-related part of auto insurance and it is 30% more, and the combined cost tops property tax in 75% of counties.

Hail and winter weather are driving increases in the Midwest and Mountain West, led by Colorado, Utah and Minnesota. In the Southeast, where property taxes are low, insurance can run 2.5 to 4 times the tax bill. In the West and Northeast, high property taxes make insurance a smaller share.

Deductibles are also rising, so owners pay more out of pocket before coverage kicks in.

So what: when buyers compare homes or states, quote insurance early. It can change what they qualify for and what they can comfortably pay.

Expand 3-minute read

This is a strong show topic because it hits the monthly payment directly, and buyers rarely think about it until escrow.

The finding. A Bloomberg Intelligence analysis, published Monday and reported by Bloomberg's Eric Roston in the LA Times on Tuesday, finds that the average home insurance premium now exceeds the average property tax bill in two-thirds of U.S. counties. The analysis uses data from the U.S. Census, state insurance regulators, and federal weather and transportation agencies.

How fast premiums rose. Homeowner policies, averaged at the county level, are up 45% in a decade after adjusting for inflation. Some states rose much faster. Nebraska is up 79%.

The gap. Homeowners pay a county-level average of 14% more for insurance than for property tax, according to Bloomberg Intelligence's Andrew John Stevenson. The analysts also estimated the part of auto insurance driven by extreme weather. When that is added, the total is 30% more than property tax, and the combined cost is higher than property tax in 75% of counties.

Where it hurts most. Hail and winter weather are pushing rates up in the Midwest and Mountain West, led by Colorado, Utah and Minnesota. Hail is also raising auto insurance in the Great Plains and Upper Midwest. Southeastern states have some of the lowest property taxes in the country, and there, home insurance can now run 2.5 to 4 times the tax bill. In the Northeast, the West and big metro areas, property taxes are high, so insurance is a smaller share of the tax bill.

Deductibles. Deductibles have been rising too. That means families pay more of the repair bill themselves before the insurer steps in.

Disaster funding. The story also notes a shift in who pays for recovery. Over the past two decades, the federal government has paid about 90% of disaster recovery. That ratio is now approaching 50-50. After Hurricane Harvey in 2017, Texas received $23 billion in federal funds versus $4 billion in local spending. After the January 2025 Los Angeles fires, the U.S. gave California $2.6 billion while Californians spent $3.5 billion. Stevenson said local budgets "just don't have that kind of capital on hand." Climate Central data puts 2025 disaster losses at a five-year average of $156 billion, 10 times the 1985 figure after inflation.

The advice. Alexandra Glickman of Arthur J. Gallagher said insurance is less affordable but no less necessary, and policyholders should be aware of changing risk and be "willing to pay the premium for it."

What to tell buyers on the show. First, the payment is not just principal and interest. Taxes and insurance can add a lot, and insurance is now the bigger of the two in most counties. This is an opinion column, but it adds a practical angle to the appraisal change Josh covered on Oct. 14, and the date is now close.

The change. On Nov. 2, the residential appraisal report "stops being a form," as HousingWire contributor Nikola Gojkovic puts it. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are moving to structured data aligned with the MISMO reference model, the industry data standard. Fannie describes the goal as a single data-driven, flexible appraisal report for any residential property type. Freddie says the data that describes the property drives the report, not a form type or number. Gojkovic says most coverage so far has been about the calendar: who can still submit under the old standard, collateral risk scoring during a reduced functionality window, and which approvals a seller needs from each agency.

His point. The appraisal is usually not the first valuation on a home. The first is the comparative market analysis, or CMA, that a listing agent brings to the seller. The CMA sets the asking price and the expectations of everyone who touches the file afterward.

A CMA adjustment grid looks a lot like an appraisal grid: a dollar figure next to each comparable sale, with a reason like size, bathrooms, age or condition. A homeowner assumes those numbers came from the local market. Sometimes they did. Often, Gojkovic says, they came from one rate applied across every comp, and the finished document looks the same either way.

He is careful not to call one method sloppy. Applying a consistent rate is defensible and sometimes the only honest option. Paired sales analysis needs two recent sales that match on everything but one feature, and in neighborhoods with varied older homes that pair may not happen in a year. Fannie's Selling Guide lists paired sales as one way to set adjustments, along with statistical analysis and modeling. USPAP, the appraisal standards, sets no method. What is required is that adjustments reflect how the market reacts to differences between properties.

The problem, he writes, is that the CMA does not say how its numbers were made.

What changes in November. After the switch, two valuations on the same home will be different kinds of things. The appraisal will be a structured record that can be read, searched and checked field by field. The CMA will still be a document with a figure and a label. When an appraisal comes in under contract price, the listing agent will have to explain a gap between two things that are no longer alike, and Gojkovic says the standard the client is judging them by "has moved without anybody telling them."

Why it matters for Josh. Low appraisals are one of the most stressful moments for a buyer. In a cooling market, with builders cutting prices and pending sales falling, appraisal gaps may come up more often. Under the new format, the lender side will have a more detailed, checkable report. That can help a loan officer walk buyers and agents through exactly which comparisons drove the value. It may also lead buyers' agents to press listing agents on how they priced the home.

Practical step: before Nov. 2, make sure the team knows how the new appraisal data will look in the LOS and how to explain it to clients and agent partners.

So what: this is a back-office change with a front-of-house effect. It is not a main show topic, but it could be a quick tip for buyers on why appraisals and list prices disagree.
