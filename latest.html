5) Lawler: the MBS yield is 6.36%, near a 3-year high, but the MBS-to-10-year spread is only 1.12 points vs. 1.73 the last time it was here

Wed show

So-what: Bond investors are not the main reason rates are high right now; the 10-year is. Real relief needs Treasury yields or bond volatility to come down.

1-minute read

Housing economist Tom Lawler, writing Monday in Bill McBride's CalculatedRisk newsletter, says the current-coupon MBS yield finished last week at 6.36%. That is the yield on the mortgage bonds that new loans get packaged into. It is a touch below its late-September close but still near the highest level since early November 2023.

Here is the twist. On Nov. 13, 2023, the MBS yield was also 6.36%. But the 10-year Treasury was 4.63% then, versus 5.24% on Oct. 9. So the gap between MBS and the 10-year is 1.12 points today, versus 1.73 back then.

Lawler gives three reasons. Bond market volatility was far higher in 2023: the MOVE index was 172.8 then and 98.5 now. The yield curve was deeply inverted then and slopes upward now. And model-based option-adjusted spreads were higher then.

He adds that MBS spreads in October have held steady at the somewhat wider levels that began when rate volatility jumped in late September.

Bottom line: mortgage bond pricing is tracking Treasuries closely. The 10-year is doing the damage.

Expand 3-minute read

Tom Lawler's Monday update in the CalculatedRisk newsletter is a useful reality check on the "spreads are the problem" story. His point: mortgage bonds are not unusually cheap versus Treasuries right now. The 10-year itself is high.

The key number. The current-coupon MBS yield closed last week at 6.36%. That is a little below the end of September, but still close to the highest since early November 2023. His chart shows the same yield was below 4.9% around late February and has climbed steadily since, with a sharp leg up from late August into early October.

The comparison table. Lawler found four days when the MBS yield was exactly 6.36% and lined up the Treasury curve on each:

July 12, 2006: 10-year at 5.10%, spread 1.26 points, MOVE 64.9.

June 14, 2007: 10-year at 5.23%, spread 1.13 points, MOVE 63.6.

Nov. 13, 2023: 10-year at 4.63%, spread 1.73 points, MOVE 172.8.

Oct. 9, 2026: 10-year at 5.24%, spread 1.12 points, MOVE 98.5.

So today's spread looks a lot like 2006 and 2007, when the market was calm, not like 2023, when it was stressed.

Why 2023 was different. First, implied rate volatility was much higher, and MBS investors demand more yield when rates are jumpy because borrowers' refinance behavior gets harder to predict. Second, the curve was inverted then: the 10-year minus the 1-year was negative 0.74. Today it is positive 0.77. Third, models showed a higher option-adjusted spread then, meaning investors were charging more for risk after accounting for prepayments.

Recent trend. Lawler says October spreads have been stable at the wider levels that started when volatility jumped in late September. His MOVE chart shows a spike above 110 in late September, now easing back to 98.5.

How this squares with yesterday's brief. Yesterday's 2.12% figure compared the 30-year mortgage rate to the 10-year. That gap includes the MBS-to-Treasury piece Lawler tracks, plus the part between the MBS yield and the rate a borrower gets. That second piece covers guarantee fees, servicing, and lender margin. Lawler's data says the bond investor piece is not unusually wide. So if the full mortgage spread looks wide, more of it is sitting in that second piece.

What it means for you. There is less room for rates to fall just from MBS spreads tightening than some headlines suggest. The bigger levers are the 10-year yield and volatility. If the MOVE index keeps easing, spreads could narrow a bit more, but the clean win needs the 10-year to come down. With the 1-year at 4.47% and the 10-year at 5.24%, the upward curve may also make shorter fixed periods and ARMs worth pricing side by side for some borrowers.

For Wednesday's show, a simple slide works: same 6.36% MBS yield in 2023 and today, very different 10-year, very different spread. Message to Emily and Jake: rates are high mostly because Treasury yields are high, so watch the 10-year.

6) MBA's Broeksmit: tri-merge credit reports 'won't be around for long' as FHFA weighs bi-merge or single-pull

Wed show

So-what: Pulling all three bureaus on every agency loan is expensive. A bi-merge or single-pull shift would cut originator and borrower credit costs after years of fee pressure.

1-minute read

At MBA's 2026 Annual Convention in Chicago, president and CEO Bob Broeksmit said he is "increasingly confident that tri-merge won't be around for long. And the sooner it goes away, the better." Most Fannie and Freddie loans still require a tri-merge report that pulls data and scores from Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. Broeksmit has pushed a single-pull model for lower-risk loans as lenders face tighter margins and higher compliance costs.

FHFA Director Bill Pulte has said on social media that a bi-merge option (two bureaus instead of three) is under consideration and that the agency is also studying a single-pull path. Pulte canceled his Monday in-person MBA appearance; industry watchers had expected a possible bi-merge announcement around the convention. The Consumer Data Industry Association pushed back, arguing more data produces better outcomes and keeps borrowing costs down.

This sits next to last week's FHFA move opening VantageScore 4.0 to all GSE lenders and the bureaus' score-later tools. The new angle today is the report itself: how many bureaus you must pull, not which score model you buy. For shops like BuyWise, fewer mandatory pulls would lower per-file cost and borrower fees if FHFA follows through.

Expand 3-minute read

The credit fight at MBA Annual moved from score models to the credit report itself. Broeksmit told the Chicago convention that credit scores matter, but credit reports matter even more. His line that stuck: the specific reform is still being worked out, but he is increasingly confident tri-merge will not be around for long, and the sooner it goes away the better. He said FHFA has known for years that tri-merge is outdated and unnecessary and understands the need to lower costs fast.

Today most agency loans require a tri-merge: one pull that merges Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. That three-bureau view is the long-standing GSE standard. It also stacks fees on every file. Broeksmit has been the industry's loudest voice for at least a single-pull option on lower-risk loans as margins tighten. FHFA's Pulte recently floated a bi-merge option on social media and said stronger single-pull ideas are also under study. A bi-merge would let lenders pick two of the three bureaus. HousingWire noted that many in the industry expected Pulte to announce a bi-merge option at MBA Annual. He then canceled his Monday in-person slot and was listed for a virtual Tuesday appearance instead, so the formal policy drop did not land on stage Monday.

The credit bureaus' trade group is not rolling over. CDIA president and CEO Dan Smith said more data, not less, produces better outcomes and keeps borrowing costs down, and that maintaining tri-merge is critical to safety and soundness. That sets up a clear fight: originators want fewer paid pulls; the bureaus want the three-file standard to stay.

Cost pressure is why Broeksmit is pressing now. Lenders have seen credit-report and score fees climb for years while purchase volume stays soft. Requiring all three bureaus, MBA argues, also limits competition among the repositories for mortgage volume. Separately, last week's brief already covered FHFA telling Fannie and Freddie to let all lenders use VantageScore 4.0, plus TransUnion and Equifax score-later tools and FHA's 2027 multi-score path. Those stories were about which score you buy. Today's story is how many bureaus you must buy from before you even pick a score.

For a retail broker, the so-what is unit cost and borrower cash to close. Every mandatory third-bureau pull is money on a thin-margin purchase file. If FHFA lands bi-merge or single-pull with guardrails, expect LOS and AUS updates, overlay rewrites, and a period where investors and MI partners disagree on what "enough credit data" means. Broeksmit also flagged appraisal readiness for Uniform Appraisal Dataset 3.6 after too many appraisers, AMCs, vendors, and some lenders were not ready for the November deadline. MBA chairman Owen Lee said a new Basel III capital proposal is expected by year-end and unveiled an affordability action plan of 20 reforms that he said need no new laws. The credit-report line is still the one that hits every funded loan. Watch for an FHFA notice, not just convention rhetoric.

Expand 3-minute read

Lance Lambert's latest ResiClub PRO charts put hard numbers on something originators already feel in underwriting: builder buyers are carrying much higher debt-to-income ratios than during the pandemic boom. The analysis covers mortgage-financed buyers only (cash buyers are out) and uses AEI Housing Center data across large U.S. builders from 2012 through 2025.

Start with averages. Every builder and ownership group in the table posted its highest average DTI of the entire 2012–2025 series sometime between 2023 and 2025. From 2021 to 2025, Ashton Woods' average buyer DTI rose from 37.8% to 43.0%. LGI Homes went from 39.5% to 44.7%. Lennar from 37.0% to 42.2%. Move-up names stretched too: Toll Brothers from 33.6% to 37.6%, PulteGroup from 35.4% to 39.5%. Higher DTI means a larger share of gross income is already spoken for by the mortgage and other debts.

The share above key thresholds is starker. Forty-three percent was the old Qualified Mortgage DTI cap before the CFPB moved to a pricing-based test, so it remains a useful stress line. In 2021 no giant builder in the set had more than 41% of financed buyers above 43% DTI. By 2025 six did, at or above half: LGI Homes 61%, Ashton Woods 55%, Lennar 51%, Century Communities 51%, KB Home 50%, and D.R. Horton 50%. Biggest jumps from 2021 to 2025: Ashton Woods +22 points, Stanley Martin +21, LGI and Lennar +20 each. Toll Brothers rose from 16% to 28%.

Above 50% DTI is the conventional GSE ceiling for many files. Buyers over that line often land in FHA, which allows higher DTIs. Ashton Woods' share above 50% rose from about 10% in 2021 to 24% in 2025. LGI went from 16% to 26%, D.R. Horton from 13% to 20%, Century from 10% to 20%. Even Pulte and Taylor Morrison moved from the low single digits to about 10%.

When the buydown cannot get a buyer under 50%, FHA often takes the file. Ashton Woods' FHA share of financed sales rose from 15% in 2022 to 39% more recently as its above-50% DTI share tripled.

Credit scores are the twist. Average scores have not deteriorated much even as DTIs spiked. LGI's 2025 average near 707 is still above its 2012–2020 range. So the story is payment stretch, not a wave of weak-credit buyers. For Emily and Jake shopping new construction, ask what buydown is baked into the payment used for DTI, whether the loan is conventional or FHA, and how much payment shock hits when a temporary buydown expires. For the show, this is the chart set behind "builders are making the payment work." Household budget buffer is thinner on recent vintages if incomes wobble.

Prop. 37 is the biggest home-finance item on California's Nov. 3 ballot, and the WSJ editorial board just put it on the national radar. Here is what the measure actually does, based on the Legislative Analyst's Office (LAO), the Secretary of State's guide, and CalMatters.

The money. CalHFA could sell up to $25 billion in revenue bonds. These are not general obligation bonds. The state General Fund does not repay them. The loan. It is called a "middle-class homeownership loan." It covers up to 17% of the purchase price. The buyer must put in at least 3%. The ballot label says the loans are fixed rate. CalMatters says buyers repay in monthly installments and that private lenders, not taxpayers, take the loss if a borrower defaults.

The fine print on eligibility. The buyer must be a California resident, live in the home, and earn no more than double the area median income. The home must be newly built, priced below about $1.5 million, and the buyer must be its first purchaser. Resales do not count.

The builder angle. Any developer can build homes that qualify. Developers can also choose a "qualified builder option" that requires higher labor standards. In return, those builders get different construction defect rules that the LAO says generally give developers more flexibility. That is a big reason labor and builders both care.
