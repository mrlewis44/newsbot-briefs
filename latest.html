2) MBA: 30-year contract rate falls to 7.30%, ARM share hits 11.4%, but purchase apps are still 19% below last year

Wed show

So-what: Rates eased for a third week, yet buyers are still waiting, and more of the ones who act are choosing ARMs to cut the payment.

1-minute read

Lower rates are not pulling buyers off the fence yet.

The Mortgage Bankers Association said applications fell 1.2% in the week ending Oct. 30. Purchase applications fell 2% from the week before and are 19% below the same week last year. Refinance applications were flat, up 0.1%, and remain about 60% below a year ago.

That came even as rates improved. MBA's average 30-year fixed contract rate dropped to 7.30% from 7.37%, the lowest since September. FHA fell to 7.02% from 7.12%. Jumbo fell to 7.20% from 7.28%.

The most interesting number is the ARM share. Adjustable-rate mortgages made up 11.4% of applications, the highest since September 2025. The 5/1 ARM rate fell to 6.29%, about a full point under the 30-year fixed. MBA's Mike Fratantoni said more borrowers are choosing ARMs "to lower their initial payments."

The so-what for the show: buyers are payment shoppers right now. An ARM can lower the first several years of payments, but it carries real reset risk. It fits some borrowers, not all, and it deserves a careful side-by-side.

Expand 3-minute read

The MBA weekly survey is one of the best real-time reads on borrower behavior. This week's message: rates are drifting lower, but buyers are still cautious.

The headline numbers for the week ending Oct. 30:

Total applications fell 1.2% on a seasonally adjusted basis. Purchase applications fell 2% seasonally adjusted and 4% unadjusted. Compared with the same week a year ago, purchase activity was down 19%. Refinance applications rose 0.1% and were 60% lower than a year ago. Refinances made up 39.8% of applications, up from 38.9%.

Rates in the survey:

The average 30-year fixed with a conforming balance fell to 7.30% from 7.37%. That is the lowest since September. Jumbo loans fell to 7.20% from 7.28%. FHA loans fell to 7.02% from 7.12%. The 15-year fixed was the odd one out, rising to 6.76% from 6.70%. The 5/1 ARM fell to 6.29% from 6.41%.

The ARM story:

ARM share reached 11.4%, the highest since September 2025. MBA's chief economist Mike Fratantoni said activity "is still quite slow" even with rates at their lowest in more than a month, and that more borrowers are choosing ARMs to lower their initial payments.

Why this matters: a 5/1 ARM has a fixed rate for five years, then adjusts once a year. At 6.29% versus 7.30%, the gap is about a full percentage point. On a $600,000 loan, that is very roughly $400 a month less in principal and interest during the fixed period. That is real money for a first-time buyer in California.

The risk is the reset. If rates are higher in five years, the payment can go up, sometimes a lot. Lenders must show the caps, which limit how much the rate can rise at each adjustment and over the life of the loan, so the worst case can be calculated up front. An ARM is a tool for people with a plan: they expect to sell, refinance, or see their income rise, and they can handle a higher payment if none of that happens.

Why buyers are still waiting:

Calculated Risk's Bill McBride noted purchase activity remains depressed and refinance activity is historically very low. Redfin's latest data, covered elsewhere in today's brief, shows pending sales at a record low and a record monthly payment for the typical home. Prices are flat, inventory is up, and buyers have more choices. That combination often makes people wait for something better.

Government share:

FHA share fell to 16.0% from 16.6%. VA share was 11.2%. Those are the loans many first-time buyers use.

For the show:

This is a good segment to explain three things in plain terms. First, rates have improved for several weeks but are still near 7%. Second, an ARM is not a trick or a trap by default, but it needs a stress test: what happens if the rate adjusts up by the cap? Third, buyers who are ready have leverage today, with more inventory and more motivated sellers. Waiting for a perfect rate means competing again when everyone else comes back.

3) Treasury keeps next week's refunding at $125 billion and holds auction sizes steady, with a small wording change markets are watching

Wed show

So-what: No surprise supply means no new pressure on long-term rates from Washington today, which helps mortgage pricing.

1-minute read

The Treasury Department did what markets expected, and that is good news for mortgage rates today.

Every three months, Treasury announces how much long-term debt it will sell. This morning it said next week's "refunding" auctions will total $125 billion: $58 billion of 3-year notes on Monday, Nov. 9, $42 billion of 10-year notes on Tuesday, Nov. 10, and $25 billion of 30-year bonds on Thursday, Nov. 12. That raises about $28 billion of new cash.

Sizes are unchanged. Treasury said it expects to keep note and bond auction sizes the same "for the next several quarters." Some market watchers noted the wording changed slightly from past statements. Peter Boockvar said the tweaks may give Treasury Secretary Bessent room to shift borrowing toward shorter-term debt later.

Why it matters: when Treasury sells more long-term debt, investors usually demand higher yields. Mortgage rates tend to follow the 10-year yield. No increase means no new upward push today.

Watch the 10-year auction on Nov. 10. Weak demand at that sale can lift rates for a day or two.

Expand 3-minute read

The quarterly refunding announcement sounds technical, but it ties directly to mortgage rates.

What Treasury announced:

At 8:30 AM ET today, Treasury said it will auction $125 billion of notes and bonds next week. The schedule is a 3-year note of $58 billion on Monday, Nov. 9; a 10-year note of $42 billion on Tuesday, Nov. 10; and a 30-year bond of $25 billion on Thursday, Nov. 12. All three auctions are at 1 PM ET. They settle on Nov. 16. The sales raise about $28 billion of new cash beyond what is needed to pay off maturing debt.

The forward guidance:

Treasury said it plans to maintain nominal coupon and floating-rate note auction sizes "for the next several quarters." It also said it is watching demand for bills, which are short-term Treasury debt, and the Federal Reserve's bill purchases. Inflation-protected (TIPS) auction sizes were also unchanged.

The wording detail:

The Wall Street Journal noted that the guidance dropped the words "at least" before "the next several quarters." That small change could be read as Treasury keeping its options open. Peter Boockvar's read this morning was that yields barely moved on the news, and that the wording gives Bessent flexibility to shift more issuance toward shorter maturities later. Treat that as one analyst's interpretation, not a policy decision.

Why mortgage people should care:

Mortgage rates move with the 10-year Treasury yield plus a spread. When the government sells more long-term debt, buyers need to absorb more supply, and they often want a higher yield to do it. That pushes the 10-year up and drags mortgage rates with it. The opposite is also true. Selling more short-term bills and fewer long-term bonds tends to take pressure off long-term rates.

Earlier this week, Treasury estimated it will borrow about $618 billion this quarter and about $800 billion next quarter. Those are large numbers. So the fact that long-term auction sizes are not increasing matters. The extra borrowing is coming through bills, at least for now.

What to watch next week:

The 10-year auction on Tuesday, Nov. 10 is the one that matters most. Traders watch the "tail," which is whether the auction clears at a higher yield than expected. A weak auction can add a few basis points to the 10-year in an afternoon, and lenders sometimes reprice mortgage rates the same day. A strong auction does the opposite.

The 30-year auction on Thursday, Nov. 12 is smaller but can move long-term rates too.

How to say it on the show:

Washington did not add new pressure on long-term rates today. That is a quiet win. The big risks for rates in the next week are Friday's jobs report and next week's auctions, not today's announcement. Nobody can promise where rates go, but today removed one possible headwind.

Two housing measures passed in California, according to the Associated Press, with results still being counted.

Prop 1 passed with about 59.4% yes. It lets the state borrow $10 billion for affordable housing and related infrastructure plus $1.25 billion for a veterans home loan program. The Legislative Analyst's Office estimates it could support up to 40,000 rental units and help 40,000 families buy a home.

Yesterday was Election Day. Here are only the housing results that matter for buyers and lenders in California, based on Secretary of State semi-official returns as of early Wednesday and calls by the Associated Press.

Prop 1: Veterans and affordable housing bond. Passed.

About 59.4% voted yes. The measure authorizes $11.25 billion in state borrowing: $10 billion for affordable housing and related infrastructure, and $1.25 billion for the CalVet home loan program for veterans. KQED, citing the Legislative Analyst's Office, said the money could support up to 40,000 rental units and help 40,000 families buy a home.

Prop 37: Middle-income homebuyer loans. Passed.

About 61% voted yes. The $25 billion bond creates a state down payment assistance loan program for buyers earning up to 200% of the area median income. That is a high cap, which means many middle-income families in coastal California could qualify. The key limit: it only applies to newly constructed homes. It was placed on the ballot by a coalition of real estate interests and building trades unions.

What we do not know yet: how much each loan will be, how repayment works, which lenders can participate, and when the first dollars will be available. Programs funded by state bonds usually take months to set up, and the bonds have to be sold first. Expect rules from the state housing finance agencies, not overnight.

Prop 43: Two-thirds vote for local special taxes. Trailing.

About 54.9% voted no. It would have required a two-thirds vote, instead of a simple majority, for special taxes that voters put on the ballot themselves. If it fails, local measures for things like schools or wildfire prevention keep the lower threshold. That can affect future property-related taxes and assessments in some cities.

Prop 45: CEQA fast track. Trailing.

About 54.1% voted no. It would have set a one-year limit on environmental reviews and a 270-day limit for court rulings on related lawsuits for nine types of projects. Supporters said CEQA is often used to delay housing. If it fails, the current rules stay in place.

Important caveat:

These numbers reflect roughly half of the expected vote. California counts mail ballots for weeks, and shares can shift. Props 43 and 45 had not been called. Final results are certified Dec. 11.

What it means for buyers:

Prop 37 is the one most relevant to the Buy Right, Borrow Smart audience. If the program launches as described, a household earning a good income but short on down payment could get help buying a new-construction home. That could also help builders move inventory, which may give buyers more negotiating room on new homes.

For now, the right message is simple. Do not wait to buy because of Prop 37. The program does not exist yet, and its timing is unknown. If you are already shopping new construction, keep an eye on it. Our team works with buyers across California and in every state we serve, and we will share program details once the state releases them.

At the same time, the typical monthly mortgage payment hit $2,786, the highest seasonally adjusted level in Redfin's records. That figure used a weekly average rate of 7.32%. So homes are not getting cheaper in a way buyers feel. Rates are doing the damage.

The so-what: this is a buyer's negotiating market in much of the country. A 0.1% dip is not a crash, and local markets vary widely.

This is a milestone number, so it is worth being careful with it.

What Redfin found:

The median U.S. home sale price was $389,601 in the four weeks ending Oct. 25, down 0.1% from a year earlier. That is the first year-over-year decline in about three years. The median asking price was $400,703.

The typical monthly mortgage payment reached $2,786, a record on a seasonally adjusted basis. That was calculated with a weekly average 30-year rate of 7.32%. Pending home sales, meaning homes that went under contract, hit the lowest level in Redfin's records, down nearly 16% from a year ago.

How to read it:

A 0.1% decline is basically flat. It is not a crash and it is not evidence that prices are about to fall sharply. Other measures we covered earlier this week also showed small gains, not declines. What it does show is that the market has lost its upward momentum.

The bigger story is the gap between prices and payments. Prices are flat, but the monthly payment is at a record because rates are around 7%. For buyers, the payment is what matters month to month. That is why so many people are sitting out.

Why rates moved:

Scotsman Guide noted that 30-year rates rose above 7% in September and moved higher in the first half of October. Freddie Mac's average was about 7.4% in the first two weeks of October, then eased to 7.24% by the end of the month as 10-year Treasury yields fell. Redfin's Chen Zhao said rates have fallen about 30 to 40 basis points from the late-September peak, and added that "there's little reason to believe the volatility will slow down."

What sellers are doing:

With sellers outnumbering active buyers, more sellers are paying for closing costs or cutting prices. Our next item has new survey numbers on just how much selling costs have risen.

What it means for buyers:

1. Leverage. In a slow market, buyers can ask for seller credits, repairs, or a rate buydown paid by the seller. A seller-paid buydown can lower the payment for the first year or two, or permanently, depending on how it is structured.

2. Timing. Pending sales at a record low means fewer buyers are competing right now. If rates fall meaningfully, those buyers may come back at the same time.

3. Local matters. National medians hide big differences. Freddie Mac's index shows some Florida and Texas metros well below their peaks, while other markets are still rising. Always look at the local numbers.

For the show:

The clean message is: prices are flat nationally, payments are high because of rates, and buyers who are ready have more negotiating room than they have had in years. We help buyers across California and in every state we serve run the numbers on price, rate, and seller credits together, because the best deal is often a mix of all three. Rates could move either way from here, so plan around a payment you can afford today.

6) Clever survey: the average cost to sell a home jumped to about $82,000 in 2026 from $62,245, and nearly 8 in 10 sellers gave buyers concessions

Wed show

So-what: Sellers are paying more to close deals, which is exactly why buyers should ask for credits, repairs, and flexible terms.

1-minute read

Selling a home has gotten a lot more expensive, and that is useful information for buyers.

Clever Real Estate surveyed recent sellers and found the average total cost to sell was nearly $82,000 in 2026. Last year it was about $62,245.

Where the money goes:

Repairs before and after listing averaged $27,649, up 32% from last year and about one-third of total costs. About 7 in 10 sellers made some repair or renovation.

Listing and buyer agent fees together averaged over $27,000.

Closing costs averaged a bit above $11,000.

Buyer concessions averaged nearly $10,000.

About 46% of sellers said selling cost more than they expected, up from 37% in 2025.

Nearly 8 in 10 sellers made at least one concession. A flexible closing date was most common at 36%. About 30% cut the price or offered an early move-in. About one-quarter paid for repairs, repair credits, or closing cost help.

The so-what: in a slow market, sellers expect to negotiate. Buyers should ask. A seller credit toward closing costs or a rate buydown can do more for the monthly payment than a small price cut.

Expand 3-minute read

This story is about sellers, but the lesson is for buyers.

The survey:

Clever Real Estate, a brokerage, surveyed homeowners who sold recently. The average cost of selling was nearly $82,000 in 2026, up from about $62,245 in 2025. That is roughly a 32% jump in one year. About 46% of sellers said the cost was higher than they expected, compared with 37% last year.

These are survey averages, not official data, and they include optional costs. Still, the direction is clear.

The breakdown:

Repairs were the biggest piece. Pre- and post-listing repairs averaged $27,649, about one-third of total costs and up 32% from last year. About 7 in 10 sellers did some repair or renovation. Scotsman Guide noted repair costs have climbed with inflation, supply chain problems, and labor shortages, and Verisk data showed repair cost increases accelerated in many states this year.

Agent commissions were second. Listing agent fees plus any buyer agent fees the seller agreed to pay averaged more than $27,000 combined.

Closing costs averaged slightly above $11,000.

Buyer concessions averaged nearly $10,000.

Concessions are now normal:

Nearly 8 in 10 sellers made at least one concession. The most common was a flexible closing date, at 36%. About 30% cut the price or allowed an early move-in. About a quarter paid for repairs, gave repair credits, or covered some closing costs. The report said some concessions only cost time and convenience, while others have a real dollar cost.

Why it is happening:

Years of weak demand mean sellers outnumber buyers in many markets. Redfin's latest data shows pending sales at a record low. When fewer buyers are shopping, sellers compete for them with money and flexibility.

How buyers can use this:

1. Ask for a seller credit. Many loan programs allow the seller to pay part of the buyer's closing costs. Limits vary by loan type and down payment, so check before writing the offer.

2. Consider a seller-paid rate buydown. Instead of a $10,000 price cut, the same money used to buy down the rate can lower the monthly payment more. The right choice depends on how long the buyer plans to keep the loan.

3. Use the inspection. Repairs are expensive for sellers too. A repair credit can be easier for a seller than doing the work before closing.

4. Be reasonable. Sellers are already absorbing higher costs. Offers that ask for everything can fall apart. A strong pre-approval and clean terms still matter.

For homeowners thinking about selling to buy their next home:

Budget realistically. If the survey is close to right, selling costs can eat a big part of equity. That affects how much is left for the next down payment. Run a net sheet before listing.

For the show:

This pairs well with the Redfin item. Prices are flat, payments are high, and sellers are paying more to get deals done. That is a window for prepared buyers in California and in every state we serve.

United Wholesale Mortgage, the largest lender in the broker channel, reported third-quarter results this morning.

UWM matters to us because it sets the tone for the wholesale channel, and many brokers send loans there. Here is what changed this quarter.

Volume:

Total originations were $33.4 billion, compared with $39.7 billion in the second quarter and $41.7 billion a year ago. Purchase volume held up best at $22.8 billion, versus $23.8 billion in Q2. Refinance volume fell to $10.6 billion from $15.9 billion, about a one-third drop, as rates moved higher over the summer. Government refinances fell the most.

UWM's servicing portfolio was $269.8 billion in unpaid balances with an average coupon of 5.97%. The company plans to sell $70 billion to $80 billion of mortgage servicing rights in the fourth quarter, expecting more than $1.8 billion in proceeds. Together with $400 million from a rights offering, that money is earmarked to pay down debt that does not fund loans. Mortgage Professional America reported its debt-to-equity ratio fell from 6.13 to 4.21, and expenses fell nearly 10% from Q2.

HousingWire reported, citing sources, that UWM sold about $60 billion to JPMorgan Chase at roughly a 6x multiple. The companies declined to comment.

Why MSRs are so valuable now:

HousingWire's coverage of an industry servicing conference this week said conventional, high-quality MSRs are trading near 7x, a level not seen in more than two decades. Some executives expect banks to buy more if capital rules change.

UWM also said about 25% of its borrowers get a better credit outcome using VantageScore 4.0.

What it means for brokers:

1. When UWM sells servicing, borrowers may get a new servicer. That is worth knowing for client retention and follow-up.

2. A lender that sells MSRs is less able to recapture those borrowers later. Brokers who stay in touch with past clients keep that relationship.

3. Lower expenses and stronger balance sheets can support pricing in a slow market. Lower margins also show competition is intense.

The earnings call is at 11 AM ET, which is 8 AM PT.

8) Rate and Compass merge their two lending joint ventures under the OriginPoint name: 672 loan officers and a projected $13.2 billion in 2026 volume

So-what: Brokerage-owned lenders are getting bigger and more connected to agents, which raises the bar for independent brokers competing for purchase referrals.

1-minute read

Two of the biggest names in real estate and lending are combining their mortgage joint ventures.

Rate (formerly Guaranteed Rate) and Compass International Holdings will merge Guaranteed Rate Affinity and OriginPoint under the OriginPoint brand, Rate CEO Victor Ciardelli told HousingWire. The combined company will have 672 loan officers and is expected to reach $13.2 billion in mortgage volume by the end of 2026. That makes it a top-10 pure retail lender. Rate will hold the majority stake.

The new venture will be connected to Compass's network of more than 80,000 real estate agents, across brands including Coldwell Banker, Compass, @properties, Sotheby's, and Corcoran. The companies estimate those brands handle over 200,000 buy-side transactions a year.

Rate says capturing 10% to 20% of those deals could add $4.5 billion to $10 billion in volume. Ciardelli said Rate is also keeping all of its servicing now, a book of more than $50 billion, to stay close to customers.

10) FHFA's inspector general tells Congress the agency obstructed its oversight of Fannie and Freddie; FHFA says it met every request

So-what: Oversight of the agencies that back most U.S. mortgages is in dispute, worth watching for any effect on GSE policy and investor confidence.

1-minute read

The internal watchdog for the Federal Housing Finance Agency says the agency got in its way.

FHFA regulates Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and the Federal Home Loan Banks. Its Office of Inspector General (OIG) sent a report to Congress on Friday alleging "unprecedented" obstruction by FHFA, led by Director Bill Pulte. Reuters first reported it.

The OIG says access limits delayed evaluations of FHFA's oversight of Fannie and Freddie's multifamily businesses and a special project on Fannie Mae's loan review and approval process. It says it now has enough access to move forward, but that FHFA's roughly 64% cut to the OIG's fiscal 2027 budget will keep it from finishing all three reports.

FHFA disagrees. A spokesperson told Scotsman Guide the agency has "delivered every document and engaged in every interview" the IG requested, including 56 document requests and 16 hour-long meetings for three recent reviews.

The OIG has had no permanent head since the acting IG was removed in November 2025.

The so-what for lenders: nothing changes in loan guidelines today. But the dispute is part of a bigger story about how Fannie and Freddie are run and overseen, which can matter for pricing and policy.

Expand 3-minute read

This is a government oversight story, so here are the facts, both sides, and why it might matter for mortgages.

Who is involved:

The Federal Housing Finance Agency regulates Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the government-sponsored enterprises (GSEs) that buy or guarantee most conventional mortgages, plus the Federal Home Loan Banks. Bill Pulte is FHFA director and also chairs the boards of Fannie and Freddie, an unusual arrangement. The FHFA Office of Inspector General is an independent watchdog inside the agency.

What the inspector general says:

In a report sent to Congress on Friday, first reported by Reuters and reviewed by Scotsman Guide, the OIG alleges "unprecedented" obstruction. It says FHFA restricted access to information, which delayed three evaluation reports: two on FHFA's oversight of the GSEs' multifamily businesses, and a special project on how effective Fannie Mae's loan review and approval process is. The OIG said it has "recently been given access to information sufficient to allow the evaluations to move forward," but the delays were significant.

The OIG also said FHFA refused records on several occasions, including information about oversight of a multifamily lender that defaulted in 2024.

The budget fight:

FHFA is cutting the OIG's fiscal 2027 budget by about 64%. The OIG says that will require major staff cuts and will keep it from publishing all three evaluations. It said talks with FHFA did not change the decision.

Leadership:

In November 2025, Pulte removed the OIG's acting head, Joe Allen. No replacement has been named. James Hodge, acting principal deputy inspector general, is performing the top job's duties.

FHFA's response:

An FHFA spokesperson told Scotsman Guide the agency has "delivered every document and engaged in every interview the IG has requested under its statutory authorities." The spokesperson said three recent reviews required FHFA to complete 56 document requests and 16 separate hour-long meetings.

Some lawmakers have called for Pulte to resign. We are leaving the political back-and-forth aside.

Why this could matter for the mortgage business:

1. No guideline change today. Nothing in this report changes how loans are underwritten or priced right now.

2. GSE governance is a live issue. Investors who buy mortgage bonds care about how Fannie and Freddie are run, especially with talk in recent years about changes to their status. Questions about oversight can add uncertainty.

3. Loan quality reviews. One delayed review covers Fannie Mae's loan review and approval process. That is the process that decides which lenders and loans meet Fannie's standards. Findings, whenever they come, could lead to tighter or looser oversight of lenders.

4. Multifamily. The multifamily reviews matter less to homebuyers but more to rental housing finance.

Bottom line:

Watch for Congressional follow-up and whether the reports are ever published. For borrowers, there is no action needed. For lenders and brokers, it is background on the agencies that set many of the rules we work under.
