2) Tuesday's 10-year auction was soft: 5.02% yield, a 1 basis point tail and dealers stuck with 10%, the most since May

Wed show

So-what: Weak demand for Treasuries at 5% is a warning sign for mortgage rates. It means investors want higher yields to lend to the government.

1-minute read

The Treasury sold new 10-year notes Tuesday afternoon, right after the CPI selloff. Peter Boockvar called the auction soft.

The yield came in at 5.02%. That was one basis point above where it traded just before the sale. That gap is called a tail, and it means buyers needed a slightly better deal.

The bid to cover, which measures how many bids came in per dollar sold, was 2.46. The one-year average is 2.53. Dealers, who must buy what others do not, took 10% of the auction. That is the most since May.

Treasury yields hit their highs of the day after the auction, though most of the move was already done.

Boockvar also noted that inflation expectations barely moved. The 2-year breakeven rose to just 2.15%, the 5-year to 2.26%, and the 10-year held at 2.22%. His read: bond traders now expect a Fed hike in December and another in January.

For clients: this is about supply and demand for bonds. When demand is weak, rates go up. That pressure does not go away just because the market is closed today.

Expand 3-minute read

The auction is the follow-up to yesterday's CPI story, and it adds a new piece of information.

What happened. The Treasury holds regular auctions to sell new debt. Tuesday's 10-year sale landed a few hours after the hot CPI report. Peter Boockvar wrote that, given the big rate move that day, the auction was soft. The high yield was 5.02%. That was one basis point above the when-issued level, which is where the notes were trading just before the auction. When an auction prices above that level, traders call it a tail. It means the Treasury had to pay a little more than expected to find buyers.

Demand measures. The bid to cover was 2.46. That means there were $2.46 of bids for every $1 of notes sold. The one-year average is 2.53, so demand was below normal. Dealers were left with 10% of the auction. Primary dealers are big banks that are required to bid. When they end up with a large share, it means fewer outside investors stepped in. Boockvar said 10% was the most since May.

Market reaction. Treasury yields moved to the highs of the day after the results. Boockvar said most of the move had already happened before the auction. So the auction did not cause the selloff, but it confirmed buyers were not rushing in at 5%.

The inflation angle. Boockvar pointed out something odd. After CPI, inflation breakevens barely moved. A breakeven is the inflation rate the bond market is pricing in, based on the gap between regular Treasuries and inflation-protected ones. The 2-year breakeven was up 4 basis points to 2.15%. The 5-year was up 1 basis point to 2.26%. The 10-year was unchanged at 2.22%. Boockvar's takeaway is that breakevens are focused on the recent drop in oil prices, while regular Treasuries are reacting to a Fed that, as of now, is definitely going to hike next month, with another hike in January.

Why this matters for mortgages. Mortgage rates track the 10-year Treasury plus a spread. If investors demand more yield to own 10-year Treasuries, mortgage rates feel it. A soft auction at 5% is a sign that the 5% level is not a magic floor for buyers. That lines up with MBS Live listing 5.00% as the next ceiling on the 10-year.

The bigger picture. The government keeps borrowing a lot, and every auction is a test of demand. Weak results tend to show up when there is a surprise like hot inflation. One weak auction is not a crisis. But a pattern of soft auctions would keep upward pressure on long-term rates even if the Fed eventually stops hiking.

How to explain it on the show. Think of an auction like an open house. If the government lists a lot of bonds and fewer buyers show up, it has to cut the price. A lower bond price means a higher yield. Mortgage rates follow that yield. Tuesday's open house was a bit quiet, and that is part of why rate sheets got worse.

This is new weekly data, and it confirms the trend Josh has been talking about on the show.

The Altos numbers. Bill McBride's CalculatedRisk post uses Altos Research data on active single-family inventory. Through Friday, November 6, inventory was up 7.2% compared with the same week in 2025. The prior week it was up 6.8%. More important, inventory was up 1.1% compared with the same week in 2019. The week before, it was only 0.2% above 2019. So inventory has now clearly moved past pre-pandemic levels for this time of year.

Why 2019 matters. For years after the pandemic, the housing market was short on listings. 2019 is the benchmark people use for a normal market. Getting back to and above 2019 levels means the extreme shortage is over in much of the country, though not everywhere.

The Realtor.com view. Realtor.com said the number of homes for sale rose 8.8% from a year ago, the highest weekly growth rate since late January. It said inventory has built momentum all fall, rising from roughly 4% annual growth in late August to nearly 9% now. Its key point is that this is not a flood of new sellers. Instead, slower buyer activity is letting unsold listings accumulate. Homes are sitting longer, so the count goes up.

McBride's conclusion. Inventory has picked up and demand is weak. With 30-year rates around 7.3%, he expects existing home sales to be weak over the coming months, with house prices under pressure. He is not predicting a crash. He is saying the balance has shifted.

How this connects. Last week's briefs covered inventory back to 2019 levels and flat prices. This week's data shows the trend is accelerating, not fading. Combine that with the HousingWire reports of large seller concessions and ARMs at a record share of locks, and you get a clear picture. Sellers who need to sell are paying to get deals done.

What it means for buyers. More listings means more choices and less pressure to waive inspections or bid over asking. It also means more room to negotiate seller credits. A seller credit can pay for a rate buydown, which lowers the monthly payment. In a 7% world, that can be worth more than a small price cut.

What it means for sellers. Pricing right from day one matters more. Homes that sit tend to get price cuts later anyway, and a stale listing gives buyers even more leverage. Sellers who offer a rate buydown credit up front may get more showings than sellers who simply wait for a full-price offer.

Note for California. This is national data. Local markets differ, and some coastal California areas still have tighter supply. But the direction is the same in many places.

This item ties October lock data to what loan officers are seeing on real deals.

The lock data. Optimal Blue's October 2026 Market Advantage report showed rate-lock volume fell 19% month over month and 38% year over year. A rate lock is when a borrower commits to a rate on a specific loan, so it is a good early signal of closings to come. Fewer locks mean fewer closings in the next month or two.

The ARM surge. ARMs were 17% of total lock volume, the highest share in Optimal Blue's report going back to 2019. Brennan O'Connell of Optimal Blue said ARM share reached another high, FHA gained share, and first-time buyers took a larger share across the major agency programs. He said the opportunity for lenders is helping borrowers find the product and payment structure that fits them.

Rates this week. At HousingWire's Mortgage Rates Center on Tuesday, the 30-year fixed averaged 7.35%, down from 7.38% a week earlier. The 30-year jumbo averaged 7.56%, down 6 basis points. The 30-year FHA was up 6 basis points to 7.22%. Note these figures may not fully reflect Tuesday's CPI selloff.

What brokers are seeing. Shannon Hoff, a California broker at Hoff Home Loans, said rates had improved about 100 basis points since early October, which she said can equate to an eighth to 0.375 in rate. She said buyers are still extremely payment-sensitive. But she framed it this way: during the pandemic people paid $100,000 over list. A $100 or $200 change in payment is not so worrisome in the big picture.

The concession playbook. Gay Veale, a 30-year Air Force veteran who owns Guidon Mortgage Group and is licensed in nine states, said many markets are leaning toward buyers. She said about 75% of her clients get seller concessions, and at least $10,000 is an average for her. In one unusual deal, a buyer got $58,000 in concessions and locked a 6% rate. Using a 3-2-1 temporary buydown, a permanent buydown and an ARM, the effective initial rate fell to 2.75%. A 3-2-1 buydown means the rate is 3 points lower in year one, 2 points lower in year two and 1 point lower in year three, paid for upfront.

ARM choices. Veale said most ARM buyers are picking products with about five years of lower rates before they reset. That matches the expectation that rates may ease over the medium term.

Who is buying. Veale said most clients are need-to-move buyers: job moves, a death in the family, a new child, getting closer to relatives. Investor purchases are much less common than in the pandemic.

The economist view. Sam Williamson of First American said hotter inflation likely prolongs the wait for real rate relief. Life moves will keep the market going while discretionary buyers wait.

How to use it. In every buyer consult, show three options: fixed with no help, fixed with a seller-paid buydown, and an ARM. Explain the reset risk on ARMs clearly.

6) Lenders are leaning into ARMs: MBA credit availability rose 0.4% in October, jumbo access hit a three-month high, and WSJ warns of reset shock for older ARMs

Wed show

So-what: ARMs are easier to get and about a point cheaper than fixed. But past ARM borrowers now face resets, so explain the risk clearly.

1-minute read

MBA's Mortgage Credit Availability Index rose 0.4% to 107.5 in October. That reversed declines in August and September. A higher number means looser lending standards.

The conventional piece rose 0.6%. The government piece, FHA and VA, rose just 0.1%. The jumbo piece jumped 0.8% to a three-month high.

Joel Kan said lenders added more ARM options for lower loan-to-value borrowers, including some that allow cash-out refinancing.

Scotsman Guide said 5/1 ARM rates have run about one percentage point below 30-year fixed rates since fixed rates went above 7%. The ARM share of weekly applications has stayed above 10%.

The Wall Street Journal ran a cautionary piece on borrowers whose cheap ARMs are now resetting higher. It opens with a Minneapolis-area buyer who took an ARM in 2019 about 1.25 points below the 4% fixed rate, planning to move before it reset in seven years.

For clients: an ARM can make sense, but only with a plan for the reset. Show the worst-case payment before they sign.

Expand 3-minute read

Two stories this week point in the same direction. ARMs are back in a big way, and borrowers need to understand them.

The credit data. MBA's Mortgage Credit Availability Index, which uses ICE Mortgage Technology data, tracks how easy it is to get a mortgage based on things like credit scores and loan-to-value limits. It rose 0.4% to 107.5 in October, reversing two months of declines. Joel Kan said conventional credit became more available, while the government segment stayed flat.

The pieces. The conventional index, covering Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac loans, rose 0.6%, offsetting a 0.4% drop the month before. Government credit access rose just 0.1%. The jumbo component jumped 0.8% to its highest level in three months.

Why lenders are pushing ARMs. Scotsman Guide reported that since 30-year fixed rates went above 7% at the end of the third quarter, 5/1 ARM rates have consistently been about one percentage point lower. A 5/1 ARM is fixed for five years and then adjusts once a year. Kan said lenders increased ARM offerings for lower loan-to-value borrowers, including some that allow cash-out refinancing. MBA said that as of the first week of November, typical 30-year rates had eased about 20 basis points below their 7.4% average in the first two weeks of October, but remain well above the roughly 6.3% of a year ago.

This matches the Optimal Blue data showing ARMs at 17% of October locks, a record for that report since 2019.

The warning. The Wall Street Journal's piece, Home Buyers Who Enjoyed Cheap Adjustable Mortgages Are Now Getting a Nasty Shock, looks at borrowers whose ARMs are resetting into today's higher rates. The opening example is Andrew Hecker, who took out an ARM in 2019. His lender offered an initial rate about 1.25 points below the 4% rate on a traditional 30-year at the time. He figured he would move from his Minneapolis-area home before the rate reset in seven years. We only read the opening of the article because of the paywall, so we do not have its full numbers.

The lesson. The plan to move or refinance before the reset is the most common ARM plan. It does not always work out. Life changes, rates rise, or home values stall.

How to talk about ARMs. One: show the start rate, the cap structure and the highest possible payment. Two: ask the borrower how long they really expect to keep the loan. Three: make sure they could afford the payment after the first adjustment, even if rates do not fall. Four: compare against a fixed rate with a seller-paid buydown. Sometimes that gets most of the savings with none of the reset risk. Five: for jumbo borrowers, note that jumbo credit access just improved, so it is worth shopping.

Show angle. This is a good Wednesday segment: ARMs are popular again, here is when they make sense, and here is when they hurt.

This is the New York Fed's quarterly check on how much Americans owe and how well they are paying.

The headline. The NY Fed's Center for Microeconomic Data said total household debt expanded by $334 billion, a 1.8% increase, in Q3 2026, reaching $19.1 trillion. That includes mortgages, home equity lines, auto loans, credit cards and student loans.

Mortgage originations. The NY Fed measures new mortgages as they appear on credit reports. It said originations were notably up, with $595 billion newly originated in Q3. That includes purchase and refinance loans.

Credit scores. The NY Fed said the credit quality of new mortgages was slightly worse. The 25th percentile credit score of new loans fell by 6 points, and the 10th percentile fell by 3 points. In plain terms, the weaker borrowers getting approved are a bit weaker than before. That fits with more FHA lending and more first-time buyers.

Still strict by history. Bill McBride pointed out that recently there have been almost no originations for borrowers below 620, and few below 660. A significant majority of new loans went to borrowers above 760. Compared with the 2003 to 2006 bubble years, lending today is much more careful. McBride has argued that solid underwriting is a key reason today's market should not be compared to the bubble and bust.

Delinquency trends. McBride flagged that the rate of current mortgages becoming 30 to 60 days late had been rising since mortgage rates increased, but has mostly flattened out and is below pre-pandemic levels. Most short-term late payments go back to current. However, the transition to serious delinquency, 90 days or more late, increased in Q3 and has been increasing over the last several years.

Foreclosures. Foreclosures increased in Q3 but are still below pre-pandemic levels. McBride also showed the share of mortgage debt 90 or more days late by state. During the bust, Nevada, California, Florida and Arizona stood out. Now, he said, no state really stands out.

Connecting the dots. On Monday, ICE reported the national delinquency rate at 3.66% in September, with 90-day delinquencies up 21% from a year ago. Earlier this month, FHA loans were reported as 47% of seriously delinquent balances. Together, these point to stress at the lower end, especially among FHA borrowers, while the overall market stays healthy.

Why brokers should care. Lenders and investors watch these numbers. If serious delinquencies keep rising, expect overlays, which are extra rules lenders add on top of agency guidelines, on lower-score FHA and high-debt files. Borrowers near the cutoffs should clean up credit early.

Client tip. A borrower at 640 who can get to 680 or 700 often gets better pricing and more lender options. Start that work months ahead.

The results. Better reported a Q3 2026 net loss of $36.5 million. That is larger than the $30.6 million loss in Q2, but slightly better than the $39.1 million loss in Q3 2025. HousingWire noted the 10-year Treasury yield rose about 15% during the quarter, which hurt demand.

A Chamber-commissioned analysis projected $1.5 billion in savings and added revenue over 10 years. Supporters said it could cut delays by two to 10 years.

What already exists. Gov. Newsom signed AB 130 and SB 131 in 2025. They exempted qualifying infill housing from CEQA and narrowed review for some other projects. What a securitization is. A lender pools many loans and sells bonds backed by them to investors. That frees up cash to make more loans. When investors keep buying these bonds, lenders can keep offering products like fix-and-flip, construction and DSCR loans, which are business-purpose loans for investors rather than owner-occupants.

The deal. HousingWire reported that Asset Based Lending closed a $200 million securitization, its largest so far. It is backed by business-purpose residential real estate loans with a large share of ground-up construction. The deal, ABL 2026 RTL-1, priced Oct. 15 and closed Oct. 26.
