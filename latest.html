1) Fed decision today at 11 AM PT: about 90% odds of a hold, and the vote count is the real tell

Wed show

So-what: A pause is priced in. Rates move on the tone and the dissents, so plan lock talks around 11 AM to noon PT.

1-minute read

The Fed announces its decision today at 11 AM PT. Chair Kevin Warsh holds his press conference at 11:30 AM PT, about 30 minutes before our show.

The market is close to certain on a hold. CME FedWatch showed a 90.6% chance of no change early this morning, up from 35.8% a month ago. A Reuters poll had 94 of 100 economists expecting a hold. The Fed's target range is 3.75% to 4.00% after September's hike.

December is a different story. FedWatch puts a December hike near 77%, and a Reuters factbox had it at 86%. Markets price roughly 0.71 percentage point of more hikes through September 2027, per Chrisman.

What to watch is the vote. Investing.com framed it simply: zero dissents reads neutral, one reads mildly hawkish, two or more is a truly hawkish hold. Hammack, Logan and Kashkari each voted against a hold in July.

So what: A plain hold with few dissents could let rates drift lower. Several hawkish dissents or firm December talk could push them up. Thursday's PCE inflation and GDP reports at 5:30 AM PT come right after.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is the setup in plain English. The Fed raised its target range to 3.75% to 4.00% in September, its first hike in three years. Today it decides whether to do it again. Almost nobody expects that. CME FedWatch showed a 90.6% chance of a hold early Wednesday, per CNBC. HousingWire noted that number was 84.5% a week ago and only 35.8% a month ago. Kalshi traders had a hold at 93%. A Reuters poll had 94 of 100 economists calling for no change.

Why not hike now? Inflation is still too high. September CPI was 3.7%, per ABC. But the job market has slowed hard. September added just 29,000 jobs, versus 162,000 in August. That mix gives the Fed reason to wait and watch. Rob Chrisman put it well: the Fed gets two more CPI and jobs reports before its December 9 meeting, so Warsh has little reason to commit today.

The December question. A hike is still widely expected. FedWatch put December near 77% this morning. A Reuters factbox had 86%, and noted most big banks, including Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan, expect a December move to 4.00% to 4.25%. Bank of America is the outlier. It expected a hike today and another in December. Chrisman wrote that markets price about 71 basis points (0.71 percentage point) of additional hikes through September 2027.

How to read the decision. There is no new dot plot or projections at this meeting, so the statement, the vote and the press conference carry everything. Investing.com laid out a simple scorecard. Zero dissents is a neutral hold. One dissent is mildly hawkish. Two or more is a truly hawkish hold. Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Dallas's Lorie Logan and Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari each voted against a hold in July, per CNBC. Chrisman said the market expects three dissents, so fewer could read as friendly to rates and more as a warning.

Expect a short show from Warsh. Peter Boockvar described his "personal choice of relative silence" and wrote, "brief will be the statement and brief will be the press conference." A Bank of America memo summed up the trap: "If the Fed hikes, investors might worry about overtightening. If the Fed holds, markets could question the Fed's inflation-fighting credibility."

What MBS Live says. Matt Graham noted the pause is almost fully priced, so any move depends on wording or the press conference. His line: "post-press-conference volatility should never be ruled out."

What comes next. Thursday at 5:30 AM PT brings September PCE inflation, personal income and spending, and third-quarter GDP. Those numbers could matter more for mortgage rates than today's decision.

So what for clients: The decision itself is not the risk. The risk is a hawkish surprise in the vote or the tone. For anyone near closing, lock before 11 AM PT if a small rate rise would break the deal. For buyers still shopping, the message is that the Fed controls short-term rates, while mortgage rates follow the 10-year Treasury and inflation data. A hold today does not mean mortgage rates drop tomorrow.

3) Mortgage applications rise 3.4%, the first gain since August; ARM share hits 11.3%

Purchase applications rose 4% from the prior week but are still 18% lower than a year ago. Refinance applications rose 3% and are 61% below last year. Refis made up 38.9% of all applications.

The trigger was rates. MBA's 30-year conforming rate fell to 7.37% from 7.41%, the lowest in three weeks. Points averaged 0.76.

The detail to watch is the ARM share. Adjustable-rate loans were 11.3% of applications, the highest in more than a year. MBA's 5/1 ARM averaged 6.41%, almost a full point under the 30-year fixed.

MBA's Joel Kan said many homebuyers have put their purchase plans on hold. Peter Boockvar noted refis rose after nine straight weekly declines.

So what: One good week is not a trend, but it shows buyers are rate-sensitive near 7.4%. Every small dip brings some back. Expect more ARM questions, and make sure clients understand the reset terms.

Expand 3-minute read

The headline. MBA's weekly survey for the week ending October 23 showed total applications up 3.4% on a seasonally adjusted basis. The prior week was down 5.3%. This was the first gain since August and broke a seven-week losing streak.

Purchase. The purchase index rose 4% from the prior week. Compared with the same week last year, unadjusted purchase activity was down 18%. Bill McBride at CalculatedRisk flagged that year-over-year drop as the key number. Boockvar noted purchase apps rose after seven straight declines.

Refinance. The refi index rose 3% but is 61% below a year ago. The refi share of applications was 38.9%, from 39.0%. With most existing borrowers holding much lower rates, refis at these levels come mainly from people who borrowed in the last couple of years or need cash out.

Rates in the survey. The 30-year conforming rate fell to 7.37% from 7.41%, with points at 0.76 including the origination fee. Jumbo fell to 7.28% from 7.33%. FHA eased to 7.12% from 7.13%. The 15-year fell to 6.70% from 6.73%. The 5/1 ARM fell to 6.41% from 6.46%.

Loan mix. FHA share rose to 16.6% from 16.3%. VA share slipped to 11.0% from 11.5%. USDA was 0.5%. The ARM share rose to 11.3%, which Kan called the highest level in more than a year.

What Joel Kan said. He tied the gain to lower rates and also said that mortgage rates are still more than a percentage point higher than the same week last year. He added that many homebuyers have put their purchase plans on hold. CNBC's Diana Olick noted that about half of existing borrowers have rates below 4%, which keeps many owners from listing.

Why the ARM number matters. An ARM at 6.41% versus a fixed rate at 7.37% saves real money on day one. On a $500,000 loan that is roughly $320 a month in principal and interest. That is why ARM demand rises when fixed rates are high. But the savings come with reset risk. Today's WSJ leads with a story about borrowers who took cheap ARMs years ago now facing payment shocks. The next item covers that math.

For the show. This is a good, simple talking point: when rates dip even a little, buyers come back. That tells us demand is waiting on the sidelines. It also shows how much the payment, not the price, is driving decisions right now.

So what for clients: Treat this as a sign that buyers are price-sensitive, not that the market has turned. If a client asks about an ARM, run both payments side by side and show the worst-case reset under the caps. Make the decision on whether they can afford the reset, not on whether rates might fall. A buyer who can only qualify with the ARM start rate needs a hard conversation before going further.

4) ARM resets: about 150,000 borrowers face their first adjustment this year, some up $1,000+ a month

Wed show

So-what: As ARM demand climbs again, every ARM conversation needs a reset-payment test, not just a start-rate quote.

1-minute read

Today's Wall Street Journal headline puts it bluntly: home buyers who enjoyed cheap adjustable mortgages are now getting a nasty shock. The data behind that story has been building.

ICE Mortgage Technology data shared with CNBC shows about 150,000 ARM borrowers face their first rate adjustment this year. Another 186,000 hit their first reset in 2027 and 155,000 in 2028.

The worst cases are big. Seven-year ARMs taken out in 2019 had a median start rate of 3% and a median balance of $548,000. ICE projects their median adjusted rate at 6.06%, which raises the payment by a median $1,080 a month.

Not everyone gets hurt. Three-year ARMs from 2023 started near 8.38%, and those borrowers could see payments drop by a median $124.

ARMs are still a small slice of the market: 3.1 million loans, or 5.6% of about 55.4 million active mortgages, per ICE.

So what: ARM share in new applications just hit 11.3%. That is fine when the borrower understands the reset. Show the capped worst-case payment upfront, and remind past ARM clients to plan seven to eight months ahead of their first change.

Expand 3-minute read

Why this is back in the news. The Wall Street Journal ran a story this morning headlined "Home Buyers Who Enjoyed Cheap Adjustable Mortgages Are Now Getting a Nasty Shock." Its summary said payments could rise sharply for tens of thousands of ARM borrowers who financed at historically low rates. We could not open the full article, so the numbers here come from ICE data published by CNBC on October 13.

The size of the issue. ICE counts about 3.1 million ARMs outstanding, the most in five and a half years. That is 5.6% of roughly 55.4 million active mortgages. About 2.05 million are still in their fixed start period, and 1.05 million have moved into the adjustable phase.

Who resets when. About 150,000 borrowers face their first adjustment in 2026, including about 64,000 scheduled from August through December. Another 186,000 reset for the first time in 2027, and 155,000 in 2028.

How big the shock can be. The clearest example is the seven-year ARM taken out in 2019. Median start rate: 3%. Median balance: $548,000. ICE projects a median adjusted rate of 6.06% given current rates and caps. That adds a median $1,080 to the monthly payment. That is a jumbo-sized household budget problem.

The other side. Three-year ARMs from 2023 started at a median 8.38% with a balance near $136,000. Those rates are set to fall to a median 7.71%, cutting payments by about $124. So not every reset is bad news. It depends on when the loan was made.

How caps work. A common setup is a 2 percentage point limit on the first change, 1 point on each later change, and 5 points over the life of the loan. Caps matter more than the start rate when you are testing risk.

Rules of the road. The CFPB requires lenders to notify borrowers 210 to 240 days, about seven to eight months, before the first adjusted payment. A second notice comes 60 to 120 days before, with the actual new rate and payment.

Options for borrowers. Keep the ARM, refinance into a fixed loan, or sell. Refinancing means new closing costs, typically 2% to 5% of the loan amount according to Fannie Mae. On a $300,000 loan that is $6,000 to $15,000. Urban Institute's Laurie Goodman summed it up: borrowers took a lower rate "in exchange for the possibility that rates could rise," and "unfortunately that scenario happened."

The tie to today. MBA says ARMs were 11.3% of applications last week, the highest in more than a year, and the 5/1 ARM averaged 6.41%. Buyers are reaching for the lower start payment again.

So what for clients: For new ARM borrowers, qualify them on the payment they can carry after the first cap, not just the start rate. For past clients with ARMs from 2019 to 2021, this is a strong reason to reach out now. A friendly review call before their notice arrives builds trust and could lead to a refinance or a purchase conversation.

5) Inventory up, demand down: Realtor.com listings +7.7% y/y as purchase apps run 18% below last year

Wed show

So-what: More homes and fewer buyers give today's buyers more leverage on price, credits and inspections.

1-minute read

Bill McBride at CalculatedRisk summed up the weekly housing data in one line: "Inventory is up and demand is down, and that will put pressure on house prices."

The supply side is growing. Altos data shows active single-family inventory up 5.7% from a year ago, up from 5.3% the week before. Realtor.com's weekly inventory is up 7.7% year over year. That is its biggest weekly gain since late January, and up from about 4% in late August.

Inventory is now close to normal. Altos puts it just 1.8% below the same week in 2019, from 3.2% below a week earlier.

Demand is soft. MBA purchase applications are 18% lower than a year ago, even after last week's 4% bump. MBA's Joel Kan said many buyers have put plans on hold.

So what: For buyers who can qualify, this is the best negotiating setup in years. More choices and fewer competing offers mean room to ask for seller credits, often used to buy down the rate. For sellers, pricing right on day one matters more than it has in a long time.

Expand 3-minute read

What the data says. CalculatedRisk tracks several weekly measures. Two stand out today.

Supply. Altos active single-family inventory was up 5.7% from a year ago, up from a 5.3% gain the prior week. Realtor.com's weekly inventory measure was up 7.7% year over year. McBride said that was its biggest weekly gain since late January. In late August that same measure was running about 4%. So inventory growth is speeding up, not slowing down.

How close to normal. Altos shows inventory now just 1.8% below the same week in 2019, from 3.2% below the week before. 2019 is a useful benchmark because it was the last fairly normal market before the pandemic. If inventory passes 2019 levels, buyers will have more choice than they have had in about seven years.

Demand. MBA purchase applications rose 4% last week but remain 18% below a year ago. Kan said many homebuyers have put their purchase plans on hold. CNBC also cited agents who say some buyers are waiting for the midterm elections.

McBride's takeaway. "Inventory is up and demand is down, and that will put pressure on house prices." He is careful and data-driven, so that line carries weight. It is not a crash call. It is a statement that the balance of power is moving toward buyers.

What it means for prices. National price growth has already slowed to low single digits, as Tuesday's Case-Shiller and FHFA reports showed. More inventory and fewer buyers usually show up first as longer days on market, more price cuts and more seller concessions, before showing up in the price indexes months later.

The California angle. Inventory growth is uneven by region, and many coastal California markets still have less supply than the Sun Belt. But the direction matters. Orange County pending sales hit their lowest October level since 2007, which we covered yesterday. Fewer pending deals mean more listings sitting.

For the show. This is a strong segment for Emily and Jake, our first-time and move-up buyers. The message: you are not competing with ten other offers anymore in most markets. You can negotiate. Seller credits can pay for a rate buydown, which lowers the payment more than a small price cut often does.

How to use it with clients. When a buyer finds a home that has been on the market a while, compare two offers. One asks for a lower price. One asks for a seller credit toward a permanent or temporary buydown. Show both payments. Many sellers prefer the credit because it keeps their sale price higher.

So what for clients: Buyers have leverage they did not have a year ago, and it is growing. The catch is still the rate. Pair the inventory story with a clear payment plan, and help clients act when the right house and the right concession line up, rather than waiting for a perfect rate.

6) ICE: September delinquencies 3.66%, still below pre-pandemic norms, but foreclosure inventory up 37%

So-what: Credit is mostly healthy. Stress is concentrated in recent loans, which matters for FHA and first-time buyer pricing over time.

1-minute read

ICE's early read on September mortgage performance is calm on the surface with some cracks underneath.

The national delinquency rate rose 12 basis points to 3.66%. That is up 7% from a year ago but still 34 basis points below September 2019 and below every pre-pandemic September on record.

Serious delinquencies, loans 90 or more days late, are up 21% from a year ago but down 5% from February's peak. Their share is 1.04%, about the same as the 2017-2019 September average.

Foreclosures are the area to watch. Starts were about 40,000, up 8% from August and down 3% from a year ago. Foreclosure sales rose 15% year over year. Foreclosure inventory is up 37%, its highest since early 2019.

Recent loans drive much of it. Loans made in 2022 or later were 43% of foreclosure starts and 49% of sales.

Prepayments fell to 0.59% a month, a 19-month low.

So what: This is not a credit crisis. It is a normalizing market with pockets of stress among recent buyers. Slower prepays also matter: they make mortgage bonds a bit more attractive to investors.

Expand 3-minute read

The big picture. ICE's First Look covers loan performance nationwide. September's report shows a market that is getting back to normal after years of unusually low defaults.

Delinquency. The national rate was 3.66%, up from 3.50% in August and up 7.02% from a year ago. ICE stresses context: it is 34 basis points below September 2019 and below every September before the pandemic. About 2.03 million loans were 30 or more days past due, and about 579,000 were 90 or more days late.

Early versus serious. Thirty-day delinquencies were up 5% from a year ago. Serious delinquencies were up 21% from a year ago but down 5% from their February peak. The serious delinquency share was 1.04%, roughly in line with the 2017-2019 September average of 1.06%. Serious FHA delinquencies are down 10% since February.

Foreclosure. Starts were about 40,000, up 8.2% month over month and down 3% year over year. Sales, meaning completed foreclosures, were about 8,200, up 15% from last year. Foreclosure inventory rose 37% from a year ago to a 0.55% rate, the highest since early 2019. That sounds alarming, but 2019 was a normal market. We are returning to normal from historic lows.

Who is struggling. Loans made in 2022 or later account for 43% of foreclosure starts and 49% of foreclosure sales. These are borrowers who bought at high prices with higher rates and have little equity cushion. Many are FHA borrowers. Fannie Mae's own report this week showed its single-family serious delinquency rate rising to 0.62% from 0.58%.

Prepayments. The single-month mortality rate, which measures how fast loans pay off, was 0.59%. That is a 19-month low, down from 1.06% in March and 20% lower than a year ago. Fewer people are refinancing or selling.

Why prepays matter for rates. Mortgage bond investors like slow, predictable prepayments. When prepays slow, bonds hold their value longer. Chrisman noted agency MBS posted their strongest weekly excess return since early August last week, and he called conventional and Ginnie Mae 30-year securities attractive on a spread basis. Stronger investor demand can help mortgage rates over time.

Why defaults matter for borrowers. Rising delinquencies among recent buyers can lead investors and insurers to price risk higher. That is most likely to show up in FHA, high loan-to-value and lower credit score pricing. It also gives the Fed and regulators a reason to watch affordability closely.

So what for clients: Most homeowners are fine. The stress sits with recent buyers who stretched. That is a lesson for today's buyers: build in reserves, do not max out the payment, and know your options if life changes. For past clients from 2022 and later, a check-in call offering help with payment options or an equity review is both good service and good business.

7) Fannie Mae earns $4.2B in Q3; net worth reaches $119.6B as credit-loss reserves more than double

So-what: The GSEs are steady and still adding capital. Higher rates slow payoffs, which helps them but signals a slower refi market for us.

1-minute read

Fannie Mae reported third-quarter net income of $4.2 billion, up 5% from the second quarter and up from $3.9 billion a year ago. Net revenue was $7.7 billion.

Its net worth rose to $119.6 billion, up 13% from a year ago. FHFA Director Bill Pulte, who chairs Fannie's board, credited a focus on "running an efficient business and reducing mortgage costs."

There were warning signs in the details. The provision for credit losses was $1.1 billion, more than double the $485 million in the second quarter. Fannie cited higher rates and newly delinquent loans. Its single-family serious delinquency rate rose to 0.62% from 0.58%.

Volume fell. Single-family acquisitions dropped to $88.3 billion from $111.2 billion. First-time buyers were 53% of purchase loans, about 103,000 households.

Fannie also expanded VantageScore 4.0 to all approved lenders this quarter. Freddie Mac reports Thursday.

So what: The GSEs are healthy, and that supports steady agency pricing. The CFO's comment that higher rates slow prepayments is a reminder that refi volume will stay thin while rates sit above 7%.

Expand 3-minute read

The headline numbers. Fannie Mae earned $4.2 billion in the third quarter, up 5% from $4.0 billion in the second quarter and up from $3.9 billion a year earlier, according to HousingWire. Net revenue rose 1% to $7.7 billion. Net worth reached $119.6 billion on September 30, up 3% from June and 13% from a year ago.

What drove it. Higher net interest income and fair value gains offset a jump in reserves for credit losses. That provision was $1.1 billion, more than double the $485 million in the second quarter. Fannie said the single-family provision reflected higher actual and projected interest rates, newly acquired loans and newly delinquent loans.

Credit trends. The single-family serious delinquency rate rose to 0.62% from 0.58% at the end of June. The book still looks strong on paper. The average mark-to-market loan-to-value was 51%, and the average FICO score at origination was 753. Multifamily serious delinquencies rose to 0.62% from 0.60%.

Volume. Single-family acquisitions fell to $88.3 billion from $111.2 billion in the second quarter. Fannie supported about 194,000 home purchases and 66,000 refinances in the quarter. First-time buyers made up 53% of single-family purchase acquisitions, about 103,000 households. So far in 2026, Fannie says it has helped about 1.2 million households, including more than 295,000 first-time buyers.

What the CFO said. Chryssa Halley explained the rate effect. Higher rates reduce new loan volume, but they also slow prepayments, which keeps guaranty fees coming in longer. The flip side: slower payoffs extend the expected life of loans and, with higher costs for some borrowers, raise expected credit losses.

Credit scores. Acting CEO Peter Akwaboah said Fannie expanded VantageScore 4.0 as an additional credit score to all Fannie-approved lenders this quarter, and added multiple credit scores to its MBS disclosures. That ties into the ongoing credit score cost fight, including the FICO pricing news we covered this week.

The Fed's role. A related number from Chrisman today: the Fed still holds about $1.89 trillion in mortgage bonds, down 30% from its $2.7 trillion peak in March 2022. At the current pace, runoff could take nearly 11 years. Sales are not expected, and a return to buying looks unlikely. That means private investors must keep absorbing new supply, which is one reason mortgage spreads stay wide.

Freddie Mac reports its third-quarter results Thursday.

So what for clients: Fannie's results mean the agency market is stable and well funded. Nothing here changes how loans get done this week. The bigger takeaway is volume. Agency purchase and refi activity is down from spring, and Fannie expects higher rates to keep slowing payoffs. For brokers, that points to the same playbook: purchase business, first-time buyers, and specialty products where the agencies are not the only option.

8) California court: cities can owe damages for blowing the 60-day ADU permit deadline

Wed show

So-what: California homeowners adding an ADU just gained real leverage against slow cities, which supports ADU and renovation financing.

1-minute read

A California appeals court just put a price on permit delays. On October 23, the Second Appellate District ruled that a Palos Verdes Estates homeowner can sue the city for damages over a roughly three-year delay in approving his accessory dwelling unit, or ADU.

State law already required cities to approve qualifying ADUs within 60 days when a home already stands on the lot. What was missing was a penalty. The court said a 1963 state law makes agencies liable when they break a mandatory duty, and it said "permit seekers are the direct and intended beneficiaries."

The homeowner, Wei Shi, applied in 2020 and got approval in 2023. He estimates his losses at more than $1 million. The case now goes back to the trial court, and no damages have been awarded yet.

UC Davis law professor Chris Elmendorf called it "a huge decision." He said the logic could reach other state housing laws that require ministerial approval, such as SB 35, SB 423 and AB 2011.

So what: For California clients thinking about an ADU, this is a strong talking point. Cities now have a financial reason to stop stalling. That helps renovation, cash-out and HELOC conversations tied to ADU projects.

Expand 3-minute read

What happened. Wei Shi applied in 2020 for a permit to build an ADU alongside his family home in Palos Verdes Estates. He says the city demanded a conditional use permit, discretionary review and sign-off from the local homes association's art jury. He also alleges a planning commissioner lobbied neighbors against the project. The city approved the permit in 2023, about three years after he applied.

He sued in October 2024 for the cost of the wait, which he estimates at more than $1 million. That number includes lost use of the property, higher construction costs, interest, design fees and reduced value. A Los Angeles County judge dismissed the case. On October 23, the Second Appellate District reversed that decision.

The legal logic. California's ADU laws require ministerial approval, meaning cities must approve compliant projects without discretionary review. The law requires a decision within 60 days of a complete application when a home already exists on the lot. But the ADU statutes named no penalty. The court said that gap does not matter. A section of the Government Claims Act, a 1963 law, makes agencies liable when they breach a mandatory duty meant to prevent a particular injury. The city argued the ADU laws were meant to help the public, not individual homeowners. The court disagreed and wrote, "Permit seekers are the direct and intended beneficiaries of the relevant duty."

What it does not do. The ruling keeps the lawsuit alive. It does not decide it. Shi still has to prove the city caused his losses. No court has awarded damages, and the appeals court only ordered the city to pay his appeal costs.

Why it could be big. Elmendorf wrote, "This is a huge decision because numerous other housing laws create similar ministerial duties to issue permits." He listed SB 35, SB 423, AB 2011, SB 684 and SB 1123. Those laws were passed over the past nine years to speed housing approval. He said the prospect of money damages "has massive deterrent effects." State Sen. Scott Wiener wrote, "When we pass housing laws allowing people to build new homes, we mean it." California YIMBY's Matt Lewis put it bluntly: "If it's compliant, you got to approve it, and if you don't, you're going to pay a penalty for that."

Why it matters for lending. ADUs are one of the few ways California owners can add value and income without moving. Many owners have low first mortgage rates they do not want to give up. That makes HELOCs, closed-end seconds and renovation loans the main ways to fund an ADU. Delays raise costs, and higher costs kill projects. A real penalty for delay makes timelines more predictable, which makes the math easier.

For the show. This is a California-specific story, but it connects to a bigger theme for buyers everywhere: a home with room for an ADU can mean rental income or space for family later. Remind viewers that the team can help with financing in every state we serve, while local permit rules vary.

So what for clients: For California homeowners with an ADU idea, this ruling is a reason to start the planning conversation. Pair it with a second-lien or renovation option that leaves the low first mortgage in place.
