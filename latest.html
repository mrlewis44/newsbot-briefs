3) WSJ: DSCR investor loans revived abandoned blocks, then fraud and defaults forced a private-lending pullback

Wed show

So-what: Non-QM rent-based loans still fund investors, but overlays and fraud controls tightened after Baltimore-style blowups. Know which desks still say yes.

1-minute read

WSJ highlights a mortgage that underwrites to rent and property value, not the borrower's W-2: the DSCR loan.

Debt service coverage ratio loans let investors qualify on expected rental cash flow. That product helped finance rehabs in long-abandoned neighborhoods, including large pockets of Baltimore. Banner reporting shows how DSCR dollars scaled fast there, then hit a wall when a New York-linked portfolio of hundreds of homes slid into foreclosure and bankruptcy stress.

Wall Street lenders pulled back. Private lending in Baltimore froze for a stretch. Honest local investors who wanted to buy and rent heard brokers say the city was off limits. MBA's Pete Mills framed the core problem as real estate fraud and bad actors, not the loan product itself, while still acknowledging tenants and lenders got hurt.

For Josh's desk: DSCR and other non-QM investor loans remain in the toolkit, but overlays, geography, and fraud controls matter more after this cycle. If a Realtor asks why investor financing tightened, this is why.

Expand 3-minute read

DSCR stands for debt service coverage ratio. Instead of qualifying on personal income the way a primary-home QM loan does, the lender looks at whether the property's expected rent covers the mortgage payment by a set ratio, plus value and reserves.

That structure opened capital for single-family rentals and light rehabs where a traditional bank loan was slow or unavailable. In Baltimore, Banner reporting found DSCR-backed financing grew from roughly 10% of single-family rental loan dollars in 2019 to about 40%, more than $150 million, in a recent year. Investors used it to buy vacant and distressed stock that city leaders wanted back in productive use. For a while, that looked like a rare private-market assist to vacant-housing goals.

Then the credit story flipped. Court and property records tied a large portfolio, at least 704 homes in Banner's count, to a small set of New York buyers who leaned hard on DSCR debt. Numerous lenders collectively lent more than $100 million into that cluster. Many loans were sold into private equity and insurer funds. When foreclosures and bankruptcies hit, several Wall Street-backed financiers blacklisted the buyers and paused Baltimore lending. Local landlords like Dontae Carroll described brokers telling them the city was off limits for months even for ordinary mom-and-pop deals.

MBA senior vice president Pete Mills called it a sophisticated scheme involving appraisers and title practices that kept lenders in the dark. He stressed it was real estate fraud rather than a blanket indictment of DSCR, and said lenders brought issues to regulators and law enforcement when they surfaced. City officials pointed at bad actors and possible appraisal-fraud reviews. Johns Hopkins finance professor Vadim Elenev said the product is not obviously flawed if underwriting is done well.

WSJ's broader framing is revival plus pullback: the same tool that reopened abandoned blocks can amplify losses when fraud, thin renovations, or weak cash flow show up. Industry chatter also points to private lending volumes down sharply year over year in some channels as risk appetite resets. Sources.md flagged private lending about 40% lower year over year in the WSJ frame.

So-what for BuyWise: know your investor overlays. Ask which geographies a DSCR desk will still fund. Document rent support carefully. Watch appraisal quality on investor files. And when Realtors pitch easy investor money, explain that post-Baltimore liquidity is pickier even if the product still exists. Non-QM is open, not wild west.

For California purchase brokers, the DSCR story is less about Baltimore geography and more about credit culture. When private credit tightens after fraud headlines, overlays rise everywhere, not only in the city that made the news. Expect more questions on lease quality, vacancy assumptions, borrower experience, and cash reserves. Expect some desks to exit thin MSAs entirely for a quarter or two.

Also separate DSCR from hard-money bridge product in client conversations. DSCR is still a longer-term rental underwrite. Bridge is a short clock. After a pullback, confusing the two is how investors get the wrong quote and blame the broker. Keep a short list of investor desks that are still clearing files, and update it weekly while private credit resets.

4) UAD 3.6 dual track to May 2027: appraisals jump ~$550 to ~$850 and add 3–5 days, Farrell warns

Wed show

So-what: Wrong lender choice means wrong fees and blown locks. Confirm 2.6 vs 3.6 before you quote, and over-disclose until the order is real.

1-minute read

NAMB president Michael Farrell told Scotsman Guide the Uniform Appraisal Dataset 3.6 rollout is chaos for brokers and borrowers.

Fannie and Freddie pushed mandatory UAD 3.6 to May 2027, but only for lenders with waivers. That creates a dual track. Some large lenders already deliver 3.6 files. Others still take older UAD 2.6 until the last minute. Brokers who pick the wrong lender can quote the wrong fee and miss dates.

Farrell's price gap: older appraisals around $550 versus modernized reports closer to $850. Expect three to five extra days on turn times. Appraiser capacity for 3.6 is still thin, so demand is bidding prices up. Delays cost consumers money on locks and extensions.

Best practice from Farrell: over-disclose fees until the actual order lands, confirm which appraisal form your lender requires, and build lock buffers for the longer clock. Closing-cost surprises are a show topic and a referral killer.

Expand 3-minute read

Appraisal modernization is no longer a back-office IT story. It is hitting Loan Estimates and lock desks now.

Uniform Appraisal Dataset 3.6 was headed for a Nov 2 mandatory start until Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac granted a limited extension in late September. Mandatory implementation moved to May 2027 for lenders that receive waivers. Everyone else is in a messy middle. Some large lenders have been selling loans with 3.6 appraisals since the GSEs began limited acceptance last November. Many other lenders delayed. Software vendors and field appraisers also lagged, which is how you get a capacity crunch right when volumes need certainty.

NAMB president Michael Farrell, speaking with Scotsman Guide's Ryan Kingsley, said all roads lead to chaos. From an originator standpoint, the damage is delay, and delays cost the consumer money. Because a dual track exists through next spring, lenders that refuse 2.6 files risk losing broker volume to lenders that still accept the older form. Brokers will route to whoever closes cleaner for the borrower. That is rational, and it will reshape wholesale scorecards for a while.

Cost and timing are the client-facing hits. Farrell puts older reports near $550 and modernized 3.6 reports near $850. The new form needs more training and more time on site. On the loan side, plan for three to five extra days in processing and delivery. That matters when a rate lock is burning and a purchase contract is tight. A $300 appraisal surprise plus an extension fee is how a first-time buyer loses trust in the process.

Farrell's broker checklist is practical. Be cautious which lender you use for each file. Confirm which appraisal framework that lender will accept before you quote fees. Over-disclose on closing cost estimates until the actual order confirms the price. Set expectations with borrowers and Realtor partners that turn times may stretch. Build lock strategy around the slower clock, not the old 2.6 assumption.

Farrell sits on The Appraisal Foundation advisory council and argues 3.6 should eventually help Fannie and Freddie with richer data, including more hybrid and waiver paths. That long-run upside does not erase today's fee and delay risk for Emily and Jake writing a check at closing. For the Wednesday show, this is a clean closing-cost card next to the rates card.

Operationally, build a lender matrix this week. Column one is lender name. Column two is whether they still take UAD 2.6. Column three is typical appraisal fee quotes for both forms. Column four is median turn time. Share that matrix with processors so Loan Estimates stop oscillating by accident. If a borrower already paid for a 2.6 report and the chosen lender now demands 3.6, escalate early rather than discovering it at underwriting conditions.

Also talk to your top Realtor partners before they hear it from a listing agent. A three-to-five-day appraisal stretch changes inspection-to-close calendars. Better to reset expectations now than to burn goodwill on a delayed COE after CPI already made payments worse.

8) Waller: AI inflation impulse may last 2–3 years; Treasury convenience yield is 'gone'

Wed show

So-what: Long yields have structural bid from AI, fiscal, and lost safe-haven premium. Do not hang a purchase file on a quick dive in the 10-year.

1-minute read

Fed Governor Christopher Waller told Bloomberg in New Delhi that AI-driven demand inflation could last two to three years.

Hyperscalers still plan to spend trillions over the next few years, he said, so demand pressure on workers, materials, components, and land may persist. Productivity gains that could later cool inflation are still a few years out. Waller still expects additional hikes to help return inflation to 2%, with flexibility on timing.

He also said Treasurys' old safety and liquidity convenience yield is gone, worth maybe 50 to 70 basis points, which helps explain why long yields stay high even when inflation expectations look anchored. Cleveland Fed's Beth Hammack added that raising rates will not reopen Hormuz or stop data-center demand.

Tied to today's hot CPI, this keeps the higher-for-longer story alive for mortgage quotes and the 10-year. Additional hikes remain on the table even as officials debate timing, which matters after today's hotter CPI re-priced October odds near 54%.

Expand 3-minute read

Waller's comments landed Tuesday and age well next to Wednesday's CPI miss.

Asked when AI-related cost pressures might fade, Waller said the impulse could last two to three years given spending already committed. He said hyperscalers still plan trillions in outlays over the next three years, so policymakers have to decide how to respond to that demand. Anticipated productivity benefits remain further out. In the meantime, AI bidding for trades, construction inputs, electronics, and land is an inflation channel the Fed cannot ignore. That sits beside energy and tariff shocks already in the 2026 story.
