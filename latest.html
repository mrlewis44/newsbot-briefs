Cook spoke Friday in Santiago at the 29th Annual Conference of the Central Bank of Chile. Her speech was titled "The U.S. Economy in Global Context."

7) New bipartisan House bill orders a GAO study of zero-down FHA loans and speeds FHA foreclosures in nonjudicial states

Wed show

So-what: This does not create a zero-down FHA loan. It asks for a study. But it puts zero-down FHA on the table in Washington, which is a strong first-time buyer talking point.

1-minute read

Reps. Young Kim (R-Calif.) and Nikema Williams (D-Ga.) introduced H.R. 11082, the Housing Process Parity Act, on Nov. 19. It would direct the Government Accountability Office to study whether zero-down-payment FHA loans are feasible. The study would look at benefits for low-, moderate-income and first-time buyers, borrower risks, safeguards, and effects on FHA's insurance fund.

The bill's other half fixes a servicing snag. Rules around FHA partial claim liens can force servicers into judicial foreclosure even in nonjudicial states, which drags out timelines and costs before a vacant home can be resold. The bill would bring FHA in line with authority Congress gave VA last year.

The MBA endorsed it. HousingWire cited an Urban Institute analysis finding that moving from 96%-99% loan-to-value to 100%-104% raised default probability by just 12 basis points, which the authors called statistically insignificant.

For buyers today, low-down options remain FHA at 3.5%, VA and USDA at zero, and down payment assistance programs.

Expand 3-minute read

This is a small bill with a big headline, so it is worth being precise.

What it does: H.R. 11082, the Housing Process Parity Act, was introduced in the House on Nov. 19 by Rep. Young Kim of California and Rep. Nikema Williams of Georgia. It is bipartisan. It has two parts.

Part one is about foreclosed FHA homes. When an FHA borrower gets help through a partial claim, HUD takes a junior lien for the deferred amount. Under current law, differing federal rules on how those liens are treated can force servicers to use judicial foreclosure on some FHA loans, even in states that normally allow faster nonjudicial foreclosure. That lengthens timelines and raises legal costs before a vacant home can go back on the market. The bill would clarify partial claim lien treatment after nonjudicial foreclosures, bringing FHA closer to authority Congress gave the VA home loan program last year.

Part two is the zero-down study. The bill directs the GAO to study the feasibility of FHA-insured mortgages with no down payment. The study would cover potential benefits for low-income, moderate-income and first-time buyers, risks to borrowers, possible safeguards and the effect on FHA's Mutual Mortgage Insurance Fund. It does not create a zero-down program.

Scotsman Guide's headline summed it up: the bill orders a study, not a program.

Who supports it: The Mortgage Bankers Association endorsed it. MBA President Bob Broeksmit said it "advances FHA program improvements, provides parity across government-insured mortgage programs," and that clarifying partial claim lien treatment would give servicers more certainty and lower costs for borrowers.

The data point: HousingWire cited an Urban Institute analysis from earlier this year by Alexei Alexandrov, Laurie Goodman, Ted Tozer and Sam Valverde. It found that moving from a 96% to 99% loan-to-value range to 100% to 104% raised the default probability by just 12 basis points, a difference the authors called statistically insignificant. In plain terms: putting 3.5% down versus zero down may not change default risk much. What matters more is income, credit and payment.

Context: FHA and VA foreclosures just hit their highest level in nearly a decade, per Scotsman Guide's headline last week, and the MBA's Q3 survey had 1.89% of FHA loans in foreclosure, the highest since early 2017. That will make some lawmakers cautious about zero-down.

Why it matters for buyers now: Down payment is still the top barrier we hear. Zero-down already exists through VA, USDA in eligible areas, and some down payment assistance programs layered on FHA. This bill is a long way from law, and Congress is on Thanksgiving recess.

For the show: "Is zero-down FHA coming?" is a great hook. The honest answer is not soon, but here is what you can do today.

Mr. Cooper's order covers the November 2023 cyberattack that exposed borrower data and shut down systems, including the online payment portal. Rocket said it has strengthened its security program, adopted a formal data disposal policy and added monitoring and training.

Cyber compliance is a licensing issue now, not just an IT task.

The settlements: NYDFS Acting Superintendent Kaitlin Asrow announced two consent orders Nov. 19. Rocket Mortgage LLC pays $2 million. Nationstar Mortgage, doing business as Mr. Cooper, pays $4.75 million. Rocket is responsible for both now that it owns Mr. Cooper.

Rocket's findings: The order traces to a 2021 on-site examination. NYDFS found Rocket failed to notify the department of cybersecurity incidents within the required 72 hours on seven occasions between December 2019 and August 2021. In six of the seven cases, the department only learned of them after examiners uncovered them. The exam also found gaps in Rocket's processes for multi-factor authentication, secure disposal of data and incident notification, and NYDFS cited insufficient policies to protect customers' nonpublic information.

Mr. Cooper's findings: The order stems from the widely reported November 2023 cyberattack. Hackers got into systems, sensitive borrower data was exposed, and the company had to lock down technology, including its online payment portal. NYDFS cited failures tied to data protection, risk assessments and incident reporting.

Rocket's response: A spokesperson told HousingWire the company "has strengthened our information security program, adopted a formal data disposal policy, enhanced monitoring, team member training and testing." Rocket noted the Mr. Cooper incident happened two years before its acquisition.

The size of the fines is less important than the pattern. Late notice, missing MFA and weak data disposal are the most common findings in state cyber exams because they are the easiest to prove. A regulator only needs one incident that was not reported on time, or one system without MFA, to write a finding. The Rocket order shows examiners will go back years: the late reports date to 2019 through 2021, and the settlement came in late 2026.

Why it matters to a broker shop: New York's Part 500 cybersecurity rule applies to licensed mortgage companies, not just banks. And the FTC Safeguards Rule applies to mortgage brokers nationwide, with its own breach-notice requirement. The three themes here show up in almost every state exam: report incidents quickly, require multi-factor authentication everywhere, and have a written policy for disposing of borrower data.

A quick self-check worth doing during the Arbor migration: Is MFA on for email, the LOS, the CRM and cloud storage for every user? Is there a written incident response plan with a named person who decides when to notify regulators? Are old borrower files and devices disposed of under a written policy? Are vendors, including AI tools that touch borrower data, covered by the risk assessment?

None of this is a fine risk for BuyWise today, but examiners are clearly reading these consent orders too.

The vote: The Senate passed S. 5653, the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, 62-33 on Thursday night, Nov. 19.
