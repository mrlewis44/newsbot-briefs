3) Fed hawks speak up: Dallas Fed's Logan wants at least two more hikes, December hike odds near 76%

Wed show

So-what: Short-term rates are likely heading higher. Mortgage rates can still fall if long-term bonds keep rallying, but the Fed is not riding to the rescue.

1-minute read

Two days after the Fed held rates steady, two Fed presidents said more hikes are coming.

Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan said Friday the Fed "needs to implement at least two more 25-basis-point rate increases." After that, she said, it would make sense to pause and reassess. She votes on policy in December.

St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem said "additional firming of monetary policy will be necessary, and soon." He called inflation "too high," pointing to PCE inflation above 3%.

The Fed's current range is 3.75% to 4.00%. It hiked once in September. Fed funds futures priced about a 76% chance of another quarter-point hike in December, per CME FedWatch on Friday.

Logan made an interesting point for mortgages. She said that as long as markets understand the Fed's plan, "the precise timing of each rate increase has little effect on the longer-term rates most borrowers pay."

So what: the Fed raising short-term rates does not automatically raise mortgage rates. Mortgage rates follow the 10-year Treasury and MBS. HELOC rates and other prime-based loans would move up with a hike.

Expand 3-minute read

The Fed held rates steady last Wednesday on a 10-2 vote. By Friday, two of its regional presidents were making the case for more hikes. Here is what they said and what it means for borrowers.

Lorie Logan, Dallas Fed. Speaking to students at Davidson College, Logan said: "It remains clear to me, given the current outlook, that the FOMC needs to implement at least two more 25-basis-point rate increases." After that, she said, "it would make sense in my view to pause and examine economic and financial developments to determine what further action may be appropriate." Reuters called it some of the most explicit rate guidance from a Fed official since Chairman Kevin Warsh took over in May.

Logan also said the Fed does not need to hike at every meeting. "Skipping a meeting does not change the monetary policy destination," she said. She argued that a gradual pace can help households and businesses adjust and reduce the risk of a sharper slowdown. She pointed to market volatility tied to tech investment news and geopolitics.

Logan is a voting member in December. She voted with the majority to hold last week, which she said fits her view that rates need to end up at least half a point higher.

Alberto Musalem, St. Louis Fed. In remarks to the Shadow Open Market Committee in New York, Musalem said: "I judge that additional firming of monetary policy will be necessary, and soon." He called the economy "resilient" and the labor market "remarkably stable and balanced," with unemployment at 4.2%. But he said inflation is "too high," citing PCE inflation above 3%. "Moving sooner in smaller steps while inflation expectations are anchored has a better chance of succeeding," he said. Musalem does not vote until 2028.

Where markets stand. The fed funds range is 3.75% to 4.00%. The Fed hiked a quarter point in September. As of Friday, CME FedWatch showed about a 76% chance of a quarter-point hike at the December meeting. Scotsman Guide led its Monday newsletter with Logan's "at least two more" comment.

Why this does not automatically mean higher mortgage rates. This is the part buyers often get wrong. The Fed sets the overnight rate. Mortgage rates follow the 10-year Treasury and mortgage-bond prices, which price in expected Fed moves well ahead of time. Logan said it herself: "As long as market participants understand the FOMC's policy plans, the precise timing of each rate increase has little effect on the longer-term rates most borrowers pay." She also said higher long-term rates are "more a message that investors believe monetary policy has work to do than an independent headwind."

That is why the 10-year yield can fall this morning even with December hike odds high. Oil, tech stocks and jobs data are driving long rates right now.

Where it does bite. HELOCs, credit cards and other prime-based loans move with the Fed. A December hike would raise those rates by a quarter point. Adjustable-rate loans tied to short-term indexes also feel it at reset.

So what. A December hike is likely, and buyers will hear "Fed raises rates" headlines. The useful message for clients is that a Fed hike and a mortgage-rate move are not the same thing.

HousingWire's Logan Mohtashami says the path to mortgage rates under 7% is open again.

In October, the 10-year Treasury threatened to close above 5.35%, which he says could have pushed mortgage rates above 8%. It held, and the 10-year is now below 5%.

His weekly data, through Oct. 30:

Pending home sales: 52,050 this year vs. 55,214 a year ago. Still lower, but higher than two weeks ago.

Inventory: 914,833 homes, up 6.78% from last year.

New listings: 57,265 vs. 55,716 last year.

Price cuts: about 43% of listings vs. 42% a year ago.

Mortgage spreads: 2.23%, flat for the week. If spreads were as bad as 2023, he says rates would be 8.15% instead of 7.27%.

His recipe for sub-7%: an end to the Iran conflict, no worse trade war, and an AI slowdown that pushes money into bonds. His rule of thumb: demand grows when rates are under 6.64%.

So what: buyers who were waiting for "better" are getting a little better now, with more inventory and more price cuts than last year.

Expand 3-minute read

Logan Mohtashami, HousingWire's lead analyst, wrote a Halloween column making the case that sub-7% mortgage rates are back in play. Here is what he wrote and the data behind it.

The October scare. Mohtashami said the market "survived the crazy month of October." The 10-year Treasury had the potential to close above 5.35%. If that had happened, he wrote, it could have triggered more bond selling and pushed mortgage rates above 8%. The 10-year held that line and has since dropped below 5%. Mortgage rates are now "closer to 7% than 8%."

Why rates fell. Mohtashami pointed to last week's Fed meeting, where Chairman Kevin Warsh said tighter policy has created financial pain for some AI companies. With some AI firms having debt-financing trouble and missing estimates, the 10-year moved lower even with high oil prices and firm economic data.

The recipe for sub-7%. He called it a "trinity": an end to the Iran conflict, with oil trading between $67 and $82; a trade war that does not get worse; and an AI slowdown. He also noted a head-and-shoulders pattern in the 10-year yield since Sept. 10 that could break lower. The other side: if AI rebounds, the conflict continues and the trade war grows, rates could stay above 7%.

Spreads are doing a lot of work. The spread between mortgage rates and the 10-year was 2.23% last week, flat from the week before. Historically, spreads have run 1.60% to 1.80%. But compared with recent years, today's spread is a gift. With the worst spreads of 2023, rates would be 8.15% today, not 7.27%. With the worst of 2024, 7.77%. With the worst of 2025, 7.58%.

Demand data. His weekly pending home sales were 52,050 last week vs. 55,214 a year ago. That is still lower year over year, but higher than two weeks ago, which he called the key takeaway. Purchase applications rose 4% week to week but remain down significantly from last year. So far in 2026, purchase apps have had 16 positive weekly prints, 23 negative and 5 flat.

Supply data. Inventory rose from 911,956 to 914,833 homes in the week of Oct. 23 to Oct. 30. A year ago, inventory fell over the same week. Inventory growth is running at 6.78% year over year. New listings were 57,265 last week vs. 55,716 a year ago, and he called 2026 the healthiest new-listing year since 2022.

Price cuts. About 43% of listings had a price cut last week, vs. 42% a year ago. He had forecast a national price decline of 0.62% for 2026, but said that may be hard to hit since most indexes show 1% to 2% growth. Case-Shiller's latest was 2.1%.

The key rule of thumb. Over the last three years and 10 months, housing demand has grown when rates are under 6.64% and heading toward 6%. It has not grown when rates are over 6.64% and heading above 7%.

So what for buyers. Today's market gives buyers more choices and more room to negotiate than last year, with rates easing from their October peak. That is a good Wednesday show segment: the data says buyers have leverage, even at 7%.

5) Zillow Home Loans will cover the full 3.5% FHA down payment as a forgivable 0% second

Wed show

So-what: A big-brand lender just made zero-down FHA a marketing headline. Brokers will hear about this from buyers and agents.

1-minute read

Zillow Home Loans launched a program that covers the full minimum FHA down payment.

Here is how it works. Eligible buyers get up to 3.5% of the purchase price as a second loan. It has 0% interest and no monthly payment. It is forgiven after five years of on-time payments if the buyer still lives in the home. If they sell, move out or refinance before year five, the full amount is due.

Requirements: a 640 minimum credit score, an FHA loan through Zillow Home Loans, a primary residence, and qualifying for the full payment. There is no income cap and no first-time buyer rule.

It is available in 43 states and D.C. It is not offered in Arizona, Delaware, Florida, Massachusetts, New York, Vermont or Virginia. California is not on the excluded list.

On a $400,000 home, that is up to $14,000, per Mortgage Nuggets.

Zillow's own survey of 57,000 buyers found 70% use savings for a down payment and about 30% rely on family.

So what: for buyers, this solves cash, not payment. FHA mortgage insurance still applies, and the refinance payback rule matters if rates fall.

Expand 3-minute read

Zillow Home Loans announced on Oct. 28 that it will cover the full minimum FHA down payment for eligible buyers. Mortgage Nuggets and HousingWire both flagged it this week. Here is what is in it and what it means.

The program. Buyers can receive up to 3.5% of the purchase price or appraised value, whichever is lower. That covers the full FHA minimum down payment. Buyers can add up to 6.5% of their own money for a 10% down payment if they want.

The structure. The money comes as a second loan with 0% interest and no monthly payment. It is forgiven after five years of on-time payments on the main mortgage and continued owner occupancy. If the buyer moves out, sells or refinances before year five, the full amount is due.

Who qualifies. A 640 minimum credit score. An FHA loan through Zillow Home Loans. A primary residence purchase. And the buyer has to qualify for the full monthly payment under standard underwriting. There is no income cap and no first-time buyer requirement. FHA mortgage insurance still applies, and the first mortgage has a standard rate and payment.

Where it works. 43 states and Washington, D.C. It is not available in Arizona, Delaware, Florida, Massachusetts, New York, Vermont or Virginia. California is not on the excluded list.

Zillow's pitch. Eric Wilson, Zillow's head of mortgages, said: "Buyers tell us the down payment is what's holding them back, and we hear the same thing from agents about their clients." Zillow cited its survey of more than 57,000 buyers and would-be buyers. 70% use savings for a down payment. About 30% rely on a gift or loan from family or friends. More than 25% sell stocks or other investments. More than 20% tap retirement accounts.

The math. Mortgage Nuggets ran the example: on a $400,000 home, the program covers up to $14,000.

What buyers should ask. First, the refinance trap. If rates drop meaningfully in the next five years and the buyer refinances, the second loan is due in full. That could make a refinance harder or more expensive. Second, it solves cash to close, not the monthly payment. The buyer still pays a full FHA payment, including mortgage insurance, on 100% of the price. Third, it only works with Zillow as the lender, so buyers cannot shop the first mortgage rate elsewhere and keep the assistance.

What brokers should know. Mortgage Nuggets put it directly: "A good rate may not be enough against an offer that removes the down payment." Expect agents and buyers to ask whether you can match it. Many state and local programs already offer forgivable or deferred seconds, but they often have income limits, first-time buyer rules, or a separate application. Zillow's version is simple and tied to a brand people know.

So what for the show. Down payment is the number one hurdle for first-time buyers. This is a clean way to explain how down payment assistance works, compare it with local programs and conventional 3% down, and show the trade-offs, with the point that the team helps buyers in many states, not just California.

6) FICO loses share: VantageScore 4.0 was about 6% of GSE loans in Q3, first time the GSEs disclosed it

Wed show

So-what: Score choice is now real at the GSEs. Pricing is aligned across models, so lenders picking the score that helps the borrower can save buyers money.

1-minute read

For the first time, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac told us how often lenders are using VantageScore instead of FICO.

The answer for the third quarter: about 6% of new loans at both companies.

At Freddie Mac, Classic FICO was used on 94% of Q3 loans, down from 100% in Q2. At Fannie Mae, VantageScore was 6% of Q3 purchases and 2% year to date.

The shift is concentrated. Analysts at KBW found that in August, 99% of VantageScore loans came from two lenders. Rocket used it on 30% of its GSE loans and UWM on 25.3%.

Why now: Fannie aligned its loan-level pricing across FICO and VantageScore starting Oct. 1, and all lenders have been eligible to use VantageScore since September.

FICO's CEO says VantageScore's ceiling is "in the 20s" percent. FICO's scores revenue still grew 41% last quarter.

One more Fannie number: loans with FICO scores under 620 were 2% of Q3 purchases, up from under 0.5% a year ago, after the 620 minimum was removed in DU.

So what: for some buyers, the score model a lender uses can change pricing. Ask how your lender handles it.

Expand 3-minute read

Scotsman Guide led its Monday newsletter with this: FICO lost "meaningful" market share to VantageScore in the third quarter. It is the first time the GSEs have disclosed how often each score model is used.

The headline numbers. VantageScore 4.0 accounted for about 6% of third-quarter new business at both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. At Freddie, Classic FICO was used on 94% of Q3 loans, compared with 100% in the second quarter. At Fannie, VantageScore was 6% of Q3 acquisitions and 2% year to date.

Why it is happening now. Fannie Mae aligned its loan-level price adjustments across FICO and VantageScore, effective Oct. 1, 2026. That removed a pricing penalty for using the newer model. All lenders have been eligible to deliver loans with VantageScore since September. Once pricing matched, lenders had a reason to choose the model that helps the borrower or costs less.

Who is using it. The shift is concentrated in a few big lenders. KBW analysts found that in August, VantageScore made up 5.6% of volume, and 99% of those loans came from two lenders. Rocket used VantageScore on 30% of its GSE production, up from 17%. UWM used it on 25.3%. An RBC analyst said VantageScore is near 20% at UWM and Rocket. CrossCountry Mortgage has said borrowers save an average of $2,500 with VantageScore 4.0.

FICO's view. FICO CEO Will Lansing put VantageScore's ceiling "in the 20s" percent. FICO is not hurting yet. Its scores revenue was $459 million last quarter, up 41%, and mortgage origination revenue rose 97%, driven by its higher per-score pricing. HousingWire also reported that VantageScore CEO Silvio Tavares has stepped down, with Anthony Hutchinson named interim CEO.

The lower-score story. Fannie's numbers also showed that loans to borrowers with FICO scores under 620 were 2% of Q3 acquisitions, up from under 0.5% a year ago. That follows Fannie's removal of the 620 minimum score in Desktop Underwriter in November 2025. It is a small share, but it is a real change in who can qualify.

Freddie's Q3 buyer profile. Freddie Mac's Q3 release added useful context. First-time buyers were 51% of its purchase borrowers. The weighted average original credit score was 759. It financed about 222,000 purchase borrowers and 64,000 refinance borrowers. The single-family serious delinquency rate was 0.61%.

What it means for brokers. Score model is becoming a pricing lever, like it has always been for credit tiers. Large retail and wholesale lenders are already using it. Brokers should know which of their wholesale partners deliver VantageScore loans, and when it helps a borrower. A borrower with thin credit history, or a FICO near a pricing break, may score differently under VantageScore 4.0.

So what for buyers. The message for the show is simple. Your credit score is not one number, and the model your lender uses can affect your rate and fees. With pricing now aligned at Fannie Mae, it is fair to ask a lender which score they use and whether another option helps.

Expand 3-minute read

If you want to know why mortgage rates moved on a given day this fall, start by checking tech stocks. That is the main point of Tom Lawler's latest update, published Sunday on Calculated Risk.

The correlation shift. Lawler wrote that the "surprisingly high correlation" between interest rates and oil prices this summer "completely broke down in October." It was "supplanted by a high correlation between interest rates and Tech stocks." Several analysts read that as investors rotating out of tech stocks and into bonds. When tech sells off, bonds rally and rates fall.

What it did to mortgage bonds. The current-coupon MBS yield fell 34 basis points from the end of September to the end of October. That reversed exactly half of the 68 basis point increase from the end of August to the end of September. Interestingly, the drop gained steam right after the somewhat hotter-than-expected September CPI and PPI reports. Spreads between mortgage bonds and Treasuries were modestly higher in October after widening more in September. Lawler tied the wider spreads since August partly to higher expected interest-rate volatility.

Friday as an example. MBS Live's Friday recap was titled "Bonds Almost Able to Break Even Thanks to Tech Turmoil." Bonds were down about an eighth by mid-morning. Around 12:40 PM Eastern, news broke that Anthropic is unlikely to get an investment-grade rating after its IPO. The NASDAQ fell further, and bonds climbed back to about unchanged.

The weekend headlines. The Wall Street Journal's Monday edition ran several AI-finance stories. "OpenAI Raises Less Than Half of $30 Billion Target." "Wall Street Is Starting to Call the Shots in AI Build-Out." "AI Investors Are Racing to Get Insurance on the Biggest Market Bet in History." These are headlines only; the articles were not read for this brief. HousingWire's Logan Mohtashami also wrote that Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh said tighter policy has caused financial pain for some AI companies, and that this helped push the 10-year lower.

GSE MBS buying. Lawler also tracked Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac's own mortgage-bond holdings. In September, Fannie added $9.923 billion of agency MBS and Freddie added $1.785 billion. Lawler called that "a far cry" from the $200 billion of GSE MBS purchases implied by the President's Jan. 8 post. So far, he wrote, the news of more GSE buying has had little market impact.

Why this matters for rates. Today, bad news for AI financing tends to be good news for mortgage rates. But this is a two-way street. HousingWire's Mohtashami warned that an AI rebound is one of the things that could keep rates above 7%. A strong tech earnings week or a big funding round could push money back out of bonds.

So what. When explaining rate moves to clients this month, tech and oil are the two stories to watch. Jobs data this week will add a third.

So-what: Renting is no longer getting cheaper. For buyers weighing rent vs. own, the 'wait and rent' discount is fading.

1-minute read

Rents have stopped falling.

Apartment List's October report, highlighted by Peter Boockvar, showed new-lease rents down 0.3% for the month, which is normal for the off-season. But rents are up 0.1% from a year ago. That is the first positive year-over-year reading in more than three years.

The vacancy rate fell to 7.0%. It is still high by historical standards, but it is falling after peaking earlier this year.

Concessions are still common. 32% of properties offer them, four points more than a year ago. Many are worth a month of free rent or more.

Apartment List said the big wave of apartment construction is ending, and the market is moving toward balance. Sun Belt and Mountain West markets that drove the declines are starting to rebound.

Boockvar added that renewal rents at public apartment REITs are running 3% to 4% higher. He expects Sun Belt supply to keep getting absorbed "as home buying remains unaffordable for many first-time buyers."

Expand 3-minute read

Peter Boockvar's Monday Boock Report highlighted a milestone in the rental market. Apartment List's October National Rent Report shows rents have turned positive year over year for the first time in more than three years.

The numbers. New-lease rents fell 0.3% from September to October, which Apartment List said is normal "as the rental market enters its off-season." Year over year, rents were up 0.1%. That is basically flat, but it marks "the first time in over three years that y/o/y rent growth is positive."

Vacancy. The national vacancy rate fell one tenth of a point to 7.0%. Apartment List said vacancy remains "elevated compared to long-run norms, but continuing to decline after peaking earlier this year."

Concessions. 32% of properties nationally are offering concessions. That is four percentage points higher than a year ago. Concessions include discounted rent, cash back, gift cards or free amenities, and they are often worth one month of free rent or more. So landlords are still working to fill units, even as asking rents level off.

Regional picture. Apartment List said the national decline in recent years was mostly driven by Sun Belt and Mountain West markets, but those regions are now starting to rebound. Many markets in the Northeast, Midwest and parts of the West Coast continue to see rents trend up.

The bigger story. Apartment List's bottom line: "For years, the U.S. rental market has been experiencing a prolonged period of sluggishness, as a large wave of multifamily construction had been driving soft pricing and occupancy. However, we're now at the tail end of that construction boom, and the market has been gradually moving toward more balanced conditions."

Boockvar's add. He noted the report covers new leases only. Renewal rents, based on what he has heard from publicly traded apartment REITs, are running 3% to 4% higher. He expects continued absorption of extra supply in the Sun Belt and mountain states "as home buying remains unaffordable for many first-time buyers."

Inflation angle. Rent is a key part of CPI. Flat rents have been helping hold down the shelter part of inflation. If rents start climbing again, that support fades, which matters for the Fed and for rates over time.

Other data in the same note. Fitch said its private credit default rate rose to 6.3% for the trailing 12 months in Q3, up from 6.0% in Q2 and the highest since it began tracking. Fitch blamed "persistently high real interest rates." Industrials and consumer products each had default rates of 9.7%. That is a sign of stress for heavily indebted companies, which can also nudge investors toward safer bonds.

So what for buyers. Many would-be buyers have been renting while they wait for lower rates or prices. With new-lease rents flat and renewals up 3% to 4%, the cost of waiting is rising. That is a useful point for the Wednesday show: rent vs. buy is not just about mortgage rates. It is also about what your landlord will charge you next year. Concessions are still out there, so renters can negotiate, but the easy discounts are shrinking.

Expand 3-minute read

ResiClub's Saturday report looked at Invitation Homes, the largest single-family rental landlord, and what its third quarter says about the housing market.

Net seller again. Invitation Homes added 216 homes in Q3 and sold 658. Gross proceeds were about $318 million, which works out to about $484,000 per home sold. It was the third straight quarter the company sold more homes than it bought. Its wholly owned portfolio now stands at 85,961 homes. The company's 2026 guidance calls for $900 million to $1 billion in home sales. It said the buyers of its homes are "mostly" families.

The stock-price gap. ResiClub pointed to the reason for selling. Since December, the company's average sale price has been about $460,000 per home. But its share price implies a value of about $280,000 per home. So selling homes at full value and buying back cheap stock makes sense for shareholders. Invitation Homes spent $250 million buying back 8.9 million shares in Q3, and about $989 million in buybacks so far.

Builders are offering landlords deep discounts. A year ago, Invitation Homes said some Sun Belt builders with unsold inventory offered it homes at 20% below market. On its Oct. 29 call, its chief investment officer said discounts are now often bigger than that, and that some builders reached out for the first time, trying to clear standing inventory before spring. Even so, ResiClub said Invitation Homes is mostly passing on the offers. "Most" of its 216 acquisitions were deliveries from builder partners. For context, PulteGroup recently reported incentives of 10.4%.

The policy backdrop. Section 1001 of the ROAD to Housing Act bars investors that own 350 or more single-family homes from buying more, effective Jan. 7, 2027. Treasury proposed its implementing rule Oct. 22, with public comments due Nov. 23. There are exceptions for build-to-rent communities and for renovate-to-rent deals, where renovation spending must reach 15% of the structure's value. That helps explain why large landlords that still buy are focused on new builds from builder partners.

Accidental landlords. ResiClub also published a Saturday note on "shadow" supply from accidental landlords, owners who kept a home as a rental instead of selling. Invitation Homes CEO Dallas Tanner asked on the call: "what do the mom and pops do that are maybe sitting on a home they're trying to sell? Does that then kind of come back into play as well?"

What it means for buyers. Three takeaways. First, the large institutional landlords that competed with first-time buyers in 2021 and 2022 are now net sellers in many markets. Their homes are going to families. Second, if builders will give landlords more than 20% off, regular buyers should ask hard about incentives, rate buydowns and price cuts on new homes. Third, new federal limits on big investors take effect in January 2027, which could ease competition further for starter homes.

So what. Investor competition is a common worry for first-time buyers. The data says the biggest players are pulling back. That makes a good, hopeful segment for the Wednesday show, backed by real numbers.

Expand 3-minute read

HousingWire's Neil Pierson reported an exclusive on Friday: CrossCountry Mortgage, a top-five lender, has sent cease-and-desist letters to at least 25 former loan officers. Mortgage Nuggets led with it Monday.

What the letters say. The letters are dated Oct. 29 and were signed by Ashley C. Wakefield, senior corporate counsel at the Cleveland-based lender. Each contains the same three parts: a cease-and-desist order, a demand for payment, and a litigation preservation notice.

The legal theory. CrossCountry cites a two-year non-solicitation window in its employment agreements. It points to a provision creating a "legal presumption" that any mortgage product or service provided to a past, present or future CCM customer violates the agreement. In the company's words, "each loan is presumed to violate your non-solicitation covenant." It also asserts that borrowers' non-public personal information, protected under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, is confidential company material, and that every loan the LOs handled "remains CCM's sole and exclusive property."

The money. CrossCountry demands 75 basis points on each refinanced loan. The amounts in the letters HousingWire saw ranged from several thousand dollars to nearly $20,000. Payment is due by check to Cleveland within 10 business days.

Other demands. Stop originating loans for any CCM customer, prospect or referral source. Stop using any CCM confidential material. Certify in writing that all company materials have been returned.

The preservation notice. Recipients are told to preserve offer letters and pay details from their current employer, loan files, lead-source data, CRM and loan origination system records, and communications with any current or former CCM borrower. It covers personal devices and digital tools, naming email, texts, instant messages, voicemails, call logs, calendar entries, shared drives, cloud accounts and "AI chat history." Recipients must stop any auto-delete practices. Failure to preserve evidence "may expose you to sanctions for spoliation." If they do not comply in 10 business days, "CCM may file suit without further notice."

How big could this get. RETR data shows 843 loan officers have left CrossCountry in the past 14 months. 61 of them did at least $20 million in volume in that period. It is not known how much of that volume came from former CCM clients. HousingWire said this could be part of a larger pattern.

Why it matters for brokers. Refinance volume is coming back slowly as rates ease. LOs who move often call their past borrowers first. That is exactly the behavior these letters target. A broker shop hiring from a big retail lender should ask to see the new LO's employment agreement and get legal advice on what is enforceable in that state. California generally limits non-compete agreements, but non-solicitation and confidentiality claims are separate issues, and this brief does not judge how they would play out.

The AI angle. Naming "AI chat history" in a preservation notice is new. If LOs paste borrower data into AI tools, that history could become evidence. That is a good reason for any shop to set clear rules about borrower data in AI tools.

So what. Recruiting is still a growth path. This is a reminder to do it carefully.

Expand 3-minute read

PennyMac Financial Services reported third-quarter results Thursday. HousingWire's Neil Pierson covered the details, and they matter for brokers because PennyMac is a large wholesale lender.

The bottom line. Net income was $29 million, down 84% from a year ago but up 32% from the second quarter. Revenue was $469 million. Annualized return on equity was 3%, up from 2% in Q2 but well below 15% a year ago.

Two different businesses. Origination is struggling while servicing is thriving. CEO David Spector said a fast jump in 30-year rates, roughly 90 basis points to an average of 7.4% at the end of Q3, created "one of the most constrained origination environments our industry has seen since 2023."

Origination numbers. Total loan acquisitions and originations were $24.4 billion, down 30% from Q2 and 33% from a year ago. Fallout-adjusted locks were $23.1 billion, down 27% from Q2 and 37% from a year ago. By channel:

Broker: $4.7 billion, down from $6.5 billion in Q2 and $5.9 billion a year ago.

Correspondent: $15.6 billion, down from $20.5 billion and $27.2 billion.

Consumer direct: $2.8 billion, down from $4.5 billion and $3.9 billion, driven by weaker refinance demand.

Production revenue margins were 76 basis points, nearly unchanged from 77 in Q2. Production revenue was $175 million, down 40% from a year ago. The segment had a $7 million pretax loss, vs. $38 million in income in Q2 and $123 million a year ago.

Servicing. Pretax income from servicing was $99 million, up from $22 million in Q2. The owned servicing portfolio reached $496 billion, up 6% from a year ago. Slower prepayments, 8.1% vs. 11.6% in Q2, helped. The total servicing portfolio is $737 billion and should pass $1 trillion after its purchase of Cenlar's subservicing business closes, now expected in early 2027.

Cost cuts and an MSR sale. PennyMac outlined about $160 million in annual expense reductions from Q2 2026 to Q1 2027. A spokesperson confirmed layoffs in mortgage fulfillment operations. The company is selling $29 billion of low-coupon conventional servicing rights for about $490 million, mostly to pay down debt and buy back stock. Spector said automation of "labor-intensive fulfillment workflows" will permanently lower the cost to produce loans.

Leadership. Chief strategy officer Kevin Ryan becomes CFO on Jan. 1, 2027, replacing Dan Perotti, who stays on as an adviser through Q1. President and chief mortgage banking officer Doug Jones will retire June 30, 2027.

What it means for brokers. PennyMac's broker channel shrank by about a quarter from Q2. The company is cutting fulfillment staff while leaning on automation. That can mean faster or slower turn times depending on how well the tech works. With margins steady at 76 basis points, PennyMac is not slashing price to buy volume. It is also worth noting Rocket's $709 million Q3 profit, reported last week, as a contrast in how differently big lenders are faring.

So what. Keep an eye on PennyMac turn times and pricing over the next few months. When a big wholesaler is in cost-cutting mode, it pays to have more than one strong partner for each loan type.
