2) Fed's favorite inflation gauge hit 3.8% in October; core 3.3%. A December hike is fully priced

Wed show

So-what: Inflation is near twice the Fed's target, so short-term rates are going up again on Dec. 9. Mortgage rates already reflect much of that, which is why bonds barely moved today.

1-minute read

The Commerce Department said the PCE price index rose 3.8% from a year ago in October, up from 3.7% in September and in line with forecasts. Core PCE, which strips out food and energy, was 3.3%, a tenth hotter than expected.

CNBC said the report reinforces a quarter-point Fed hike at the Dec. 8-9 meeting, which markets already fully price. CME FedWatch had traders expecting two more hikes in 2027, with the odds of a third falling to about 1 in 3 from 50-50 on Tuesday.

Consumer spending rose 0.3% in October, a tenth below forecast. Personal income jumped 0.7% against a 0.4% forecast, and the saving rate rose to 4.1%. CNBC tied the inflation pickup to the energy shock after the late-February strikes on Iran, tariffs and AI-driven demand.

Plain English: inflation is moving the wrong way, the Fed will hike again, and that is already baked into today's mortgage rates. Surprises from here matter more than the hike itself.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is what the inflation report means for mortgage clients.

The personal consumption expenditures price index, or PCE, is the inflation gauge the Federal Reserve officially targets. In October it rose at a 3.8% annual rate, according to the Commerce Department. That matched the Dow Jones consensus and was a tenth higher than September. The Fed's goal is 2%, so inflation is running close to double target. CNBC noted inflation has been above target for about five and a half years.

Core PCE, which removes food and energy, came in at 3.3%. That was a tenth above forecast. Policymakers tend to treat core as the better signal of where inflation is going. MBS Live's Matt Graham noted that the so-called supercore measure, services excluding housing, was actually lower. That helps explain why bonds did not sell off.

The spending and income side was mixed. Consumer spending rose 0.3% for the month, a tenth below forecast and matching the pace of price increases. Income surged 0.7% against a 0.4% estimate. The personal saving rate climbed to 4.1%. Higher income with steady spending means households have a bit more cushion, which supports purchase demand at the margin.

On the Fed: CNBC reported the market fully prices a quarter-point hike when the Fed meets Dec. 8-9. The bigger question is 2027. CME FedWatch showed traders still expect two more hikes next year, but they cut the odds of a third hike to about 1 in 3, down from 50-50 on Tuesday. That small shift is part of why yields fell today even with a hot core number.

Why is inflation reheating? CNBC pointed to three drivers. The energy spike after the late-February U.S. and Israeli attacks on Iran. Tariffs, which the White House expanded again this morning with proposed duties on 14 trading partners. And strong demand tied to the artificial intelligence buildout. Fwdbonds chief economist Chris Rupkey put it simply: "Inflation is too hot and it will not be brought under control as long as economic demand is also too hot."

The mortgage link: a Fed hike lifts short-term rates directly, which shows up fastest in HELOCs, which are usually tied to prime, and in some ARMs. The 30-year fixed follows the 10-year Treasury and MBS spreads, not the Fed funds rate. Because the December hike is fully priced, the 10-year has already absorbed it. What could move long rates now is a change in how many hikes come in 2027.

How to explain it to a buyer: "The Fed is going to raise its rate again in December, but that is already in today's mortgage pricing. What we watch now is whether inflation keeps rising into next year." That avoids promising lower rates and keeps the conversation on payment.

For HELOC borrowers and anyone holding a prime-based line, the December hike will raise their rate. That is worth a heads-up call before year-end, and it is a reason some may want to consider fixed-rate second options.

So-what: A strong economy plus hot inflation keeps the Fed hiking and keeps a floor under mortgage rates. Do not tell clients a recession rescue for rates is coming soon.

The Bureau of Economic Analysis revised third-quarter GDP growth up to a 4.3% annual rate from 3.4% in the first estimate. Second-quarter growth was 2.2%. The upgrade came mostly from investment and consumer spending.

Real final sales to private domestic purchasers, a cleaner read on underlying demand, grew 5.8%. Gross domestic income rose 4.4%. Corporate profits from current production rose $206 billion in the quarter.

Separately, initial jobless claims for the week ending Nov. 21 were 189,000, below the 200,000 forecast. Continuing claims were 1.697 million, below the 1.721 million estimate. Core durable goods orders also beat, per MBS Live, though headline durable goods rose only 0.2% vs. 0.6% expected, per Investing Live.

Plain English: the economy is not weakening. That supports jobs and incomes for buyers, but it also means the Fed has room to keep hiking, so rate relief has to come from somewhere else, like the stock market or inflation cooling.

Here is the growth picture and why it matters for rates.

The Bureau of Economic Analysis released its second estimate of third-quarter GDP this morning. Real GDP grew at a 4.3% annual rate in July, August and September. That is up 0.9 percentage point from the first estimate of 3.4%, and up from 2.2% in the second quarter. BEA said the revision mainly reflected higher investment and consumer spending. Government spending also turned up, while exports fell.

A few other numbers from the release are worth knowing. Real final sales to private domestic purchasers, which is consumer spending plus private fixed investment and strips out noisy items like inventories and trade, grew 5.8%. Real gross domestic income, the income-side measure of the economy, grew 4.4%. The average of GDP and GDI was also 4.4%. Those agree with each other, which makes the strength harder to dismiss. Corporate profits rose $206 billion in the quarter, after $384 billion in the second quarter. The price index for gross domestic purchases was revised down to 2.7%, while third-quarter PCE inflation held at 2.1% and core at 2.5%.

The labor market data also leaned strong. Initial jobless claims for the week ending Nov. 21 were 189,000, compared with a 200,000 estimate and 203,000 the prior week. Continuing claims, which track people still collecting benefits, were 1.697 million, under the 1.721 million forecast. Layoffs remain low.

Durable goods were mixed. Investing Live reported headline October durable goods orders up 0.2% against a 0.6% estimate. MBS Live's Matt Graham said core durable goods came in much stronger than expected. Core capital goods orders are a read on business investment, and the forecast was 0.7%.

Why bonds did not react: as covered in the rates item, MBS Live said these are not top-tier movers, and the market is focused on stocks and forward-looking risk. GDP is backward-looking. The third quarter ended two months ago. Still, the data tells you something about the backdrop for 2027.

The mortgage takeaway is about expectations. A 4.3% growth rate with core inflation at 3.3% gives the Fed no reason to cut and plenty of reason to keep hiking. ResiClub's Lance Lambert noted this week that most forecasts do not expect a big drop in mortgage rates unless something material shifts, such as a spike in unemployment or the AI buildout losing momentum. Today's data shows neither is happening yet.

There is a good side. Strong income and job security help buyers qualify and stay current. Purchase applications rose 10% last week. Delinquencies and layoffs stay manageable when growth is strong.

For conversations with clients: "The economy is strong, which is good for your job and income, but it means rates are not likely to fall fast. Plan around today's payment, not a hoped-for rate." Next big data point: the GDP third estimate on Dec. 23, and the Fed decision on Dec. 9.

4) FHFA set the 2027 conforming loan limit at $855,400; high-cost ceiling $1,283,100

Wed show

So-what: More California buyers stay in conforming instead of jumbo. FHFA's number is higher than the early limits Rocket, UWM, CrossCountry and Pennymac set this fall.

1-minute read

FHFA announced Tuesday that the 2027 baseline conforming loan limit for one-unit homes will be $855,400, up $22,650 from 2026. The 2.72% increase matches the rise in FHFA's house price index from Q3 2025 to Q3 2026.

The high-cost area ceiling, which is 150% of the baseline, rises to $1,283,100 for one-unit homes. Alaska, Hawaii, Guam and the U.S. Virgin Islands get a $1,283,100 baseline and a $1,924,650 ceiling. FHFA said limits will be higher in all but 24 counties.

Mortgage Nuggets noted the final number beat every early limit lenders adopted this fall: Rocket and CrossCountry at $845,000, UWM at $847,440 and Pennymac at $850,000. HousingWire said the 2.71% bump is smaller than last year's 3.25% and the 5%-plus gains of the two years before, reflecting a softer housing market.

Plain English: a buyer in a high-cost California county can now borrow up to $1,283,100 with agency pricing and guidelines. That is often cheaper and easier than jumbo. Check county limits before quoting.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is what the new loan limits change for California and multistate business.

Each year the Housing and Economic Recovery Act requires FHFA to adjust the baseline conforming loan limit to match the change in average U.S. home prices. On Tuesday, after publishing its third-quarter house price index, FHFA said prices rose 2.72% from Q3 2025 to Q3 2026. So the 2027 baseline for one-unit properties rises by the same percentage, to $855,400. That is $22,650 more than the 2026 limit of $832,750.

For high-cost areas, where 115% of the local median home value exceeds the baseline, the limit is set by local values up to a ceiling of 150% of the baseline. For 2027 that ceiling is $1,283,100. FHFA said median home values generally rose in high-cost areas this year, which lifted their limits. Alaska, Hawaii, Guam and the U.S. Virgin Islands have special rules, with a baseline of $1,283,100 and a ceiling of $1,924,650. FHFA said loan limits will rise in all but 24 U.S. counties or county equivalents. The full county list and map are on FHFA's site.

How it compares: last year's increase was 3.25%, and the two years before were above 5% each. HousingWire tied the smaller bump to a weaker housing market and higher-for-longer mortgage rates. Mortgage Nuggets pointed out that the final $855,400 is higher than every early limit lenders announced this fall. Rocket and CrossCountry went to $845,000. UWM went to $847,440. Pennymac went to $850,000. Expect those lenders to update to the official figure, and watch for effective dates, since some will accept 2027 limits on loans delivered after a certain date.

Why it matters for brokers: the gap between conforming and jumbo pricing has been meaningful this year. MBA's weekly survey had jumbo rates at 7.29% and conforming at 7.38% last week, so jumbo is not always more expensive. But agency loans offer lower down payment options, more flexible guidelines and mortgage insurance, which jumbo usually does not. A buyer who was just over the 2026 limit in Orange County or Los Angeles may now fit inside agency guidelines with less cash down.

California angle: most of coastal California sits at or near the high-cost ceiling. Confirm the exact 2027 county limit before structuring a deal, since not every high-cost county hits the full $1,283,100. For the other states the team serves, many counties will simply move to $855,400.

There is a show angle too. The limit rising more slowly is a quiet signal that national home price growth is cooling. FHFA's index rose 2.72%, compared with mortgage rates that are about a full point higher than a year ago, per Mortgage News Daily. Prices up a little, rates up a lot, is the affordability story in one line.

Action items: update pricing engine and scenario tools for 2027 limits as lenders adopt them, flag any pipeline loans near the old limit that could benefit from waiting for a lender's 2027 effective date, and use the new number in content for buyers shopping near the high-balance line.

5) October new home sales jumped 14.2% to a 701,000 pace; median price $391,300, down 3.2% from a year ago

Wed show

So-what: Builders are selling by cutting price and buying down rates. New homes are now cheaper than existing ones, which is a real option for first-time buyers.

1-minute read

The Census Bureau said new single-family home sales ran at a 701,000 annual rate in October, up 14.2% from a downwardly revised 614,000 in September and up 7.5% from a year ago. The forecast was 650,000. Census notes the monthly change is not statistically significant, with a margin of plus or minus 22.6%.

The median new home sold for $391,300, down 0.3% from September and 3.2% from October 2025. The average price was $459,900, down 12.1% from a year ago. Inventory fell to 470,000 homes, or 8.0 months of supply, down from 9.3 months.

A separate NAHB/Wells Fargo index found the typical new home sold for $393,300 in Q3, 9.2% less than the typical existing home at $432,900, the widest gap on record, per Newsweek.

Plain English: builders are moving homes with incentives while resale buyers stay stuck. For a buyer shopping on payment, new construction with a builder buydown deserves a hard look.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is what the new home sales report says and why it matters for buyers and the show.

The Census Bureau and HUD reported this morning that sales of new single-family homes ran at a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 701,000 in October. That is 14.2% above September's 614,000 and 7.5% above October 2025's 652,000. Forecasts were near 650,000. There is an important footnote. September was first reported at 676,000 and was revised down to 614,000. And Census flags both the monthly and yearly changes as not statistically significant, with margins of error above 20%. This series is notoriously volatile, so treat one strong month carefully.

Prices are the clearer story. The median new home sold for $391,300 in October, down 0.3% from September and 3.2% from a year ago. The average price fell to $459,900, down 2.2% from September and 12.1% from a year ago. The average dropping more than the median suggests a mix shift toward smaller, cheaper homes, which matches what builders have been saying about building to a payment.

Supply improved. There were 470,000 new homes for sale at the end of October, down 1.5% from September and 4.1% from a year ago. At the current sales pace, that is 8.0 months of supply, down from 9.3 in September and 9.0 a year ago. Six months is often called balanced, so builders still have plenty to sell, which keeps incentives alive.

The price comparison with resale is striking. Newsweek reported on the NAHB/Wells Fargo Cost of Housing Index, which found the typical new home sold for $393,300 in the third quarter, 9.2% less than the typical existing home at $432,900. NAHB said it is the largest gap since the index began. A median-income family earning $106,800 would spend 33% of income on a median new home payment versus 36% for an existing home. Historically, new homes cost more than resale.

Why the flip? Existing home sellers are holding firm on price because many have low-rate mortgages and little pressure to sell. Builders, by contrast, carry inventory and pay to hold it, so they cut prices and pay for rate buydowns. ResiClub reported this week that D.R. Horton is guiding to 79,000 to 84,000 closings in fiscal 2027 and expects rates to stay near current levels, so incentives are not going away soon.

For buyers: a builder offering a permanent or temporary buydown can lower the payment more than a small price cut. Compare total cost, not just rate. Make sure the buyer understands how temporary buydowns step up, and watch for builder-affiliated lender requirements tied to incentives. Brokers can often compete by showing the buyer a side-by-side.

For the show: "New homes are now cheaper than resale homes, by about $40,000 nationally" is a clear, data-backed headline. Pair it with today's 701,000 sales pace and the 8.0 months of supply. Add the caveat that one month of new home sales data can be revised a lot.

7) FHFA set the FICO 10T roadmap: Classic FICO retired at the GSEs by September 2028; FHA adds 10T and VantageScore 4.0 in January

So-what: Credit score choice is coming, but slowly. Plan for pricing grid and LOS changes in 2027-2028, not next month.

1-minute read

FHFA approved FICO 10T for use by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac and released a timeline to make it broadly eligible and retire Classic FICO by September 2028, HousingWire reported. The rollout mirrors the VantageScore 4.0 path.

A FICO 10T pilot is expected around December 2027, when lenders would deliver a subset of loans using it. Based on pilot results, FHFA expects to update upfront pricing grids and mortgage insurer eligibility rules, known as PMIERs.

At FHA, VantageScore 4.0 and FICO 10T are expected to go live alongside Classic FICO in January. MBA CEO Bob Broeksmit welcomed the roadmap and pushed again to end the tri-merge requirement.

The MBA statement also said it will work with FHFA to speed the timeline responsibly. Scotsman Guide framed it as FHFA clearing the decks for 10T.

Plain English: lenders will eventually pick between two newer credit models that can count things like rent and trended payment history. Some borrowers may score differently. Nothing changes for today's conventional pricing.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is what the credit score roadmap means for brokers.

FHFA announced Tuesday that it approved the FICO 10T credit score model for use by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, according to HousingWire. It also released an implementation roadmap that would make FICO 10T broadly eligible and retire Classic FICO across the GSE channels by September 2028. FHFA said the plan prepares lenders, mortgage insurers and loan origination system providers for "the final step in credit score modernization: lender choice between FICO 10T and VantageScore 4.0."

The timeline is staged. FHFA first validated both FICO 10T and VantageScore 4.0 in October 2022. In July 2026, Fannie and Freddie released historical FICO 10T performance data so lenders and investors could back-test. A FICO 10T pilot is expected to begin around December 2027. In that "launch and refine" phase, lenders will deliver a subset of loans underwritten with FICO 10T while FHFA and the GSEs monitor data quality, pricing and delivery. After the pilot, FHFA expects to update the upfront pricing grids, known as LLPAs, and PMIERs to reflect the new model. A limited and then broad rollout follows, ending with Classic FICO's retirement by September 2028.

At FHA, a separate track: VantageScore 4.0 and FICO 10T are expected to go live alongside Classic FICO in January. That is the near-term change brokers should watch, since FHA lenders may start offering the newer scores as options.

Industry reaction: MBA CEO Bob Broeksmit welcomed the milestone and said MBA will work with FHFA to "expedite the implementation timeline responsibly." He also repeated MBA's push for "continued reforms to end the tri-merge requirement," which would let lenders pull fewer credit reports and could lower costs for borrowers. Scotsman Guide's headline called it FHFA clearing "the decks" for FICO 10T and Classic FICO retirement.

What is different about the newer models: both FICO 10T and VantageScore 4.0 use trended data, meaning they look at payment patterns over time rather than a snapshot, and can incorporate things like rent payments where reported. Some borrowers with thin files or steady paydown habits may score higher. Others who carry revolving balances may score lower. Pricing grids will need to be rebuilt, which is why the GSEs are moving slowly.

Why it matters for a broker shop: credit report costs have been a hot issue all year, and a lender-choice world could create pricing differences between lenders based on which score they use. Loan origination systems, including the Arive setup in the Arbor Financial migration, will need updates to handle multiple score types. None of this affects loans being locked today.

Action items: track FHA's January go-live and which wholesale lenders adopt alternate scores first, and build a simple client explainer for when a borrower asks, "Which credit score are you using?"

Here is the compliance lesson for broker shops working with agents.

Pennsylvania Attorney General David Sunday reached a settlement with Bright Financial Group, Conquest Mortgage, several affiliated mortgage businesses and their owner, Barry Newhart, over alleged violations of federal and state consumer protection laws involving mortgage referrals and affiliated business arrangements. HousingWire framed the case as showing "where mortgage joint ventures can run into RESPA trouble."

The allegations, as summarized by Mortgage Nuggets: the companies sold real estate agents cheap, nonvoting stock in Newhart-owned mortgage brokerages. They also paid for event tickets and expensive dinners. The state said those benefits were given in exchange for client referrals, and the complaint put the value above $500,000, potentially as high as $1 million. A federal judge refused to dismiss the case in August, which likely pushed the parties to settle.

Settlement terms: a $65,000 payment, plus an additional $100,000 if the companies violate the deal. They did not admit wrongdoing and keep their licenses. Going forward, ownership and profit distributions in any joint venture cannot be tied to referrals. They must set up compliance monitoring, file sworn reports with the state and keep detailed records.

Why it matters: Section 8 of RESPA bans giving or accepting anything of value for referrals of settlement service business. Affiliated business arrangements are allowed only if they meet specific conditions, including disclosure to the consumer, no required use of the affiliate, and returns based on ownership interest rather than referrals. The red flags in this case are familiar: below-market ownership stakes offered to referral sources, and perks like tickets and dinners.

The bigger trend is that state attorneys general are stepping into RESPA enforcement. With federal enforcement shifting, states have used their own consumer protection laws and RESPA authority to bring cases. For a multistate broker, that means each state where the business operates can be a source of scrutiny.

One more detail worth noting: the complaint survived a motion to dismiss in federal court before settling. That means a judge found the alleged structure, discounted stock for agents plus perks, plausible enough as a RESPA violation to go forward. Settlement amounts in state cases can look small, but the compliance monitoring and sworn reporting terms are the real cost, and they last.

Practical checklist for any agent relationship: document the business purpose, price any ownership stake at fair market value, make sure distributions follow capital and ownership rather than referral counts, avoid gifts or entertainment that look like rewards for referrals, and use clear AfBA disclosures. Co-marketing must be paid at fair market value for actual services, split in proportion to use.

This is a good item to share with the team or a compliance consultant as a real-world example, since it shows exactly what a regulator counted as a thing of value.

11) Build-to-rent industry pushes back on Treasury's ROAD Act rules ahead of Jan. 7 institutional investor ban

Here is the fuller picture on the build-to-rent rules.

Earlier this year, the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act included a ban on large institutional investors buying single-family homes, with an explicit carve-out for build-to-rent communities. In October, Treasury released a notice of proposed rulemaking explaining how it would implement that exemption. The comment period closed Monday, Nov. 23. HousingWire TBD reported Treasury received more than 450 letters. The ban takes effect Jan. 7, so Treasury has only weeks to finalize the rules.

What Treasury proposed: newly built, never-owner-occupied BTR communities are exempt if a large investor buys them within 18 months of the certificate of occupancy and uses them only as rentals. Communities with both rental and for-sale homes would let builders sell the rental portion to big investors and market the rest to individual buyers. Transfers of existing BTR communities between large investors would be allowed, and the renovate-to-rent exemption would stay.

The pushback: HousingWire reviewed 10 letters from groups including NAA, NMHC, the National Rental Home Council, the MBA, Up for Growth Action, Berkadia, LGI Homes, Landmark Properties, Quinn Residences, AHV Communities and a coalition of 12 developers including Greystar and Brookfield Residential. All opposed the 18-month window. Quinn Residences CEO Richard Ross said, "To build and stabilize a new community takes about 30 months," so early certificates would start the clock long before the whole community is sellable.

The Greystar and Brookfield coalition warned that "a smaller potential buyer pool reduces liquidity and creates greater uncertainty regarding terminal value." MBA's letter said, "If large institutional investors cannot purchase horizontal multifamily communities (which individual families cannot), the values of those properties will fall and new construction and necessary maintenance and reinvestment will slow." Scotsman Guide's newsroom also reported MBA disputing Treasury's interpretation of the build-to-rent provision. NAA and NMHC asked Treasury to drop the deadline or allow purchases directly from the builder.

Amherst Group CEO Sean Dobson summed up the frustration: the law reads like BTR is carved out, but the rules make it feel like you have to "pat your belly and tap your head and hop on one foot" to qualify.

Another open question from the letters is how Treasury will treat single-plat projects and rehab exceptions, which developers say are too vague in the proposal. Until those definitions are final, BTR operators say capital for new projects will stay cautious.

Why brokers should care: the investor ban is meant to give individual buyers more access to single-family homes. With 8.0 months of new home supply nationally and builders like Dream Finders already marketing unsold homes to small investors, the final rules will shape who buys builder inventory in 2027. Small investors buying with DSCR loans are generally not the target of the ban, which is aimed at large institutions, so that channel may grow.

Watch for Treasury's final rule before Jan. 7, and whether it widens the 18-month window.
