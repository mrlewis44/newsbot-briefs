3) Lawler: the current-coupon mortgage bond yield is 6.27%, still near its 2026 high, and most of this year's 10-year rise came from higher expected short rates, not term premium

So-what: If the market expects short rates to stay higher for years, mortgage rates have a higher floor than many buyers hope, even on good rally days like today.

1-minute read

Housing economist Tom Lawler posted an update on mortgage bond yields Wednesday on Calculated Risk.

The current-coupon MBS yield, the yield on the mortgage bonds that back new loans, hit a new yearly high on Oct. 5. It has eased a bit since, but at 6.27% it is still well above where it sat in the first eight months of the year.

Spreads to Treasuries are close to the year's highs. Lawler says they are still not especially wide by historical standards, outside the years when the Fed was buying mortgage bonds. He ties the wider spreads to higher expected rate swings in the market.

The bigger point is why the 10-year rose this year. Many analysts blame a higher term premium, the extra pay investors want for holding long bonds. Lawler checked three Fed models. Two of them say almost all of the rise came from higher expected short-term rates over the next 10 years. The third puts slightly less than half on term premium.

Inflation expectations from TIPS are only slightly higher than at the end of 2025. So the market expects higher real rates.

So what: this is not a fear premium that can vanish overnight. It is a view about where rates settle.

Expand 3-minute read

Tom Lawler, a longtime housing economist, posted an update on mortgage bond yields and spreads on Bill McBride's Calculated Risk Wednesday afternoon. It is a short, data-heavy note, but it answers a question buyers keep asking in different words: why are rates so stubborn?

Mortgage bond yields. The current-coupon MBS yield is the yield on the mortgage bonds that new loans get pooled into. It is the closest market number to what lenders price rate sheets from. Lawler says it hit a new yearly high on Oct. 5. It has come down a bit since, but at 6.27% it remains well above the levels seen during the first eight months of the year.

Spreads. Lawler says spreads to Treasuries are close to the highs of the year. He adds that they are still not particularly wide by historical standards, if you leave out the periods when the Federal Reserve was buying mortgage bonds. He links the wider spreads to an increase in implied interest rate volatility. In plain terms, when the market expects bigger rate swings, investors want more extra yield to hold mortgages, because borrowers refinance when rates drop and keep their loans when rates rise.

The term premium question. A popular explanation for this year's higher long-term rates is a rising term premium. That is the extra return investors demand for tying money up in long bonds and taking the risk that rates change. It cannot be seen directly, so economists estimate it with models. Lawler compared three: the ACM model from the New York Fed, the KW model from the Fed Board, and the CR model from the San Francisco Fed. He looked at the change from the end of 2025 through Oct. 16, 2026.

The result. For the ACM and CR models, almost all of the increase in 10-year yields this year comes from a rise in the market's expected short-term interest rate over the next 10 years. For the KW model, slightly less than half of the increase comes from a higher term premium. His conclusion: "much of the increase in 10-year yields this year is not because of a rising term premium, but because of an increase in market expectations for short-term interest rates over the next 10 years."

Inflation or real rates? Lawler then checked TIPS, the inflation-protected Treasuries. Breakeven inflation rates, the market's implied inflation forecast, are only slightly higher than at the end of last year. Put together, he says that means the market expects higher real, inflation-adjusted, interest rates. He also says the market's view of the neutral real rate has gone up this year, and by more than the median forecast in the Fed's latest projections.

The AI link. Lawler adds that it is "perhaps not coincidental" that higher expected real rates began about when actual and expected borrowing tied to the AI buildout started to surge.

What it means for borrowers. Today's bond rally is real, and it is covered elsewhere in this brief. But Lawler's work suggests the bigger driver of higher rates this year is a lasting expectation that short rates stay higher, not a temporary fear premium. That kind of shift tends to move slowly. It also means spreads matter. With mortgage bond yields at 6.27% and spreads near the year's highs, any calming in rate volatility could help mortgage rates even if Treasuries do not move much.

So what for Josh: a useful framing for clients is that rates are not "stuck high" because of panic. The market thinks money will cost more for years. Buyers should plan on payments they can live with at today's rates, and treat any drop as a bonus to refinance into, not a plan.

French government bonds keep falling, and two of Josh's regular sources looked at what that means for U.S. rates. Their takes point in different directions, which is the useful part.

The French move. Peter Boockvar's Boock Report Thursday morning opened with France. The French 10-year yield was up 11 basis points today and 32 basis points this week alone. Wednesday's brief noted the French 10-year had touched 5% for the first time since July 2002, so this is a further move. Boockvar said that, for now, German bunds and U.S. Treasuries are the flight-to-safety trade, meaning money leaving French bonds is landing partly in U.S. Treasuries. He added: "I continue to believe that at least in the short term, yields in the US have seen a temporary peak." That is his opinion on the short term, not a promise about rates.

The real-world damage in France. Carrefour stock fell 14% Wednesday after the retailer disclosed losses from riots and theft. The company said about 50 of its stores were hit on the first day, and that curfews, shorter hours and precautionary closures are hurting sales at stores that were not damaged. French business confidence for October rose 1 point after falling 2, but Boockvar notes the survey likely came before last weekend's violence, and retail, employment and construction confidence all fell.

Chan's flip side. Anthony Chan, former global chief economist at JPMorgan Chase, wrote Wednesday that Wall Street is focused on the French-German spread, but the U.S. may have a spread problem too. The French-German 10-year spread was about 152.5 basis points as of Oct. 20, after nearly 159 basis points earlier in October. On the same date, the U.S. 10-year was about 5.12% and Germany's was about 3.42%. That puts the U.S.-German spread at roughly 170 basis points. It is wider than the French-German spread and, Chan says, has expanded considerably since the start of the year.

His question is whether investors are "rewarding the relative strength of the American economy" or "demanding compensation for the risks associated with U.S. inflation, federal deficits, and the expanding supply of Treasury securities." The free version of his post cuts off before his full answer, so the brief does not guess at it.

How the two fit. Both can be true at once. In a crisis abroad, money runs to Treasuries, which can pull U.S. yields down for a while. That is Boockvar's short-term point, and it fits this morning's bond rally covered elsewhere in the brief. Over a longer stretch, the U.S. has to sell a lot of debt, and investors may want extra yield for that. That is Chan's warning. It also lines up with Tom Lawler's note in this brief that the market now expects higher real rates for years.

What it means for borrowers. Mortgage rates follow the 10-year, plus a spread. Events in Paris can move a buyer's rate in Huntington Beach or in any of the other states the team serves. Short term, French stress may help. Longer term, U.S. deficits and Treasury supply are a reason rates may not fall as far as buyers hope.

So what for Josh: a good talking point is that rates are a global market. When other countries look riskier, U.S. bonds can benefit. But the U.S. is paying a growing premium over Germany, and that cost shows up in mortgage rates. No one, including these economists, is promising where rates go next.

7) A $55,000 cheaper house can cost $34 more a month: HousingWire shows why price cuts aren't pulling buyers back, as new pendings fall 13.1%

Wed show

So-what: Price cuts alone do not fix the payment when rates jump. This is the clearest way to explain to buyers and sellers why the monthly payment, not the list price, is what matters.

1-minute read

HousingWire has a simple example that explains this market.

In February, the average 30-year rate was 5.98%. A $450,000 home with 20% down had a principal and interest payment of about $2,154.

Now that same buyer finds a home for $395,000, but rates are 7.40%. The payment is about $2,188. The house is $55,000 cheaper, yet the payment is $34 a month higher.

That is why lower-priced markets are not seeing more buyers. HousingWire Data shows Omaha, Huntsville and Tulsa all have median asking prices under $400,000, yet fewer new contracts per new listing than in past years. In Tulsa, nearly 48% of listings have had a price cut.

Nationally, for the week ending Oct. 16, active single-family inventory hit 905,166 homes, up 5.3% from a year ago. New pending contracts fell 13.1% to 51,947. The median asking price was $434,900.

So what: for buyers, a price cut plus a rate buydown or a future refinance can matter more than price alone. For sellers, pricing has to work for the monthly payment buyers can actually afford.

Expand 3-minute read

This HousingWire piece, by Rachel Bader and HW Data, published Wednesday afternoon, is one of the most useful explainers of the week for buyers.

It starts with a payment math example. In February, the average 30-year fixed rate was 5.98%. A buyer looking at a $450,000 home with 20% down would pay about $2,154 a month in principal and interest. Now picture the same buyer finding a $395,000 home, but with rates at 7.40%. The payment rises to about $2,188. The home costs $55,000 less, yet the monthly payment is about $34 higher, before taxes and insurance.

That gap explains why price cuts have not brought buyers back, even in cheaper markets. HousingWire looked at three metros where median asking prices are under $400,000. It compared eight weeks from late August through mid-October in 2024, 2025 and 2026, using new pending contracts per 100 new listings.

Omaha has slowed for three years. It went from about 122 new pendings per 100 new listings in 2024 to 109 in 2025 and 94 in 2026. Inventory grew from about 1,940 homes to 2,350. Yet the median asking price is $388,375, up 1.7% from a year ago. About 31.8% of listings have had a price cut.

Huntsville's slowdown is newer. It was 98 in 2024, 105 in 2025, and 84 in 2026, with the latest four weeks between about 64 and 77. Median days on market rose to 82. The median asking price is $361,900, down 3.5%. About 40.9% of listings have been cut, and supply is about 4.1 months.

Tulsa shows the same direction. It went from 103 in 2024 to 97 in 2025 and 87 in 2026. The median asking price is $334,900, down 1.5%. Nearly 48% of listings have had a price cut, and supply is about 3.6 months.

The national numbers match. For the week ending Oct. 16, active single-family inventory reached 905,166, up 5.3% from a year ago. New listings rose 1% to 61,402. New pending contracts fell 13.1% to 51,947. The national median asking price was $434,900.

Altos Research data cited in Mark Hossler's Wednesday rate note, which Jeb forwarded, points the same way. About 43% of active listings have cut their asking price, more than at this point in October in any year from 2022 through 2025.

HousingWire is careful not to blame rates alone. Local incomes, jobs, supply and the mix of homes also matter. But the core point holds. A lower price and better affordability are not the same thing.

For the show, this is a strong segment. Emily and Jake hear that prices are falling and think it is time to wait for more. The math says the payment is what matters.
