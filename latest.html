2) Fed minutes: "almost all" officials see more hikes ahead, a couple floated 50 bps, and AI demand is now named an inflation risk

So-what: The October hold was a pause, not a pivot. A December hike is close to fully priced, which keeps a floor under mortgage rates into year-end.

1-minute read

Minutes from the October 27-28 FOMC meeting came out Wednesday. The Fed held at 3.75% to 4.00% on a 10-2 vote. Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack wanted a 25 bp hike right then.

The key line: "almost all participants" judged that further increases "would likely be appropriate" to get inflation back to 2%. Some favored a hike at that meeting. A couple said a 50 bp move might be needed if inflation data came in hot, which is what last week's CPI did.

CNBC reported that AI was central to the discussion. Officials called AI-related demand "a material source of inflation risk" and a possible source of financial instability. Several holders called the decision a "close call" and wanted markets to know it did not mean softer resolve.

Officials also said they expect inflation to fall sharply in 2027 and beyond. On jobs, many saw "modest tightening" in the labor market and called risks broadly balanced.

Rates barely reacted, per MND. Markets had already priced this after CPI and Waller's comments.

Expand 3-minute read

The minutes confirm what bonds have been trading for a week. The Fed is in a hiking cycle, and the only open questions are size and timing.

The vote and the debate. The Committee kept the target range at 3-3/4% to 4% by 10-2. Cook and Hammack dissented for a 25 bp increase. The minutes say some participants favored a hike at the meeting because inflation "had remained too high" with "few signs of it abating," and they judged policy "not sufficiently restrictive." A few warned that delay risked letting inflation become entrenched or forcing "a larger and more disruptive tightening later." A few of the hawks said they could have accepted a hold as long as markets understood more tightening was likely.

The forward guidance. "Almost all participants judged that, under their baseline outlooks for inflation and the labor market, further action to raise the target range for the federal funds rate ... would likely be appropriate," broadly in line with the September projections. Yahoo Finance noted the September dot plot showed one more hike this year and then a hold through 2027. Reuters highlighted that "a couple" argued for a half-point increase "if incoming inflation data were unfavorable," and that a few said inflation could need "a shift to a more restrictive policy stance." Reuters added that recent releases have met that unfavorable bar.

AI as an inflation story. CNBC's top takeaway was that officials put AI at the core of the outlook. Demand tied to the AI build-out was called "a material source of inflation risk," and officials saw it as a possible driver of financial instability. They also said inflation pressures were "broad based." At the same time, they projected inflation to fall off sharply in 2027 and beyond.

Labor. Reuters quoted the minutes saying "many pointed to signs of modest tightening in the labor market," and that officials expected conditions to stay stable with balanced risks. That matters because today's continuing claims jump (1.763M) is the kind of data that could start to shift that view, but one week will not do it.

Market reaction. MND said the minutes had no impact on rates Wednesday. ALM First said futures are heavily skewed toward 25 bps on December 9, with about 9% odds of 50 and zero odds of a hold. There is one more jobs report before the meeting, but the next CPI arrives the day after the decision.

What it means for us. The path of least resistance for short rates is up. Long rates, which drive mortgage pricing, can still improve if markets believe the Fed is getting ahead of inflation, as the 2-year to 10-year relationship shows. But do not tell clients a Fed cut is coming to rescue rates. The Fed's own minutes say the opposite. For the Wednesday show, the clean line is: the Fed is raising, not cutting, and buyers should plan payments around today's rates.

3) Lender CEO: "The market fell off a cliff." Highland Mortgage's October locks fell 51% year over year, and he wants g-fee relief

So-what: This is the IMB pain behind the data. The policy ask, cutting g-fees and repealing the 10 bp surcharge, is a talking point brokers can back.

1-minute read

In a HousingWire op-ed, Highland Mortgage CEO Mark Milam says his company was setting production records this spring. Then October lock volume fell 51% from October 2025. "Not a seasonal slowdown... A cliff."

He lines up the leading indicators. MBA's applications index was down 42% year over year as of October 30. MCT said September locks were down 27.25%. NAR's pending index hit 66.0 in September, a record low, and October fell further.

His point is that policymakers look at closings and existing sales, which lag. "When apps disappear, locks follow. When locks disappear, closings follow." He says layoffs have started again.

Meanwhile the agencies are flush. Fannie earned $4.2 billion in Q3 and Freddie $3.2 billion, with combined net worth over $200 billion. FHA's fund holds $188.9 billion. Fannie said over 70% of Q3 g-fee income came from loans made in 2022 or earlier.

His asks: FHFA should recalibrate g-fees, which averaged 65.2 bps on 2024 acquisitions, and Congress should repeal the 10 bp surcharge from 2011, which he says has repaid $56 billion against a $30 billion cost.

Expand 3-minute read

Milam has been originating for 21 years. His piece is useful less for the outrage and more for the specific numbers and policy levers.

The cliff. Earlier this year his company was setting production records despite weak existing sales. It had grown, cut its cost to manufacture and invested in technology. Then October lock volume dropped 51% versus October 2025. "Trust me, we're not alone," he writes. The MBA Mortgage Applications Index was down 42% year over year as of October 30. Mortgage Capital Trading reported September locks down 27.25% year over year. NAR's September Pending Home Sales Index fell to 66.0, the lowest since the series began in 2001. Since he wrote, NAR's October pending sales fell another 4.7%, down 16.7% from a year ago, another record low.

Why lag matters. MCT and NAR figures reflect September. "By the time those reports reach policymakers, lenders have already experienced October's deterioration and are projecting November." Housing begins with interest, credit pulls, applications, locks and contracts. "If you're looking at existing home sales or September's data, you're sitting comfortably in the caboose while the front of the train has gone over the edge." He says downsizing has begun again, with Q4 and Q1 the slowest seasons ahead, and calls this worse than the prior four years of housing recession.

The agencies' results. Fannie Mae earned $4.2 billion in Q3, lifting net worth to $119.6 billion. Freddie Mac earned $3.2 billion, with net worth at $81 billion. That is $7.4 billion in quarterly profits and more than $200 billion combined. FHA's MMI Fund held $188.9 billion with an 11.47% capital ratio in its latest annual report, more than five times the statutory minimum. (Note: Tuesday's brief cited a 10.29% ratio from a different FHA measure; check the source before quoting either on air.) Fannie said more than 70% of Q3 guarantee-fee income came from loans and securities issued in 2022 or earlier. Translation: the GSEs keep earning on old books even as new business shrinks.

The policy asks. In 2024, Fannie and Freddie collected an average of 65.2 bps in g-fees on new single-family acquisitions. Milam wants FHFA to weigh those fees against actual credit risk, capital needs and the earnings from existing guarantees. He also wants Congress to repeal the 10 bp g-fee surcharge imposed in 2011 to pay for a two-month payroll tax cut. CBO scored that cut at $30 billion, and he says $56 billion has been repaid. He also takes a shot at the PMI-cancellation push, saying in 21 years no client has asked him for help canceling PMI.

Why it matters to us. A g-fee cut of even 10 bps flows straight into rate. It is one of the few concrete affordability levers that does not depend on the Fed. It is also a clean, non-partisan point for the Wednesday show and for realtor partners: the fee on a conforming loan still includes a charge for a 2011 tax cut. Expect more IMB layoffs and consolidation headlines into Q1, which can mean more experienced LOs looking for a new home.

4) Construction slides toward COVID-era lows: permits at 1.295M, single-family permits 772K, and analysts doubt October's multifamily starts jump

So-what: Fewer homes started now means less new supply in 2027, but in the near term builders keep buying down rates to move inventory. That is a live competitor for your purchase clients.

1-minute read

Logan Mohtashami at HousingWire says permits are already at COVID-recession levels. October permits were 1.295M annualized, 7.4% below September and 8.7% below a year ago. Single-family permits fell 8.7% to 772K.

His reason is simple: "there simply isn't enough housing demand to build more homes" with rates near 7.5% and the Fed hiking. New home sales and starts are still near 2019 levels because big public builders are buying down rates. If they lose that ability, sales could break below 2019.

Multifamily is murkier. Census said 5-plus unit starts jumped 25.5% to 433K. Analyst Jay Parsons says builder "vibes" are the most bearish since about 2010 and questions the data. Cushman & Wakefield says pipelines shrank in over 70% of 90 markets.

Calculated Risk adds the architecture billings index at 44.9 in October, with multifamily billings below 50 in 49 of the last 51 months. That points to fewer apartment projects into 2027.

Apartment demand is strong, though. Vacancy fell to a three-year low of 8.6% and asking rents rose 2.3% to $1,950.

Expand 3-minute read

Yesterday's brief covered the headline starts number (1.309M). Today's angle is what the pipeline says about 2027 and how builders are behaving now.

Permits. From Census: total permits were 1,295,000 annualized in October, down 7.4% from a revised 1,398,000 in September and 8.7% below October 2025. Single-family authorizations were 772,000, 8.7% below September's 846,000. Units in buildings of five or more were 475,000. Logan writes that permits are "not only at levels last seen during the COVID-19 recession," but on pace to hit those lows if the trend continues. He notes this is happening even after Congress passed the ROAD to Housing Act this summer to spur building.

Why builder sentiment and new home sales disagree. Logan fielded a good question: why is builder confidence at a 14-year low (NAHB 25) while new home sales grew in recent months? His answer: NAHB's index tilts toward smaller builders, while big public builders are still buying down rates. New home sales, starts and permits all sit around 2019 levels. "If the big builders lose their ability to buy down rates, this sales data should eventually break below 2019 levels." With margins falling, he says "it doesn't look good for housing construction in 2027."

Multifamily. Census said starts in 5-plus unit buildings rose 25.5% to 433,000 annualized, 22% above a year earlier, while multifamily permits fell 5.2% to 475,000. Jay Parsons wrote that "the vibes among multifamily builders today are as bearish as I've seen since about 2010," citing the Fed's September hike and signs of more. He rejects the idea that this is just monthly noise and questions whether the Census survey captures multifamily starts well. Cushman & Wakefield's Q3 report said pipelines shrank in more than 70% of the 90 markets it tracks, with the steepest drops in former boom markets like Sarasota, Savannah and Austin. Only 25 markets grew, led by Palm Beach and the Bay Area.

Leading indicator. Bill McBride at Calculated Risk reports the AIA/Deltek Architecture Billings Index at 44.9 in October. Below 50 means billings are falling. Multifamily residential was 42.7, and multifamily billings have been below 50 for 49 of the last 51 months. The ABI leads construction by about 9 to 12 months, so McBride expects slower commercial and apartment investment into 2027. The West was close to flat at 49.8.

Rental demand is the twist. Cushman & Wakefield said apartment demand through September ran at the strongest pace since 2021. National vacancy fell to 8.6%, a three-year low. Net absorption was nearly 350,000 units, up 14%, while deliveries fell 25% to just over 250,000. Asking rents rose 2.3% to $1,950, the fastest since early 2023. The firm said higher rates and home prices "have widened the affordability gap between renting and owning."

Separately, HousingWire reported that Japanese-owned builders Daiwa House, Sekisui House and Sumitomo Forestry are cutting U.S. outlooks and focusing on synergies. Sumitomo said it has "not seen a recovery" in October orders and expects tough conditions through next spring.

For clients: new construction is where the payment deals are, because builders are still paying for rate buydowns. Rising rents also strengthen the rent-versus-own math for buyers who can qualify.

5) HELOCs are back: balances hit $481 billion in Q3, up 14% in a year, as owners tap equity instead of giving up low-rate first mortgages

So-what: Cash-out refi is mostly dead with rates near 7.5%. The equity conversation is now a second-lien conversation, and that is a product gap many brokers can fill.

1-minute read

The WSJ ran "These Charts Show How More People Are Pulling Cash Out of Their Homes." Its point: HELOCs are popular again as owners try to keep their low-rate first mortgages. Homeowners sit on nearly $36 trillion in equity.

The hard numbers come from the New York Fed's Q3 household debt report, per Wolf Street. HELOC balances rose 4.9% in Q3 and 14.0% year over year to $481 billion. That is up 52% since the 2021 low. Total mortgage balances rose to $13.37 trillion.

Credit still looks fine. The 90-day-plus delinquency rate was 1.13% on mortgages and 0.85% on HELOCs. New foreclosures were 61,900, low by historical standards.

Barry Ritholtz pushed back on the WSJ framing. He says mortgage debt and home values both rose about 14% since 2022, so loan-to-value is flat near 28%. More dollars, not more leverage.

Bottom line: the trend is real in dollars, but it is a product shift from cash-out refis, not a sign of reckless borrowing.

Expand 3-minute read

Two things are true at once here, and both help when you talk to clients.

The WSJ angle. Terell Wright and Veronica Dagher's chart piece (print A2 today) says home equity lines are "becoming popular again as people try to avoid giving up their low-rate primary mortgages," with American homeowners sitting on nearly $36 trillion in equity. Back in February, Dagher reported HELOC balances at $434 billion in Q4 2025, the 15th straight quarterly rise, and noted that approvals were not automatic. Some lenders denied applications for rentals or certain condos even with high credit scores.

The data. Wolf Richter's write-up of the New York Fed's Q3 2026 Household Debt and Credit Report: mortgage balances rose $257 billion in Q3 to $13.37 trillion, up 2.3% from a year ago. Part of that jump was catch-up from a servicing-transfer reporting gap in Q2. HELOC balances drawn rose 4.9% in the quarter and 14.0% year over year to $481 billion, up 52% since the 2021 low. Richter explains the math simply: if you have a big balance at 3%, you do not refinance the whole thing to pull cash; you add a smaller second lien.

Risk check. Richter's housing debt-to-income ratio (mortgages plus HELOCs over disposable income) rose to 57.2% in Q3, still among the lowest readings in the data. The 90-plus day delinquency rate edged up to 1.13% for mortgages and dipped to 0.85% for HELOCs, roughly 2018 to 2019 levels. New foreclosures rose to 61,900, at the low end of pre-pandemic years. He does flag big price declines in some markets, especially condos.

The pushback. Barry Ritholtz called the WSJ chart a "chart crime." His math: equity is about 72% of home value now, the same as the 2022 peak. Mortgage debt rose from about $12 trillion to $14 trillion since 2022, roughly 14%, and total housing value rose about the same. So the loan-to-value ratio is flat near 28%. "Show me that the percentage of home equity is increasing faster than the underlying value of homes, and you have something." Fair point: more HELOC dollars partly reflect bigger home values and fewer cash-out refis, not more risk.

Product note. Lenders are leaning in. Today's HousingWire Daily carried a sponsor note for a Deephaven HELOC with a $1M max, full doc and bank statement qualification, and non-warrantable condos allowed. That is relevant for self-employed borrowers who need equity but cannot or should not touch a low-rate first.

How to use it. For past clients with 2020 and 2021 loans, the best equity tool is usually a HELOC or closed-end second, not a cash-out refi. Run the blended-rate math for them. For the show, the honest framing is: more people are tapping equity, but leverage overall is not rising. That avoids the doom angle while still showing the opportunity.

6) Fix-and-flip index falls to 53, a near four-year low: 40% of flippers see lower prices and 1 in 5 are selling below their ARV

So-what: Investor and DSCR-adjacent borrowers are getting squeezed. Expect tighter lender underwriting on exit values and more caution on purchase prices.

1-minute read

The Burns + Kiavi Fix and Flip Market Index fell to 53 in Q3 from 59 in Q2 and 56 a year earlier. That is the lowest in nearly four years. Above 50 still means expansion, but barely.

Only 23% of the roughly 280 flippers surveyed expect good sales over the next six months, a four-year low. 19% expect poor sales, the most since Q4 2022.

Prices are the problem. 40% said sale prices in their markets are lower than a year ago, versus 16% higher. Southern California was at 50% lower, behind only the Southwest at 67%. About one in five are selling mostly below their after-repair value estimates.

Costs are up too. Average renovation spend was $72,000 per flip, and $133,000 in Southern California. 41% said immigration enforcement hurt their labor force, up from 33% in Q2 2025.

John Burns told lenders to underwrite to the expected sale price at exit, not recent comps, which lag in a falling market. Flippers now target a max purchase of 67% of ARV.

Expand 3-minute read

This survey matters for anyone doing investor loans, bridge or DSCR exits, because it shows the stress at the asset level before it shows up in delinquencies.

The index. The Burns + Kiavi Fix and Flip Market Index, built from a quarterly survey of about 280 investors, dropped to 53 in Q3 2026. It was 59 in Q2 and 56 a year earlier. The reading is the lowest in nearly four years. Investors blamed mortgage rates above 7%, softer prices and labor constraints.

Sentiment. Just 23% expect good sales over the next six months, the lowest share in four years, while 19% expect poor sales, the highest since Q4 2022. For the first time since Q3 2022, large flippers (eight or more homes bought or sold in 18 months) reported worse conditions than smaller operators. That is notable because the big operators usually have better data and financing.

Prices by region. 40% of flippers said home sale prices in their markets were lower than a year earlier, versus 16% higher and 36% flat. The Southwest led at 67% reporting lower prices, followed by Southern California at 50%. Texas and the Southeast were at 47%, and Florida at 44%. About one in five said they were selling mostly below their original after-repair value estimate, near the survey's high since 2021.

Costs and labor. The national average renovation cost per flip was $72,000 in Q3. Southern California was the most expensive at $133,000, then the Northeast at $86,000. The Southeast and Texas were near $47,000 and $46,000. 41% said increased immigration enforcement had hurt their labor force, up from 33% in Q2 2025, with the biggest effect in Florida and the Southeast. Flippers reported trouble finding workers, higher labor costs and longer timelines.

Some upside for buyers of distressed deals. 24% said competition for deals was lower than usual, the highest share in the survey's history. But investors are not chasing: the average maximum purchase price target fell to 67% of after-repair value.

Lender guidance. John Burns told flippers to stay disciplined and build in margin for price declines and labor costs. It told lenders to underwrite based on expected sale prices at disposition rather than recent closed comps, "which can lag in a declining market."

Context. This fits other recent price data. ResiClub counted 76 of 300 major metros with falling home prices this week, while national prices were still up about 0.7% year over year. Southern California shows up on both lists as a softer market. For flippers, that means the comps used at purchase may be higher than the price they get at sale six months later, which is exactly the lag John Burns is warning lenders about

What it means for BuyWise. If you place fix-and-flip or bridge loans, expect lenders to cut ARV-based leverage and stress exit values, especially in Southern California. For DSCR rental buyers, softer flip margins can mean better entry prices on finished product. And flippers who cannot sell may pivot to renting, which can create DSCR refinance demand on homes that did not hit their exit price.

7) 10 million sales studied: off-MLS private listings sold for $3,500 to $5,000 less, while coming-soon listings that later hit the MLS sold for more

So-what: Exposure wins. This is a useful, data-backed point for sellers and listing-agent partners as Compass pushes its private "See It First" model.

1-minute read

An ARELLO study of more than 10 million transactions from January 2024 to June 2026 split listings into three groups.

Coming-soon listings that were pre-marketed and then hit the MLS sold for about $9,200 more than comparable MLS listings in the mid-market and close to $17,000 more at the top end.

Listings that stayed private and off the MLS did worse. They sold about $5,000 below MLS in the mid-range and about $3,500 below at the high end.

The discount was bigger in some neighborhoods: about 0.88% below MLS in majority-white areas, 2.21% in majority non-white areas and 3.33% in majority-Hispanic areas. ARELLO said this does not prove discrimination but warrants more study.

Coming-soon volume grew 13% in the year to June 2026, while private listings fell 3%. HousingWire's columnist framed it as: "The premium belongs to exposure, not exclusivity." That contrasts with Compass's September launch of See It First, which showcases private and coming-soon listings.

Expand 3-minute read

This is an opinion-style HousingWire piece built on hard data, and it lands in the middle of the Clear Cooperation fight.

The setup. On September 22, Compass launched "See It First," the centerpiece of its biggest Compass.com redesign in a decade. It pulls coming-soon and private exclusive listings from its nine brands into one early-access showcase. Compass cited 56% year-over-year site traffic growth in August and a multimillion-dollar ad campaign.

The study. The Association of Real Estate License Law Officials (ARELLO) reviewed more than 10 million residential sales from January 2024 through June 2026. It separated two things that often get lumped together. Coming-soon listings, pre-marketed and then placed on the MLS, sold for about $9,200 more than comparable MLS listings in the mid-market and close to $17,000 more at the top. Listings that stayed private and off the MLS sold for roughly $5,000 less than MLS in the mid-range and about $3,500 less at the high end.

Equity angle. Private listings sold about 0.88% below MLS in majority-white neighborhoods, 2.21% below in majority non-white areas and 3.33% below in majority-Hispanic areas. ARELLO stressed these gaps "do not establish causation or unlawful discrimination" but warrant "further examination." The columnist's line: "A smaller audience is a smaller check, and the sellers with the least room to lose lost the most."

Trend. Coming-soon volume rose 13% in the year ending June 2026, while private listings fell 3%. So the market is already shifting toward the approach the data favors. Sellers and agents appear to be choosing pre-marketing that ends in a full MLS launch over listings that never go public.

Compass's side. CEO Robert Reffkin has called the Clear Cooperation Policy "anti-competitive and anti-consumer" and said Compass "has not and will not adhere" to it. The column gives Compass credit for building a real consumer destination, keeping listing agents front and center, and making pre-marketing easy. It argues the coming-soon half is what the data rewards, while the gated half is where sellers should slow down.

Related MLS news. HousingWire also reported that MGMLS will merge into FMLS, adding 12 compulsory counties in central Georgia. Closing date and terms were not disclosed. Consolidation like this widens exposure for listings in those counties, the same direction the ARELLO data favors

Why it matters to a broker. Appraisals and comps work best with open market data, and price discovery helps buyers too. For seller clients who are also buying, an extra $5,000 to $17,000 at sale can matter for a down payment or closing costs. For referral partners, this is a neutral, data-first talking point: pre-market if you want, but get full MLS exposure. It also fits the larger theme that Compass is litigating and lobbying hard on listing access, so expect more fights over who controls listing data.

The jump. In ALTA's 2024 survey, 28% of title firms reported at least one seller impersonation attempt in the prior calendar year. In the latest study, that figure is 59%. The author calls it "an attack on property rights and on what is, for many Americans, their greatest source of wealth."

How the scams are evolving. Title professionals are seeing spoofed contact information, deepfake voice and image technology, and more sophisticated identity tactics. Targets have broadened from vacant land to vacation homes, rental properties, agricultural land and even primary residences. Fraudsters favor absentee owners, properties owned free and clear, and properties tied to recently deceased owners. Those are exactly the properties where the real owner is least likely to notice quickly.

Defenses. 94% of firms use multiple tools, citing an average of 5.3 as helpful. The list includes identity verification systems, direct contact with the seller, multifactor authentication, approved notaries and knowledge-based authentication. The column's main lesson is that prevention is layered and depends on experienced people who notice when something "does not fit." In the survey, the clearance or curative process before closing was the most common point where fraud was caught.

The policy subtext. ALTA notes the inflation-adjusted cost of title insurance fell 32% between 2016 and 2024. Read that as a warning against cutting title steps in the name of lower closing costs.

The column's call to action. The author argues that much of the title industry's prevention work is "invisible when it succeeds": a suspicious file is questioned, an identity is independently checked, and a closing is stopped before money moves. It asks lenders, real estate agents and title companies to repeat the same fraud-awareness message to consumers, technology vendors to keep building defenses, and law enforcement to prosecute the people behind these schemes. It also asks policymakers to protect consumers without stripping out steps that keep fraudulent sales from closing

What to do on our side. This also ties into the broader AI and cyber theme this week, with the tech outages and FBI alert on an AI-related exploit. Practical steps: verify seller identity through an independent channel, not contact info provided in the file. Be cautious with free-and-clear properties, remote sellers who only want e-mail or text, rushed timelines and below-market pricing. For purchase clients, remind them that wire instructions only come from verified contacts. For the show, it is a useful consumer tip: owners of rentals, second homes or vacant lots can sign up for county recorder alerts where available.
