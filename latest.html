1) Hot October CPI knocks UMBS 6.0 down about 0.55 to 99.50 and pushes the 10-year to 4.98%

Wed show

So-what: Expect worse rate sheets and possible mid-day reprices today. Anyone close to locking should lock.

1-minute read

October CPI came in hot across the board. Headline CPI rose 0.4% for the month and core rose 0.3%, each a tenth above forecasts. MBS Live put core at 0.296% against a 0.22% forecast, and supercore, which is services without housing, at 0.399%.

Bonds sold off right away. In MBS Live's 9:38 AM ET Morning update, UMBS 6.0 was at 99.50, down 0.55, and the 10-year was at 4.977%. A 6:49 AM PT alert showed 99.48, down 0.57. Monday closed at 100.09 and 4.879%.

Short-term yields took the biggest hit. The 2-year jumped about 15 basis points to 4.719%. That is the market pricing a bigger chance of a Fed hike in December.

Matt Graham's headline says it plainly: no 'yeah buts' in this report, and it is all bad for bonds. He said a real reversal would take a big surprise headline or a large stock selloff.

There is a 10-year Treasury auction at 10 AM PT today, which can add more movement.

For clients: half a point in MBS price usually means roughly an eighth worse in rate or extra cost for the same rate. Lock anything that cannot handle more of this.

This is the morning's main event, so here is the full picture.

The data. The Bureau of Labor Statistics said headline CPI rose 0.4% in October and core CPI, which leaves out food and energy, rose 0.3%. Both were one tenth above what economists expected. MBS Live's calendar shows a 0.3% headline forecast against 0.6% in September, and a 0.2% core forecast against 0.3% the month before. Unrounded, core was 0.296%. Supercore, which is services without housing and is a number the Fed watches closely, rose 0.399%. Because of last year's government shutdown there is no official year-over-year CPI this month. MBS Live estimated core at about 2.8% year over year, against a 2.7% forecast and 2.6% the month before, using an interpolated base, so treat that as an estimate.

What drove it. Peter Boockvar listed the details. Energy rose 0.7% for the month, with gasoline up 1%, fuel oil up 2% and electricity up 0.9%. Food at home rose 0.4% and food away from home rose 0.5%. Services excluding energy rose 0.3%. Owners' equivalent rent, the biggest housing piece of CPI, rose 0.3%, while rent of primary residence rose 0.2%, which Boockvar says is close to what is happening in real rents. Airfares rose 0.4% after big gains in prior months. Hotels rose 0.7%. Vehicle maintenance rose 0.6% and car insurance bounced 0.7%. Health insurance fell 1.8%, which Boockvar says reflects BLS methodology rather than real life. In other words, the one category that helped the number may not be real.

Graham's read. He said some inflation reports have counterpoints even when they look high at first glance. This one does not. The only possible silver lining is that a few categories moved a hair lower than last month, but last month was very high, so that is not much comfort.

How bonds reacted. The hit was immediate and biggest in short-term yields. By the 9:38 AM ET update, the 2-year was at 4.719%, up about 15 basis points. The 5-year was 4.802%, the 10-year 4.977% and the 30-year 5.334%. On the mortgage side, UMBS 6.0 was 99.50, down 0.55. UMBS 6.5 was 101.36, down 0.36. GNMA 6.0 was 99.93, down 0.48. The 15-year UMBS 5.0 was 98.16, down 0.56. Before the report, at 4:30 AM PT, the 10-year was 4.890%, only slightly higher than Monday's close.

The technical map. Monday's MBS Live Recap listed 4.93% and 5.00% as the next ceilings on the 10-year. This morning's 4.977% is already through 4.93% and close to 5.00%. A close above 5.00% would undo much of the rally of the last few weeks.

Monday's context. Monday's Recap was titled Bonds Looking Increasingly Comfortable With Recent Gains, and the lock note said risk-tolerant clients had no reason to second-guess floating, but that CPI brought data-driven risk. That risk showed up.

Today's calendar. The 10-year note auction is at 1 PM ET, which is 10 AM PT. Weak demand there would add pressure. Chrisman Commentary notes the bond market is closed Wednesday for Veterans Day, so whatever happens today could carry into Thursday.

What to do. Lock anyone closing within 30 days. For clients who were floating, call them this morning and show the change. Many lenders will reprice worse today. For the Wednesday show, this is the lead: inflation came in hot, the Fed is more likely to raise rates, and mortgage rates moved up. The message to buyers is to watch payment, not the headline rate.

2) Waller floats a 50 basis point December hike, and markets now price a bit more than one full hike

Wed show

So-what: The Fed debate moved from whether to hike in December to how much. That keeps short rates and ARM pricing under pressure.

1-minute read

Fed Governor Chris Waller spoke just before CPI. He said he leans toward a 25 basis point hike in December even if October inflation improves. But if core CPI came in around 0.3% or higher, he said a 50 basis point hike should be considered, and that only 25 would put Fed credibility at risk.

Core came in at 0.296%. MBS Live says markets now price a bit more than one full hike for December. Boockvar titled his note 'now fully priced for December rate hike.'

Boockvar notes Waller said nothing about the AI selloff, which Chair Kevin Warsh flagged at the last meeting. Boockvar argues the Fed has to watch AI spending closely because it drives jobs, inflation and stocks.

Other data: the NY Fed's survey held one-year inflation expectations at 3.9%. Expected chances of missing a debt payment rose 1.6 points to 13.8%. Dallas Fed bankers reported tighter credit and higher loan pricing.

For clients: ARMs and HELOCs tied to short rates are the most exposed. Fixed rates follow the 10-year.

Expand 3-minute read

Waller set the stage for this morning's move, so his words matter.

What he said. In remarks released about two hours before CPI, Waller said, 'Let's just say it. September's inflation data were bad. There was no silver lining.' He said he was hoping for improvement in October, but even if that happened he would still support a 25 basis point increase at the December meeting. Then the key line: if October core CPI was similar to the past two months, around 0.3% or higher, then 'a 50 basis point hike in the policy rate should be considered at the December meeting. Hiking only 25 basis points would put our credibility at risk, a risk I am not eager to take.' He said he would also watch whether prices of high-tech goods tied to the AI build-out keep rising quickly, and whether the energy price run-up is passing through to other goods and services.

The result. Core came in at 0.296%, which rounds to 0.3%. That is right on Waller's trigger. MBS Live said December rate expectations jumped in two phases, first on Waller and then on CPI, and markets now price a bit more than one full 25 basis point hike. Last week's brief carried December hike odds around 68% to 80% depending on the source, so this is a clear step up.

Two views at the Fed. Boockvar points out that Chair Warsh and Waller do not see eye to eye on forward guidance. Waller tends to share his views right before data. Waller's speech did not mention the AI trade, the rising cost of financing it or the uncertain pace of data center building. Boockvar says that seems to be mainly Warsh's focus, as Warsh said at the last press conference. Boockvar calls himself an inflation hawk but says the Fed has to watch AI closely because of its weight in the economy and the stock market. Separately, Chrisman Commentary reports that Warsh plans to move to fewer rate-setting meetings starting in 2027, with two of eight meetings used for broader strategy by 2028.

Consumers and credit. The NY Fed's Consumer Expectations Survey held one-year inflation expectations at 3.9%. Expectations of higher unemployment reached the highest level since April 2020, though job-finding expectations rose to their highest since November 2025. The average perceived chance of missing a minimum debt payment in the next three months rose 1.6 points to 13.8%, the highest since December 2025. The Dallas Fed Banking Survey said loan volume kept growing, credit standards tightened, loan pricing rose broadly, and loan performance weakened slightly. Bankers expect business activity to decline six months out.

Small relief. Boockvar notes the average gallon of gas fell below $4 on Monday for the first time since mid-July, at $3.98, though still up from $3.07 a year ago.

What it means for us. The Fed sets short rates. A bigger December hike mostly hits products tied to short rates: ARMs at reset, HELOCs, and bridge or construction lines. Fixed mortgage rates follow the 10-year and spreads, and the 10-year moved up less than the 2-year today. That is why the yield curve flattened. For clients with ARMs or HELOCs, this is a good time to review payment risk. For the show, a clean message: the Fed is fighting inflation, and that is why rates are not falling as fast as people hoped.

3) Zillow: October pending sales fell 17% from a year ago as payments ran nearly 13% higher

Wed show

So-what: Demand is soft, which gives buyers who can still qualify more room to negotiate on price and terms.

1-minute read

Zillow's October report shows the sharpest annual drop in pending sales in more than three years. Newly pending sales fell 17% year over year and existing-home sales fell nearly 9%.

The cause is payment. The typical mortgage payment was nearly 13% higher than a year ago, and Zillow says that is almost entirely due to higher rates. Freddie Mac rates averaged 7.33% in October versus 6.86% in September.

Prices are flat. Typical home values were up only 0.7% from a year ago and down 0.6% from September. Homes took a median 31 days to go pending, two days longer than a year ago.

Affordability got worse. A median-income household would need 34.7% of monthly income for the typical payment with 20% down, including taxes, maintenance and insurance. That compares with 32.7% a year ago.

Zillow's chief economist Mischa Fisher said there are deals for buyers who can brave the affordability headwinds. MBA purchase demand has run about 10% below last year since late August.

For clients: slow markets are when buyers get concessions. Ask for seller credits toward a buydown.

Expand 3-minute read

This is a clean data point for the show and for buyer conversations.

The numbers. Scotsman Guide's write-up of Zillow's October market report says homebuying demand kept sliding. Newly pending sales fell 17% from a year ago, the sharpest annual decline in more than three years. Existing-home sales fell nearly 9% year over year. Homes took a median of 31 days to go pending, two days longer than a year ago and two days longer than September.

Why. Zillow says higher monthly costs are almost exclusively due to higher rates. The average mortgage payment was nearly 13% higher than a year ago. Freddie Mac's 30-year averaged 7.33% in October, up from 6.86% in September. Rates have been above 7% since the third week of September, averaged 7.4% in the first half of October, and eased to 7.18% by early November. Insurance and taxes added to the squeeze.

Affordability. Zillow estimates a household earning the median income would spend 34.7% of monthly income on the typical payment for a median-priced home with 20% down, including taxes, maintenance and insurance. That is up from 32.7% a year ago and 33.5% in September. A common rule of thumb is that housing above 30% of income is a stretch, so the typical buyer is now well past that line.

Prices. Typical home values were only 0.7% higher than a year ago and fell 0.6% from September. That fits other data this week. ICE's home price index was up 1.0% year over year in October. Prices are not crashing. They are flat, and falling in some Western and Southern markets.

The outlook. Fisher said at the end of the third quarter that he expects monthly home sales to show annual declines through year-end. He also said there are deals for buyers who can handle the affordability headwinds. MBA data show purchase applications have been about 10% below last year since late August.

What this means for buyers. Slow markets shift leverage to buyers. Sellers who need to move are more open to price cuts, credits and repairs. A seller credit used for a temporary or permanent buydown can lower the payment more than a similar price cut. With today's CPI pushing rates up again, that conversation matters even more.

For the show. A simple storyline: payments are up 13% in a year because of rates, not prices. Demand fell 17%. That means less competition and more negotiating room for buyers who are ready. It also sets up Thursday's existing-home sales report, which Chrisman Commentary says is expected to fall about 5%.

A California note. Zillow's national numbers hide big local gaps. In high-cost California markets, a 13% payment increase is a much bigger dollar amount, so the pullback can be sharper. Buyers here should run payment scenarios at today's rates, not last month's.

What to watch. Existing-home sales this week, and whether today's CPI move pushes rates back toward the October highs.

Expand 3-minute read

Calculated Risk posted the key findings of ICE's November Mortgage Monitor. Here is what matters for a California broker.

Equity. Mortgage holders collectively held $17.5 trillion in equity in the third quarter, up 1.4% from a year ago and a record for a third quarter. Tappable equity, which is what a borrower can access while keeping 20% equity, rose 1.2% to an average of $204,000 per borrower, up $16,600 from a year earlier.

Equity millionaires. 2.4 million mortgage holders had at least $1 million in equity, up 6% year over year. Nearly 9 million held $500,000 or more, up 4%. California accounted for 49% of the nation's equity millionaires. New York had 189,000, New Jersey 141,000 and Florida 104,000. That makes California the national center of this opportunity.

Who is borrowing and how. Andy Walden of ICE said cash-outs are a bigger share of refinances in a higher-rate world, and those borrowers are giving up low rates and taking higher payments to tap equity. Cash-outs were about 1 in 7 rate locks in recent weeks, and purchase loans were roughly 80% of locks. ARMs accounted for more than 10% of early October locks as 30-year fixed rates rose above 7%. The points-adjusted spread between 5/1 ARMs and 30-year fixed loans widened from about 75 basis points over the summer to more than 100 basis points in early October. In plain terms, ARMs got cheaper relative to fixed loans, so more people chose them.

Home prices. ICE's Home Price Index was up 1.0% year over year in October, holding steady after reaching 1.4% earlier this year. Single-family prices rose 1.3% while condo prices fell 1.2%, and single-family beat condos in 98 of the 100 largest markets. The Northeast and Midwest had 22 of the 25 fastest-appreciating markets, led by Scranton at 7.1% and Rochester at 6.0%. The biggest declines were in the West and South: Seattle down 4.0%, Austin down 2.4%, San Antonio down 2.2%, Denver down 2.1% and Las Vegas down 2.0%.

Delinquencies. The national delinquency rate rose 12 basis points to 3.66% in September, roughly in line with seasonal norms. It is up 24 basis points from a year ago but below every pre-pandemic September on record. Loans 30 days late rose 49,000 to 1,114,000. Loans 90 or more days late but not in foreclosure rose to 579,000, up 21% from a year ago. The serious delinquency rate was 1.04%, in line with the 2017 to 2019 September average of 1.06%. FHA's seriously past-due share fell for a second straight quarter, while VA rose 9% and GSE loans 6%.

Why it matters. Most homeowners have a low first-mortgage rate and lots of equity. A cash-out refinance at 7% replaces that low rate on the whole balance. A closed-end second or a HELOC only prices the new money. For many clients that is the better math. For self-employed borrowers, bank-statement second liens may also fit.

For the show. California owners hold half the nation's equity millionaires. That is a strong hook for a segment on using equity without refinancing the first mortgage.

5) Servicers kept only 27% of refinancing borrowers in Q3, and just 15% of older cash-out borrowers

So-what: Servicers are losing equity-driven borrowers. Brokers who reach those homeowners first win the loan.

1-minute read

ICE data reported by Scotsman Guide shows servicers retained 27% of refinancing borrowers in the third quarter, down four points from the prior quarter.

Rate-and-term retention fell nine points to 30%. Cash-out retention was flat at 26%, but servicers kept only about 15% of cash-out borrowers whose loans were made in 2019 or earlier.

Rate-and-term refinance prepayments have fallen more than 80% from four-year highs in March. Prepayments overall hit their lowest monthly reading since early 2025 as September rates averaged 6.86%.

ICE's Bob Hart said rate-based retention does not work for borrowers motivated by equity rather than rate savings.

The lock-in effect is slowly fading. 49% of active mortgages had rates below 4% at the end of Q3, down from 52% a year ago and the first time under half since Q2 2020. More than 1 in 5 loans are under 3%.

For brokers: the opportunity is equity conversations with past clients and agent referrals, not rate-drop alerts.

Expand 3-minute read

This is the business-development side of the ICE report.

Retention is slipping. According to ICE Mortgage Technology data reported by Scotsman Guide, servicers retained 27% of refinancing borrowers in the third quarter, down four percentage points from the second quarter. Retention among rate-and-term refinancers fell nine points to 30%. Cash-out retention was flat at 26%. But among cash-out borrowers whose loans were originated in 2019 or earlier, servicers kept only about 15%. The ICE Mortgage Monitor adds that cash-out refinances made up 75% of all refinance-portfolio attrition.

Why. Bob Hart, president of ICE's mortgage technology division, said traditional rate-based retention strategies are not effective for borrowers who want access to equity rather than a lower rate. Most servicer recapture systems are built to send an offer when rates drop. These borrowers are not waiting for a rate drop. They want cash for remodeling, debt payoff or another purpose. Hart said servicers need better visibility into equity positions and must engage borrowers before they look elsewhere.

The market backdrop. As 30-year rates rose to an average of 6.86% in September per Freddie Mac, prepayment activity fell to its lowest monthly reading since early 2025. Rate-and-term refinance prepayments plunged more than 80% from the four-year highs set in March. Scotsman Guide says rates are broadly expected to stay above 7% next year, which would mean even lower refinance volume than 2026. Some experts have flagged liquidity concerns for lenders heading into next year.

The lock-in effect. The share of active mortgages with rates below 4% fell to 49% at the end of the third quarter, down from 52% a year ago. It is the first time below half since the second quarter of 2020. More than 1 in 5 loans still have a rate under 3%. There were 1.9 million fewer sub-4% mortgages than a year earlier. ICE says runoff remains slow. If 2026 patterns held, sub-4% loans would still be about 45% of active mortgages next year and about one-third in five years.

What it means for a broker shop. Your edge is the relationship, not the servicer's database. Past clients with older loans and high equity are the most likely to leave their servicer, and the servicer is unlikely to catch them. Practical steps: sort your past-client list by loan year and estimated equity, start equity-check conversations, and pitch second liens where the first-mortgage rate is low. Pair this with the ICE equity data: average tappable equity is $204,000 per borrower.

A note on today's market. With CPI pushing rates up again this morning, rate-and-term refinances will stay scarce. That makes equity-driven loans an even bigger share of refinance volume, and it raises the value of reaching those borrowers before their servicer does.

For the show. A useful buyer-side angle: about half of owners have a rate under 4%, which is why inventory stays tight. But that share is shrinking every year, which slowly frees up supply.

It found support in Los Angeles and San Francisco but lost statewide. Orange County voted no by a wide margin.

More than $400 million was spent on Prop. 40 and two competing measures, with more than 90% spent opposing the tax. Google co-founders Sergey Brin and Larry Page moved out of California because of the proposal, the Times reports.

This is a California story with a housing and tax angle.

The result. According to the Los Angeles Times, Proposition 40 was officially rejected Monday night. Roughly 53% of voters said no and 47% said yes, per the Associated Press call. The measure had trailed from the first day of vote counting.

What it would have done. The measure sought a one-time 5% tax on California billionaires' assets, to offset federal healthcare funding cuts. Supporters said about 90% of the money would go to healthcare, with the rest to schools and food assistance. The state has more than 200 billionaires.

Where it won and lost. The late tally showed major pockets of support in Los Angeles and San Francisco, which are home to most of the state's billionaires. Orange County voted no by a wide margin. It lost in the vast majority of counties.

The money. The political battle over Prop. 40 and two competing measures was among the most expensive in California history. More than $400 million was spent on the three related measures, with more than 90% spent opposing the billionaire tax. Billionaires, including Google co-founder Sergey Brin, put hundreds of millions of dollars into groups that ran ads against it. The Times reports that Brin and fellow co-founder Larry Page moved out of California because of the proposal.

Who opposed it. Gov. Gavin Newsom, the California Teachers Assn. and Planned Parenthood Affiliates of California were among Democrats and Democrat-aligned groups that warned billionaires would flee. Ads featured teachers, firefighters and other middle-class Californians and argued the tax would hit everyone. After the result, a coalition including the California Medical Assn. and the California School Boards Assn. said the measure risked the state's competitiveness and fiscal stability.

What comes next. Supporters say this is not the end. Sen. Bernie Sanders and Rep. Ro Khanna, who both backed Prop. 40, are pushing a federal 5% tax on billionaires, the Make Billionaires Pay Their Fair Share Act. With Democrats winning control of the House and Senate, it may get a hearing, but the Times says it would likely face constitutional challenges if it passed.

Why it matters to a mortgage business. High earners and wealthy owners anchor coastal California's luxury, jumbo and investor markets. A wealth tax that pushed some of them out would have hit those segments and local tax revenue. That risk is off the table for now. It also keeps California's tax story focused on income tax and property tax, which are the numbers that matter most in underwriting.

Other ballot results. This brief covered Props 1 and 37 passing last week. Prop. 40 was the last major California tax measure still being counted. Reason reports that Prop. 42, which would bar new state taxes on personal property, was expected to pass, so watch for its final count.

For the show. The audience is buyers, so this is a light mention, not a lead. It is useful context for anyone worried about California's direction.

9) Massachusetts narrowly rejects Question 7, which would have allowed single-family homes on 5,000-square-foot lots

So-what: Statewide zoning reform for starter homes came within about 32,000 votes. Expect copycat efforts, including in California.

1-minute read

Massachusetts voters narrowly rejected Question 7, about 50.6% to 49.4% per Boston news reports. HousingWire says the gap was roughly 32,000 votes. It was the first statewide ballot measure of its kind.

It would have required towns to allow single-family homes on lots of at least 5,000 square feet, about 0.12 acres, with 50 feet of frontage and public water and sewer. Many towns now require one-half to two acres.

It was the closest of nine statewide questions. The AP called it Friday, nearly three days after the election.

Local government groups opposed it as top-down. Supporters called the close loss a win and pointed to voter confusion.

Boston voted strongly yes even though it was exempt. Rural towns on well and septic were also outside the rule but voted no.

For us: lot-size rules are a quiet driver of home prices. This shows voters are close to accepting smaller lots for starter homes.

Expand 3-minute read

This is a supply-side story with national reach.

The measure. Question 7 would have required Massachusetts cities and towns to allow single-family homes on residentially zoned lots of at least 5,000 square feet, with at least 50 feet of street frontage and access to public sewer and water. In effect, the state would have overridden local rules requiring larger lots. Towns could still set reasonable rules on setbacks, height, bulk and short-term rentals. HousingWire says many localities zone for one-half to two acres per single-family home.

The vote. It failed narrowly, about 50.6% to 49.4% according to Boston news reports. HousingWire says the gap was roughly 32,000 votes, the smallest margin of the nine statewide questions. The AP did not call it until Friday, nearly three days after the election.

Who fought it. Local government organizations argued it was a top-down approach that took away local control. The Massachusetts Municipal Association's CEO, Adam Chapdelaine, thanked voters for recognizing it was not a workable way to create entry-level homes.

The supporters' read. Jonathan Berk of Abundant Housing Massachusetts said that six years ago he would not have believed a statewide zoning question would lose by less than one point. He points to voter confusion. Boston was exempt but voted strongly in favor. Rural towns on well water and septic were also exempt but voted against. Berk thinks many rural voters believed they were voting on something that applied to Boston.

Boston context. HousingWire Intelligence shows Boston's median rent at $3,900 and median list price at $890,000, which puts it in the same tier as California cities. Berk says Boston's zoning code is the longest in the country and 99% of its residential land is 'existing non-conforming.'

Why it matters here. Lot size is one of the biggest drivers of what a new home costs. Land is a large part of the price, and minimum lot rules force builders to buy more of it. California has already moved on lot splits and ADUs, so this is a reminder that starter-home supply depends on local rules, not just rates.

The bigger lesson. Supporters now have a town-by-town map of where small-lot zoning has voter support. Berk hopes the close result gives Boston Mayor Michelle Wu cover to pursue bigger zoning changes, including smaller single-family lots and modest middle housing such as townhomes and duplexes. Boston planning chief Kairos Shen pushed back at an October forum, saying building in the city should be hard and that his job is to hold standards. That is the tension in almost every high-cost metro, including Southern California: local control versus more homes.

For the show. A good explainer: why are there so few starter homes? One reason is that many towns ban small lots. Massachusetts almost changed that statewide.
