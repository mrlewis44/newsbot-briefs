1) Bonds hold most of the rally: UMBS 6.0 at 99.08 (-0.01) and the 10-year at 5.084% after the best week since February

So-what: Pricing is near the best levels in weeks, but MBS Live says the short-covering fuel is spent. Without a new tech scare or weak data, the next move is more likely sideways or a little weaker.

1-minute read

Bonds are mostly holding on to the big two-day rally this morning.

MBS Live's Morning update, sent at about 7:06 AM PT, had UMBS 6.0 at 99.08, down just 0.01. The 10-year Treasury was 5.084%, roughly flat. Thursday's recap had UMBS 6.0 closing at 98.98, up 0.38, with the 10-year at 5.090%, down about 10 basis points.

The Wall Street Journal called Thursday's 10-year drop the largest one-day decline in more than a year. The 10-year is down about 12.6 basis points this week, its best week since the one ending Feb. 13, before the Iran war began.

Matt Graham's read: tech stocks slid about 5% over the same stretch that the 10-year fell from 5.34% to 5.07%. That is not a coincidence. But the wave of short covering and bargain buying in bonds has run its course. It would take a fresh tech rout or weak economic data to push rates lower from here.

Today's reports, import prices and industrial production, are not big enough to do that. Graham calls mild morning weakness "entirely forgivable." In plain terms, holding these gains is a win.

The story this morning is less about a new move and more about whether the market can keep what it just gained.

Start with the overnight path. MBS Live's 4:30 AM PT alert had the 10-year at 5.111%, up 3.3 basis points. By 5:33 AM, UMBS 6.0 had slipped to 98.91, down 0.19 from the prior close, after starting the day at 99.09. Then bonds steadied. By the 7:06 AM Morning update, UMBS 6.0 was 99.08, down 0.01. On the Treasury side, the 10-year was 5.084%, the 2-year 4.723%, the 5-year 4.884% and the 30-year 5.425%.

Now the week. Thursday's MBS Live recap said the past five days were the best five days for rates "in a long time," and more surprising because two of those days brought much hotter inflation data. The WSJ said the 10-year's Thursday drop of about 10 basis points was its largest one-day decline in more than a year, and the weekly drop of about 12.6 basis points is the biggest since the week ending Feb. 13.

Why did it happen? Graham points to two forces. One is the AI and tech correction, led by CoreWeave, with the word "contagion" being thrown around. Money moved out of stocks, and possibly out of AI corporate debt, into safer bonds. The other is seller exhaustion. Graham has argued that 5.34% to 5.35% on the 10-year may be 2026's version of 2023's 5.00% peak, a level where value buyers finally step in.

HousingWire's Logan Mohtashami makes the same point from a chart angle. The 10-year tested 5.35% several times over the last month and it held. He says if that level had broken, the 10-year could have run toward 6.41%, and mortgage rates would easily be over 8%. For a rally with staying power, he wants to see the 10-year below 4.70% and the conflict ending. Peter Boockvar wrote this morning that he still thinks the short-term peak in long rates is in, while the longer-term backdrop is still rising rates.

Here is the caution. Graham calls the rally possibly "act 1" of a bigger correction, but it could also be "a short story that ends here." If AI fears fade, bonds lose a big source of support. He says the only surefire path to a bigger rally is a sustained economic slowdown, ideally alongside the war ending and oil falling.

MBS Live's lock and float notes say the tech correction is no bigger than it was in June, so it is too soon to call this a major shift. Still, demand at the long end gives a bit more room for judgment on near-term float decisions.

Technical levels to watch on the 10-year: ceilings at 5.34% and 5.41%, which MBS Live suggests as lock triggers, and floors at 5.27%, 5.14%, 5.00%, 4.93% and 4.83%. In plain terms, a close back above 5.34% would be the warning sign that this break is over.

Freddie Mac's weekly rate survey finally stopped climbing.

The 30-year fixed averaged 7.40% for the week ending Oct. 15, unchanged from last week. A year ago it was 6.27%. The 15-year fixed averaged 6.71%, down from 6.73%. A year ago it was 5.52%.

Freddie Mac's Primary Mortgage Market Survey is the rate most consumers and reporters quote, so it is worth knowing exactly what this week's number does and does not say.

The headline: the 30-year fixed averaged 7.40% as of Oct. 15, unchanged from the prior week. The 15-year fixed averaged 6.71%, down two basis points from 6.73%. One year ago the 30-year was 6.27% and the 15-year was 5.52%. So rates are more than a full percentage point above last year on both terms.

The streak: according to Mortgage Nuggets, the 30-year average had risen for seven straight weeks before this flat reading. Over the past seven months, rates have climbed alongside higher oil prices tied to the Iran war and a broad bond selloff. The newsletter notes the 30-year averaged 5.98% the week before the conflict began. AP described the level as the highest in nearly three years.

How the survey works: Freddie Mac uses applications submitted through its Loan Product Advisor system, not lender quotes. The rate reflects purchase loans with strong credit and 20% down on single-family, owner-occupied homes. Results come out Thursdays at noon Eastern, and they average applications from the prior Thursday through Wednesday. That is the key detail this week. Thursday's bond rally, when the 10-year fell about 10 basis points and UMBS 6.0 gained 0.38, happened after the survey window closed.

Other rate gauges help fill in the picture. Mortgage News Daily's daily index had the 30-year at 7.33% on Oct. 15, according to figures NAR cited. The same data put a 7/6 SOFR adjustable-rate mortgage at 6.79%, about 0.54 percentage point below the fixed rate. That spread helps explain why ARM applications reached 10.5% of all applications in MBA's latest weekly survey, the highest share in a year.

What buyers feel is the payment. NAR's math on a $300,000 mortgage puts principal and interest at about $2,075 a month at 7.4%, versus about $1,853 at 6.27% a year ago. That gap of about $222 a month is roughly $2,660 a year, before taxes and insurance.

NAR also passed along a warning from MBA's convention panels. Buyers who stretched their budgets on the "marry the house, date the rate" idea, expecting to refinance soon, are now carrying higher payments for longer than they planned.

How to use this with clients: the published 7.40% is a lagging average. Thursday's and Friday's pricing reflects a better bond market, so a borrower who checked rates last week may see a modest improvement now. But the improvement depends on bonds holding their gains, which MBS Live says is not guaranteed. A clean way to say it on a call: the survey stopped going up, live pricing is a little better, and next Thursday's number will tell us if the break is real.

3) Warsh won't tip his hand, and October hike odds fall to about 15% from 28% a day earlier

So-what: A likely pause on Oct. 28 removes one near-term scare for rates. December is still priced for a hike, so this is breathing room, not relief.

1-minute read

Speaking Friday in Bangkok at the IMF and World Bank meetings, Warsh said, "I don't prejudge meetings." He added that it is "a new chapter at the Fed" and the Fed is "not in that business anymore," meaning forward guidance.

Traders still moved. The WSJ reported CME odds of an October hike fell to 15%, from 28% a day earlier. Peter Boockvar put October at 14% and December at 88%. Goldman Sachs economists wrote that Warsh's comments, alongside other Fed officials, indicate a pause in October, with a "second and final hike" in December.

The Fed raised its target range to 3.75% to 4% last month, its first hike since 2023.

Warsh also said central banks around the world are in a "synchronous tightening," with "no free riders." He was optimistic about AI making economies more productive, but warned the path will be "very bumpy."

For mortgage rates, an October pause takes away one source of fresh selling. But with December still priced for a hike, short-term rates are not heading lower soon.

Expand 3-minute read

The Fed's next meeting is Oct. 27-28, and until this week, a second straight hike looked like a real possibility. Hot September CPI and PPI pushed those odds up. Warsh's Friday remarks pulled them back down, even though he said almost nothing about policy directly.

Here is what he said. In a fireside chat with IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva in Bangkok, Warsh said, "I don't prejudge meetings. I don't believe in saying now what I might do at the meetings that we have in the balance of this year." He framed that as a deliberate break from forward guidance, the practice of telling markets in advance what the Fed is likely to do. "It's a new chapter at the Fed [and] we're not in that business anymore," he said. He acknowledged that might disappoint people who wanted a steer.

Why did markets read that as a pause? Because the bar for a hike in two weeks is high, and the chair did not use the platform to prepare markets for one. Goldman Sachs economists led by Jan Hatzius wrote that, alongside comments from other Fed officials, Warsh's remarks indicate the FOMC will pause in October. They still expect a second and final hike in December.

The pricing moved quickly. The WSJ reported CME futures put the chance of an October hike at 15%, down from 28% a day earlier, with the next hike now priced for December. Boockvar's numbers were similar: 14% for October and 88% for December. Highway.ai's morning note said the same thing in plain words: the market sees Warsh's comments, or lack of them, as a Fed pause on Oct. 28.

Context on where the Fed stands: it raised the federal funds target range to 3.75% to 4% in September. That was the first hike since 2023. The inflation data since then has been hot, with September PPI up 6.3% from a year earlier, the biggest gain since 2022.

Warsh's broader message was about global policy. He described "a synchronous tightening in monetary policy in most places around the world," and said "there's no free riders." In other words, the U.S. is not alone in raising rates to fight energy-driven inflation. CNBC noted that the Iran war and the AI investment boom are pushing up prices and borrowing costs worldwide, just as global public debt is on pace to exceed 100% of world GDP.

He also spoke about AI. He was optimistic it will make economies that use it well more productive and wealthier, but said the road will be "very bumpy," and its near-term effect on jobs is uncertain.

What this means for mortgages: the Fed sets short-term rates, while mortgage rates track the 10-year Treasury and MBS demand. An October pause removes one possible shock, which helps the bond market hold its recent gains. But a December hike still priced in tells you the Fed sees inflation as unfinished business. That keeps a floor under rates and argues against promising clients that big drops are coming.

So-what: This is the data that can undo the bond rally. If AI fears calm down, a wide inflation story pushes yields back up.

1-minute read

The bond rally was about fear, not cooler inflation. The inflation picture is still getting worse underneath.

RSM economist Joe Brusuelas, featured in Mauldin Economics' Over My Shoulder, found almost 61% of all prices in September CPI rose 3% or more over the past year. That points to broad inflation, not just a gas spike. September was also the sixth straight month of falling real wages. Owners' equivalent rent, a big housing piece of CPI, was up 3.3%.

Brusuelas sees three supply shocks hitting at once: tariffs, energy and AI. He expects inflation to stay above 3% unless oil, gasoline and diesel prices reverse. His call is a Fed hold in October and a hike in December.

Today's import price data adds to the picture. Peter Boockvar reported September import prices up 1.1% for the month and 8.1% from a year ago. Excluding food and fuel, they rose 0.7% for the month and 6.2% for the year.

Industrial production also beat, up 0.7% versus 0.4% expected, according to a headline in MBS Live's morning note. Strong output plus wide inflation is not the recipe for lower rates.

Expand 3-minute read

Two different stories are running in the bond market at once. One is the AI and tech scare, which pushed money into bonds. The other is the inflation data, which keeps saying rates should be higher. This item is about the second story, because that is what takes over if the first one fades.

Start with the breadth of consumer inflation. Mauldin Economics' Over My Shoulder highlighted RSM chief economist Joe Brusuelas's breakdown of September CPI. Almost 61% of all prices in the index rose 3% or more in the past year. When most prices are rising fast, it is hard to argue that inflation is just a one-off energy spike. Brusuelas also noted it was the sixth consecutive month of declining real wages, meaning pay is not keeping up with prices.

Housing costs inside CPI are not helping either. Owners' equivalent rent, a large shelter measure in the index, was up 3.3% from a year earlier. Brusuelas said rising shelter prices "aren't relenting."

His big-picture view is three supply shocks combining in the last months of 2026: tariffs, energy and AI. He expects inflation to stay sticky above 3% unless oil, gasoline and diesel prices reverse. He expects the Fed to hold in October and hike another 25 basis points in December. His bottom line is that upper-income spending keeps the economy growing, while middle-class households and below will draw down savings and lean on credit.

Now today's data. Peter Boockvar reported September import prices rose 1.1% for the month and 8.1% from a year earlier. Excluding food and fuel, import prices rose 0.7% for the month and 6.2% for the year. Capital goods import prices were up 8.4% from a year ago. MBS Live's calendar had the monthly forecast at 0.8%, so the reading came in hotter. Truflation's morning note carried the same theme in its subject line: import cost pressure is broadening beyond fuel.

Industrial production also came in strong. MBS Live's headline feed showed September output up 0.7% versus 0.4% expected. Boockvar's weekly summary added solid regional factory readings: the New York Fed manufacturing index at 25.5 and the Philadelphia Fed index at 20.9 for October. He also noted the Beige Book had three districts reporting robust price growth and eight reporting moderate growth, with diesel and fuel surcharges showing up everywhere.

Remember the bigger numbers from earlier this week. September PPI rose 1.3% for the month and 6.3% for the year. Core PPI was 0.7% for the month and 5.0% for the year. Headline CPI was 3.7% year over year.

Why it matters for mortgages: MBS Live's Graham said the only surefire justification for a bigger rally is a sustained economic slowdown. This data does not show one. Output is beating, regional factory surveys are strong, and inflation is spreading across categories. That is why he expects the rally to stall without a fresh tech rout. For clients, the plain message is that the inflation backdrop has not improved, even though rates got a break this week.

6) MBA's 'Affordability in Action' roadmap: single-file credit above 700, lower FHA premiums and LLPAs, a streamlined refi and LO comp flexibility

So-what: None of this is adopted yet. But every item maps to a real cost on your files, and the LO comp change could reshape how you match competing offers.

1-minute read

The Mortgage Bankers Association released a policy wish list at its annual convention in Chicago on Oct. 14. These are recommendations, not new rules.

The ideas aim at costs originators see every day. MBA wants single-file credit reports for borrowers with scores above 700, instead of the tri-merge requirement. It says tri-merge costs have tripled or quadrupled in five years.

On pricing, MBA wants Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac to cut and align loan-level price adjustments on owner-occupied purchases. A quarter-point reduction would cut upfront costs by $1,000 on a $400,000 loan.

For FHA, MBA wants lower annual mortgage insurance premiums. A 20 basis point cut would save a borrower nearly $800 a year on a $400,000 loan. MBA pairs that with tighter underwriting for layered risks like low scores, high DTI and no reserves.

It also proposes a streamlined GSE refinance for borrowers with strong payment histories, and removing VA's 36-month recoupment rule for some ARM-to-fixed and shorter-term refis.

The sleeper item: letting lenders reduce an LO's compensation to match a competing offer. Today, that is generally not allowed.

Expand 3-minute read

MBA's new paper is titled "Affordability in Action: A Policy Roadmap to Lower the Cost of Financing a Home to Own or Rent." It was released Oct. 14 at the MBA Annual Convention and Expo in Chicago. The key framing is that every recommendation can be done through administrative or regulatory action, without a new law. That makes these more realistic than a typical trade-group wish list, but it is still an advocacy agenda, not adopted policy.

Credit reports. MBA proposes single-file credit reports for borrowers with scores above 700, while keeping the option to pull more reports on higher-risk loans. National Mortgage Professional reports tri-merge costs have tripled or quadrupled over five years. MBA argues single-file lending would increase competition among the credit bureaus and lower closing costs. The roadmap does not set criteria beyond the score threshold. We covered the tri-merge fight earlier this week. What is new is that it is now one piece of a formal package.

GSE pricing. MBA wants Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac to reduce and align their loan-level price adjustments, or LLPAs, on owner-occupied purchase loans. LLPAs are the risk-based fees that show up as worse pricing for lower scores or higher loan-to-value. MBA's example: a quarter-point cut would lower upfront costs by $1,000 on a $400,000 loan.

FHA premiums. MBA wants a modest reduction in FHA's annual mortgage insurance premium. Its example is that a 20 basis point cut would save nearly $800 a year on a $400,000 loan. It pairs that with underwriting adjustments for loans with layered risks, such as low credit scores, high debt-to-income ratios and no reserves. In plain terms, cheaper MIP for most borrowers, with more scrutiny on the riskiest files.

Refinancing. MBA proposes a streamlined GSE refinance option for borrowers with strong payment histories who would get a lower payment. Lower underwriting cost and fewer delays could make smaller savings worth pursuing. For VA IRRRLs, MBA supports removing the 36-month recoupment requirement for certain ARM-to-fixed and shorter-term refinances, while keeping protections against loan churning.

LO compensation. This is the item most relevant to brokers and retail LOs. Today, lenders can improve pricing to save a deal, but generally cannot fund that concession by cutting the LO's agreed compensation on a specific loan. MBA wants that flexibility. It also argues comp restrictions discourage HFA, construction, down payment assistance and assumption loans, because those files take more work for the same pay. More flexibility could make those products more economical. The roadmap does not explain how lenders and LOs would share the cost of a concession.

Other pieces. MBA also wants safer expansion of automated valuation models and AI-assisted underwriting, and lower title insurance costs.

MBA CEO Bob Broeksmit said the paper lays out "focused steps policymakers can take now." Incoming 2027 chair Owen Lee of Success Mortgage Partners said the goal is to "strip away unnecessary costs and complexity that ultimately show up in a higher mortgage rate, excessive closing costs, or a higher rent."

What to watch: FHFA and HUD responses. FHA MIP and LLPA changes are the two items that would show up fastest in borrower payments.

7) Senators ask the FTC to probe portal 'contact agent' routing, steering to in-house lenders and private listings

So-what: Scrutiny of agent-to-lender steering gives independent brokers a clean talking point. It is also a reminder to keep your own referral and co-marketing setups clean.

1-minute read

Sens. Elizabeth Warren and Cory Booker sent a letter Thursday asking FTC Chair Andrew Ferguson to investigate "anticompetitive and deceptive practices" in online homebuying.

Warren is the top Democrat on the Senate Banking Committee. Booker is the top Democrat on the Senate Judiciary antitrust subcommittee.

The letter flags three practices. First, "contact agent" buttons that route buyers to platform-affiliated agents who pay the platform a cut of their commission, often without clear disclosure. Lance Lambert notes reports of Zillow Flex referral fees as high as 40%.

Second, steering to affiliated mortgage lenders. The senators say platform agents are often required to send buyers to in-house lenders. The letter cites reporting on Zillow Home Loans and agents' RESPA concerns. It says these practices "saddle homebuyers with higher-cost mortgages."

Third, private listings that keep homes off public sites. The letter cites the Compass-Anywhere merger.

Zillow said it shares the senators' focus and agrees private listings deserve more scrutiny. Compass declined to comment.

This builds on the FTC's August settlement with Zillow and Redfin over rentals. A letter is not an investigation, but it puts lender steering on the record in Washington.

Expand 3-minute read

The online real estate fight just added a mortgage angle that matters for brokers.

On Thursday, Oct. 15, Sens. Elizabeth Warren and Cory Booker wrote to FTC Chair Andrew Ferguson. They asked the agency to "investigate anticompetitive and deceptive practices in the home-buying market, using every enforcement tool available to restore competition to the entire online real estate marketplace." Warren is ranking member of the Senate Banking Committee, and Booker is ranking member of the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Antitrust.

The background is the FTC's August case against Zillow and Redfin. In that settlement, Zillow agreed to unwind its $100 million partnership with Redfin in rental listings, and Redfin must restart its rental business within six months. The senators want the same scrutiny applied to home buying and selling.

The letter names three practices.

Contact agent routing. When a buyer clicks "contact agent" on a listing, they may be sent to a platform-affiliated agent who pays the platform part of the commission. The senators say that arrangement often is not disclosed. ResiClub's Lance Lambert points to Zillow's Flex program, where referral fees have been reported as high as 40%.

Lender steering. The senators say platform-affiliated agents are often required to send buyers to in-house mortgage lenders without disclosing it. The letter cites reporting on Zillow Home Loans and agents' concerns about RESPA, the federal law that limits referral fees and kickbacks in mortgage settlement services. The letter's sharpest line: "These practices saddle homebuyers with higher-cost mortgages and thousands of dollars of additional agent fees, a large share of which are funneled to the market-dominant platforms or Brokerages." RISMedia notes Zillow has faced lawsuits over hidden fees and alleged steering to Zillow Home Loans. One, known as Taylor/Armstrong, was dismissed, and plaintiffs are trying to revive it.

Private listings. Large brokerages are keeping more inventory off public platforms. The senators argue that shrinks buyer access and can hurt sellers' price discovery. They cite the Compass v. Zillow lawsuit, since dropped after Zillow changed its Listing Access Standards, and the Compass-Anywhere merger.

Responses: Zillow said "too many Americans are locked out of homeownership" and that it shares the senators' focus. It also said private listings "deserve further scrutiny." Compass declined to comment.

The WSJ's Mansion section ran a related feature this week, "The Fierce Battle to Control How Americans Buy and Sell Their Homes." HousingWire framed the same letter for its readers as a question: could your lead-gen and referral practices face FTC scrutiny?

Why it matters for BuyWise. The lender-steering complaint is the mortgage broker's argument in a nutshell: buyers do better when someone shops lenders for them instead of being routed to one in-house option. That is a useful, factual talking point for agent partners and for content. Keep it factual: this is a letter from two senators, not an FTC action.

It also cuts both ways. Any referral, co-marketing or lead-sharing arrangement with agents should be clean under RESPA. If Washington starts looking at steering, it will look at everyone's setup, not just the portals.

8) Buyers are the shortage now: lockbox visits down 20%, 16% of contracts delayed, and four years of historically low resale turnover

So-what: Inventory is no longer the bottleneck. Payment is. Delays and late locks are hurting closings, so lock timing and payment cushions matter more than usual.

1-minute read

NAR's chief economist says the housing slowdown is now about demand, not supply.

Lawrence Yun cited lockbox data showing visits to homes for sale were down 20% in September from a year earlier. "Low sales activity is clearly due to lack of demand, not limited supply," he said. Inventory is back to pre-COVID levels.
