Here is how last week played out for mortgage pricing, and what it sets up for this week.

That is why Friday looked worse than the bond market did. On Friday itself, UMBS 6.0 slipped only 8 ticks to 98.68 and the 10-year edged up 1.3 basis points to 5.142%, per MBS Live's 4:34 PM ET Recap. 2) Fed speakers lean toward more hikes: Collins wants steady, gradual steps; Schmid says inflation is near 3% even without energy

Wed show

So-what: A December quarter-point hike is fully priced and January is expected too. A bigger move is the risk, not the base case.

1-minute read

Boston Fed President Susan Collins told Bloomberg Friday that "soon some additional firming" is appropriate and that the path may be tighter than she penciled in two months ago. On Saturday she added that "a steady, gradual path" fits best for now, and she would not back a half-point December hike. She also said higher long-term rates may slow demand more than she expects.

Kansas City Fed President Jeff Schmid said Sunday that inflation is "uncomfortably above" 2% and "moving in the wrong direction," and would still run near 3% with energy stripped out. He did not call for a specific hike.

Futures are fully priced for a quarter-point hike at the Dec. 8-9 meeting, with a small chance of a half point, and another hike is expected in January. The Fed funds range is 3.75% to 4.00%.

At her bank's housing conference, Collins said there is "no silver bullet" for housing and that tighter policy will likely hurt housing activity near term, even if beating inflation helps affordability later. This week brings Waller Tuesday, then Williams, Kashkari and Barr.

Expand 3-minute read

The Fed debate has moved from whether to hike to how fast. Weekend comments from two regional presidents put a little more weight on "steady" than on "bigger."

Susan Collins, Boston Fed. On Friday, in a Bloomberg Television interview, Collins said "it's clear the direction that monetary policy needs to go" and that "soon some additional firming, tightening of policy is appropriate." She said recent inflation data show price pressures broadening, and that a somewhat tighter policy path than she had penciled in a couple of months ago may be right. She called her best case "a careful adjustment of policy."

On Saturday, speaking to reporters at her bank's housing conference, she got more specific about pace. "A steady, gradual path seems appropriate to me at the moment. But I'm open-minded," she said, per Reuters. She sees risks as two-sided: inflation is high and spreading, the job market is stable and could pick up, but more shocks could hit, and the rise in medium- and long-term rates this year "may actually weigh on demand more than I'm thinking." She declined to back the half-point December move that Governor Christopher Waller floated last week. Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee has also declined to endorse 50 basis points.

Collins is not a 2026 voter. She rotates into a voting seat in 2028, so her view matters as a signal more than as a vote.

Her housing remarks were blunt. "There is no 'silver bullet' that will solve the many challenges facing housing today," she said, per Scotsman Guide. She echoed Governor Michael Barr's point that beating inflation should help long-run affordability by bringing mortgage rates down. But she conceded that tighter financial conditions will likely hurt housing near term and can affect "consumers' willingness and ability to obtain a mortgage."

Jeff Schmid, Kansas City Fed. Speaking Sunday to a credit union conference in Hawaii, Schmid said the Fed "has some work to do on inflation." His key data point: inflation would still be around 3% even without energy, which says the problem is wider than the oil shock. He pushed back on two popular arguments, that inflation is all supply shocks or that demand is immune to higher rates. Both, he said, assume inflation is beyond the Fed's control, and "I don't believe this to be true." He is not a 2026 voter either.

Where markets stand. The Fed hiked a quarter point in September to 3.75% to 4.00%, then held in October with Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack dissenting in favor of a hike. Futures now fully price a quarter-point hike on Dec. 8-9, with a small chance of a half point, and markets expect another hike in January.

One schedule change worth knowing for 2027 planning: the Fed said Thursday it will still meet eight times next year, but the October 2027 meeting will be a strategy session with no rate decision. The meeting usually held in early December moves up to mid-November. Warsh has said he expects two strategy-only meetings in 2028.

This week's Fed calendar is crowded: Warsh today at 9 AM PT, Waller Tuesday at 6:10 AM PT, the October meeting minutes Wednesday at 11 AM PT, plus Williams, Kashkari, Barr, Goolsbee and Paulson. For client conversations, the plain-English version: the Fed is still raising short-term rates, and mortgage rates will take their cue from whether officials sound steady or more aggressive.

Expand 3-minute read

Mohtashami's weekly tracker is one of the best quick reads on real-time housing demand. He wrote this one stuck in Dallas after Friday's FAA ground stop. Here are the main points.

Inventory. Weekly active inventory fell to 895,988 as the seasonal decline is now "in full force." Growth is running 7.11% above last year, even against easy comparisons, since late-2025 demand was heading toward a nine-month high in December. Inventory growth had been tame most of this year, sometimes negative year over year. It picked up after rates went above 6.64% and then toward 7.60%. His point: as inventory gets back to normal, it takes less of a demand drop to push inventory growth higher. This rise is about homes taking longer to sell, not a flood of new sellers.

New listings. Last week's new listings were 53,162, slightly lower than a year ago. Normal peak-season weeks run 80,000 to 100,000; during the bubble years, they ran 250,000 to 400,000. Despite a bigger year-over-year dip last week, 2026 has been the best year for new listings since 2022. His worry was that higher rates would make people stop listing, as happened in the second half of 2022. That has not happened.

Price cuts. About one-third of homes usually take a price cut before selling. This year cuts had been running below last year, until rates moved above 6.64% and then 7%. At that point demand fell and the price-cut share rose about 1 point year over year. His 2026 forecast called for a national price decline of -0.62%. Most indexes show 1% to 2% growth, so that will be hard to hit, but higher rates and more cuts are moving things that way. He adds that without rates above 6.75%, the recent slowdown in prices would not have shown up.

Spreads. Spreads typically widen after a big rate reversal, but this time they improved. The spread fell to 2.13% from 2.27%, while the historical range is 1.60% to 1.80%. If spreads kept healing toward normal, mortgage rates could improve even with a flat 10-year.

Demand. Weekly pending sales have stabilized for four straight weeks, which he calls the key takeaway. They are still well below last year, when rates were heading toward 6%. Purchase apps, which look 30 to 90 days ahead, rose 8% week to week as rates tried to get under 7%, but he expects the next print to get hit. His bottom line: "if we can get rates back below 7% and just have them stick, we can work with this."

This week. He flags many Fed speakers, a builder survey that "won't be great," weak housing starts, and pending home sales that may lag his stabilization by a month but start from record lows. He also flags retail sales and Trump headlines on Iran and trade.

For BuyWise: the message for buyers is that inventory is still up year over year and price cuts are rising, so negotiation power is real even at 7.50%. For sellers, new listings are flat, so competition is steady but not swelling.

5) ResiClub: 105 of the 200 largest metros now have more homes for sale than in 2019; national inventory is just 2.1% below pre-pandemic

Wed show

So-what: More than half of big markets are back to buyer-friendly inventory. Prices tend to be weakest where inventory is highest.

1-minute read

Lance Lambert's ResiClub counted 1,183,520 active listings at the end of October, per Realtor.com data. That is only 2.1% below October 2019's 1,208,311, and up 7.6% from a year ago. In October 2021, listings were 565,707, or 53.8% below 2019.

Now 105 of the 200 largest metros have more active inventory than in October 2019. In 2020 and 2021, none did. The softest markets are in the South and Mountain West, such as Cape Coral, Austin, Tampa and Colorado Springs, where pandemic price gains outran local incomes and builders added lots of supply. The Northeast and Midwest are tighter, though they have posted some of the biggest recent percentage gains, partly because they start from such low numbers.

CalculatedRisk's mid-November overview adds that October existing-home sales fell 8.8% year over year to a new cycle low, months of supply is the highest in over a decade, and the median price is up only 0.4%. Realtor.com's own count shows listings up 7.4%, the fourth straight month of faster growth.

Plain English: buyers have leverage in more places than at any time since before COVID.

Expand 3-minute read

Two inventory reads landed Friday and Monday, and they tell the same story with slightly different math.

ResiClub's metro count. Lance Lambert compared each of the 200 largest metros to the same month in pre-pandemic 2019. At the end of October 2026, there were 1,183,520 active homes for sale nationally, just 2.1% below October 2019's 1,208,311. During the boom, homes sold so fast that listings barely counted as inventory: October 2021 had only 565,707 active listings, 53.8% below 2019. At the end of October 2020 and 2021, not one of the top 200 metros had more inventory than in 2019. Today, 105 do. National active inventory is up 7.6% year over year.

Where it is softest. Lambert says many of the softest markets are former pandemic boomtowns in the South and Mountain West. Places like Cape Coral and Austin saw prices stretch far beyond local incomes. When migration slowed and rates spiked, they had to rely on local buyers. Lots of new construction in the Sun Belt made it worse, because builders cut net prices and offer incentives, and that pulls buyers away from resale homes. He pushes back on the idea that population growth alone explains the inventory jump in Tampa or Colorado Springs. The bigger driver is that their for-sale markets cooled harder after the boom.

Where it is tighter. Many Northeast and Midwest markets relied less on pandemic migration and have less new construction, so sellers still have the edge there, relatively speaking. Some of the biggest recent year-over-year gains are in those regions, but part of that is a base effect: when inventory is very low, a small increase shows up as a big percentage.

A methodology change. Comparing to 2019 will get less useful as populations grow, so ResiClub now also measures inventory per 1,000 household residents against the 2018-2019 average. The general picture holds. Lambert notes the count is not adjusted for sales volume. On months of supply, which accounts for slower sales, far more markets are above 2019 levels.

Why it matters for prices. Lambert's longer-running finding: metros where inventory is back to or above 2018-2019 levels have had weaker price growth or declines over the past 52 months. Metros still far below 2019 have had stronger price growth.

CalculatedRisk's read. Bill McBride's Part 1 overview for mid-November lists the key points for existing homes: October sales were down 8.8% year over year to a new cycle low, sales have been very low for four years, months of supply is at the highest level in more than a decade, inventory growth picked up again, and the median price is up 0.4%. He cites Realtor.com's October report showing 1,183,500 active listings, up 1.9% from September and 7.4% from a year earlier, the fourth straight month of accelerating growth. Realtor.com puts inventory 6.6% below typical pre-pandemic levels. That gap differs from ResiClub's 2.1% because Realtor.com compares with a 2017-2019 average rather than October 2019 alone. McBride also says 2026 has been tough for builders, with many completed homes unsold and builders cutting prices.

Expect active listings to drop through year-end, as they do every year. For the show, the 105-of-200 number is an easy headline.

So-what: Oil is not the main driver of bonds right now. A real supply shock would change that fast, so the Gulf stays on watch.

1-minute read

Iran launched drone strikes at U.S. military assets in Bahrain over the weekend, its first direct strike on the Gulf state in weeks. Bahrain and U.S. officials said all drones were intercepted. Iran's Revolutionary Guards said two tankers struck mines in the Strait of Hormuz, and six tankers caught fire Sunday and Monday. Reuters could not verify those claims. Oil barely flinched. Brent fell 69 cents to $95.15 and WTI to $88.19 this morning, per CNBC. The reason: the International Energy Agency said Friday the world will return to a surplus this quarter, with supply about 1.7 million barrels a day above demand as Gulf exports recover. Goldman Sachs expects Brent to average $88 in 2027.

Geopolitical Futures' George Friedman notes a Trump, Putin and Xi summit set for Nov. 19 on the APEC sidelines, with Iran a likely topic. He expects "very little" to come of it. Plain English: oil is a lower-priority rate risk this week than the Fed and inflation data.

Expand 3-minute read

The weekend brought escalation headlines from the Gulf, but the oil market treated them as noise. Here is why, and what would change that.

What happened. Iran targeted U.S. military assets in Bahrain, claiming it destroyed an early-warning radar. Bahraini and U.S. officials said they intercepted all the drones with no damage or casualties, per StoneX. The WSJ reports U.S. estimates that Iran still has up to 2,500 missiles, which counters the President's claims. At sea, the UK's maritime agency said a tanker was hit by an unidentified projectile while leaving the Strait of Hormuz early Monday, causing an engine-room fire with no injuries. Iran's Revolutionary Guards said two more tankers struck mines and warned oil companies that the southern Hormuz route is mined. Those claims have not been verified. A third U.S. carrier arrived in the region, and reports say the U.S. is not considering imminent airstrikes.

Why oil did not jump. Brent fell 69 cents to $95.15 and WTI fell 37 cents to $88.19 this morning, per CNBC. The big change is the supply outlook. The IEA said Friday the oil market will return to a surplus in the fourth quarter, with supply averaging 105 million barrels per day, 1.7 million above demand. It had previously forecast a deficit. It also said 71 million barrels from the record emergency reserve release will be available by the end of February, including 15 million barrels of diesel. KCM Trade's Waterer said that for the first time since the conflict began, traders are "actively contemplating" too much supply instead of too little. Goldman Sachs expects Brent to average $88 in 2027 and fall to $78 by late 2027, though it flagged near-term upside risk.

Washington's stance. Recent comments suggest the administration is leaning on economic pressure and control of shipping lanes rather than a negotiated deal. The President has said the U.S. "doesn't need a deal." Iranian news agency ILNA reported the U.S. plans to escalate pressure to secure an initial Strait of Hormuz agreement.

The summit. Geopolitical Futures founder George Friedman writes that a Trump, Putin and Xi meeting is now set for Nov. 19 on the sidelines of APEC. He sees two possible shared topics. One is nuclear arms, but he doubts Russia would agree because nukes are its main claim to global power. The other is Iran: the U.S. needs the war to end on terms that remove Iran's nuclear program, and China needs it to end because more than 20% of its GDP comes from exports. Russia has benefited from selling oil and diesel and may not want a fast end. Friedman's forecast: "very little will result from this trilateral meeting."

The rate channel. Oil matters to mortgages through inflation. Schmid said Sunday that inflation would be near 3% even without energy, so lower oil alone will not end Fed hikes. MBS Live's Graham said Friday that oil was not a convincing reason for the bond selloff, since oil was also higher Monday when yields were lower. Still, a real closure of Hormuz would push oil, inflation fears and yields higher fast.

For BuyWise: this is a watch item, not a lead story. If Brent breaks back above $100 on a real supply loss, it moves up the list.

7) Signing bonuses have strings: Movement wins a $1.235M clawback from two ex-LOs, while PRMG lands a 100-person team without paying bonuses

So-what: Upfront recruiting money works like a loan. Fall is peak season for team moves, so expect more offers and more fine print.

1-minute read

Movement Mortgage is asking a federal court in Charlotte to confirm an arbitration award requiring two former loan officers to repay $1,235,000 in "start-up payments," per HousingWire. Victor Shkolnikov got $835,000 and Igor Velyunskiy $400,000 when they joined in March 2022. The contracts called the money advances on future wages, due back within 30 days if they left before 36 months. Both left around March 2023. In July the arbitrator ruled for Movement, threw out their counterclaims, and added 8% annual interest from April 2023.

Separately, PRMG hired the roughly 100-person Responsive Mortgage team from Gold Star, including 55 LOs who have produced about $252 million since January, mostly in Texas and Florida. PRMG says the team turned down a bigger signing bonus elsewhere. PRMG pays no signing bonuses and buys no companies; it offers a share of future revenue plus some transition funding.

Also: Mortgage Solutions Financial is winding down originations, with its sales team joining Fairway. Plain English: retail lenders are fighting hard for producers, and the money has terms.

Expand 3-minute read

Two recruiting stories from Friday show the two sides of the LO talent market, and both are relevant to how a broker shop competes for producers.

The Movement clawback. According to court and arbitration documents reviewed by HousingWire, Movement paid $1,235,000 in "start-up payments" to loan officers Victor Shkolnikov ($835,000) and Igor Velyunskiy ($400,000) when they joined in March 2022. The employment agreements said these were advances on future wages and had to be repaid in full within 30 days if the LOs left before working 36 consecutive months. Both left around March 2023, about a year in. Public records show Shkolnikov now originates with United Lending Team in Illinois and Velyunskiy with Smart Rate Lending.

Movement started arbitration in February 2024. The LOs filed counterclaims alleging breach of contract, unjust enrichment, fraudulent inducement and Truth in Lending Act violations. In July, arbitrator H. William Constangy ruled for Movement, found the agreements valid, and dismissed every counterclaim. The award accrues 8% annual interest from April 2023, North Carolina's legal rate. Each side pays its own lawyers. In early November, Movement asked the U.S. District Court for the Western District of North Carolina to confirm the award as a court judgment, which makes it easier to collect.

The PRMG move. Paramount Residential Mortgage Group hired a roughly 100-person team of loan officers, processors and underwriters from Gold Star Mortgage. The team, Responsive Mortgage, is led by Mike Benton. InGenius data show 55 LOs moved, with about $252 million in production since January, mostly in Texas and Florida. It is the second team of more than 20 people to join PRMG in 90 days. PRMG has about $2.7 billion in production this year, 805 sponsored LOs and 117 branches. Gold Star has originated about $1.6 billion this year, down from $1.9 billion a year ago.

PRMG's Kevin Peranio said the team turned down a large retail lender's offer with a sizable signing bonus. His pitch against bonuses is pointed: "If you write a check to trap someone for a certain amount of years, the reason that's a trap is because the lenders that write those checks usually give them worse pricing." PRMG instead offers upside on future revenue and some upfront transition funding. He calls August through February "peak transition season," and RETR data show mega-producers are most likely to move in October and May.

More moves.
