For buyers in contract with closings inside 30 days, locking on a morning like this is the conservative call.

2) Case-Shiller: home prices up 2.1% a year in September; FHFA's Q3 index up 2.5%, the number that sets 2027 loan limits

Wed show

So-what: Prices are still rising on paper but falling after inflation for a 16th straight month. FHFA's 2.5% points to a modest bump in the 2027 conforming limit, which FHFA has not announced yet.

1-minute read

S&P Cotality's Case-Shiller National Index rose 2.1% year over year in September, the same as August. The 20-City index was up 2.6% and the 10-City up 3.5%. After seasonal adjustment, the national index rose 0.2% for the month. Without that adjustment it fell 0.3%, which S&P says is mostly the normal fall slowdown.

Inflation ran 3.7% in September, so real home prices fell for the 16th month in a row. Chicago led again at +6.9%, then New York +5.8% and Miami +4.6%. Seattle was weakest at -1.9%, followed by Denver -1.0% and Dallas -0.9%.

FHFA's House Price Index rose 2.5% from Q3 2025 to Q3 2026 and 0.7% for the quarter. The Middle Atlantic led at +4.9%. The Pacific region, which includes California, was up just 0.5%. Bill McBride at CalculatedRisk notes the FHFA index is used to set conforming loan limits. As of this morning FHFA's site still shows only 2026 limits, with a $832,750 one-unit baseline.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is what today's two price reports say, and why the second one matters for your loan sizes.

Case-Shiller first. Keep the lag in mind. The September index is a three-month average of July, August and September closings, and July closings include contracts signed in May. So this is mostly a snapshot of late spring and summer deals, before rates jumped back above 7% in mid-September.

The national index rose 2.1% year over year, matching August. The 10-City composite slowed to 3.5% from 3.6%, and the 20-City held at 2.6%. On a seasonally adjusted basis, the national index rose 0.18% for the month and the 20-City rose 0.1%. Unadjusted, both the national and 10-City indices fell (0.3% and 0.5%). Rebecca Kaufman at S&P Dow Jones Indices said that gap suggests much of the monthly weakness is the normal fall slowdown, not a broad breakdown in prices.

The bigger theme is real prices. Consumer prices rose 3.7% in September, up from 3.4% in August. That makes 16 straight months where home prices lost ground after inflation. For owners, a 2% gain with 3.7% inflation is a real loss. For buyers, it means homes are slowly getting cheaper relative to everything else, even if the sticker price is not falling.

Regional splits stayed wide, with a nearly 9-point gap between the best and worst metros. Chicago led for the sixth straight month at +6.9%, followed by New York at +5.8% and Miami at +4.6%. Miami has now climbed into the top three, a sharp turn from its weak 2025. Seattle had the largest drop for the fourth straight month at -1.9%, followed by Denver at -1.0% and Dallas at -0.9%. CalculatedRisk said annual changes were close to expectations.

Now FHFA. Its House Price Index rose 2.5% from Q3 2025 to Q3 2026 and 0.7% from Q2. The September monthly reading was flat versus August. All nine census divisions were positive year over year. The Middle Atlantic led at +4.9%. The Pacific division, which covers California, Oregon, Washington, Alaska and Hawaii, rose just 0.5%.

Why it matters for originators: under the HERA formula, the baseline conforming loan limit moves with FHFA's house price data. FHFA's conforming loan limit page still lists only the 2026 numbers as of this morning: $832,750 for one unit in most counties, $1,066,250 for two units, $1,288,800 for three and $1,601,750 for four. Industry write-ups expect the 2027 figures in late November alongside this Q3 release. The Mortgage Reports noted some lenders are already offering higher 2027-style limits before FHFA acts. Treat any 2027 number as unofficial until FHFA publishes it.

What it means for you: a 2.5% index gain would imply a modest increase in the baseline, not a big jump. Borrowers sitting right at the current $832,750 line may get a little more room in January. Watch for the FHFA release in the next few days, and do not quote a 2027 limit to clients until it is official.

3) Consumer confidence jumped 11.4 points to 103.3 in November, the best since January 2025; home-buying plans ticked up

So-what: Good news for buyer demand, less good for rates. A stronger consumer gives the Fed one more reason to keep hiking, and inflation expectations are still 5.7%.

1-minute read

The Conference Board's Consumer Confidence Index rose 11.4 points to 103.3 in November from 91.9 in October. That is the highest reading since January 2025. The Present Situation Index jumped 13.7 points to 132.8, and the Expectations Index rose 9.9 points to 83.6.

The labor market drove it. The share saying jobs are "plentiful" minus those saying jobs are "hard to get" improved 7.6 points to +12.4%. Senior economist Fergus McCormick said the gain may reflect lower uncertainty after the midterm elections.

Two numbers matter for housing. Plans to buy a home improved slightly in November. But consumers' 12-month inflation expectations, while down for a second month, are still elevated at 5.7%, and consumers still expect higher interest rates over the next year.

For rates, this is a stronger-than-hoped print in a week where the Fed is already priced for a December hike. It does not help the bond market's case.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is what the November confidence survey says and why it matters for your pipeline.

The headline: the Conference Board's index rose 11.4 points to 103.3 (1985=100), up from 91.9 in October. After two straight monthly gains, it is back to levels last seen in January 2025. The survey period for the preliminary results was November 1 to 17, so it captures the post-election period and the first half of the month's stock market swings.

The Present Situation Index, which measures how people see business and job conditions today, jumped 13.7 points to 132.8. Net views of current business conditions rose 3.5 points to +6.5%. The labor market differential, which is the share saying jobs are plentiful minus the share saying jobs are hard to get, improved 7.6 points to +12.4%.

The Expectations Index, which looks six months ahead, rose 9.9 points to 83.6. Net expectations for business conditions moved back into positive territory at +2.3%. Net expectations for the labor market improved 9.2 points to -1.2%, still slightly negative but much less gloomy. Expected household income eased slightly to +6.3%.

Fergus McCormick, the Conference Board's senior economist, said the rally may be due to lower uncertainty after the midterm elections. Every age group improved, with the biggest gain among Gen Z. The largest improvements were among higher-income households. Write-in answers still complained about high prices, but some cited hope for the economy.

Housing details: on both a monthly and a six-month average basis, plans to buy autos and homes improved slightly. Planned spending on durable goods generally increased. Vacation plans rose to 44.9% of consumers from 42.7%.

Inflation details: average 12-month inflation expectations fell for a second month but remain elevated at 5.7%. Consumers still expect higher interest rates over the next 12 months. More consumers expect stock prices to rise than fall over the next year, despite November's volatility.

Context for the jump: the October reading of 91.9 came during a stretch of rising mortgage rates and stock volatility, so part of November's gain is a rebound from a weak base.

The rate angle: the Fed is weighing whether to keep hiking. Governor Lisa Cook said last week that inflation is unacceptably high and financial conditions are too easy, partly because stock gains support spending. A confident consumer with 5.7% inflation expectations fits her argument. CME's FedWatch already shows about a 100% chance of a quarter-point hike on Dec. 9, per CNBC.

What it means for you: expect a bit more buyer interest into early 2027, especially from younger buyers whose confidence improved most. But do not expect this data to help rates. If anything, a strong consumer plus a hot PCE print Wednesday would push yields back up. Use the confidence rebound in your content as a reason buyers are coming back, and pair it with a reminder that pricing can move fast.

4) Fed watch: Williams says there is still work to do, Bessent warns against overtightening, and a December hike is fully priced

Wed show

So-what: The Fed and the Treasury are now publicly pulling in different directions. Markets believe the Fed, so plan around a Dec. 9 hike and let Wednesday's PCE decide how much more is priced after that.

1-minute read

New York Fed President John Williams said Monday that policy needs to adjust to "get inflation back to our 2% goal in a timely manner," adding "there is still work to do." He said the economy has stayed strong and inflation has kept running too high. CNBC reports CME FedWatch now shows about a 100% chance of a quarter-point hike at the Dec. 8 to 9 meeting.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent pushed back Sunday on CBS. "Many people believe that they may be making a policy mistake if they overtighten," he said, pointing to "a substantial tightening in financial conditions over the past few months" and "very hawkish remarks" from Fed officials.

That follows Governor Lisa Cook's Friday speech calling inflation "unacceptably high." The Fed hiked a quarter point in September. Wednesday's October PCE, expected at +0.3% for the month by Dow Jones, is the last big inflation read before the meeting.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is where the rate-hike debate stands heading into Wednesday's data.

The Fed side keeps getting more hawkish. On Monday, New York Fed President John Williams, one of the most influential voices on the committee, said monetary policy will need to adjust to "get inflation back to our 2% goal in a timely manner and on a sustained basis." He said "there is still work to do to get that done" and that the latest data shows "the economy has remained strong and that inflation has continued to run too high." When the New York Fed president talks like that a few weeks before a meeting, markets take it as a signal.

Last Friday, Governor Lisa Cook called inflation "unacceptably high" and said urgency is needed. She dissented in October in favor of a hike, along with Cleveland's Beth Hammack, and said holding rates steady is as much a policy action as raising them. She also argued that financial conditions are too easy because stock gains are supporting spending and the AI build-out.

The Treasury side is pushing back. Scott Bessent told CBS's Face the Nation on Sunday that "many people believe that they may be making a policy mistake if they overtighten." He pointed to "a substantial tightening in financial conditions over the past few months" and "very hawkish remarks" from Fed officials in recent weeks. Scotsman Guide noted the Fed raised its benchmark rate by a quarter point in September. Mortgage rates are the clearest example of that tightening: the 30-year has been above 7% since mid-September.

Market pricing: CNBC reports CME's FedWatch tool shows about a 100% chance of another quarter-point hike at the Dec. 8 to 9 meeting. Last week that was in the mid-90s. A hike is now the base case, so the meeting itself may not move mortgage rates much. What moves rates is how many more hikes the market prices into 2027, and that depends on the data.

That is why Wednesday matters. October PCE, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, is due with a Dow Jones consensus of +0.3% for the month. Cook said earlier data suggested PCE ran 3.8% year over year in October. Revised Q3 GDP is also due, expected around 3.4% annualized per Trading Economics, compared with 2.2% in Q2. Jobless claims and new home sales round out the morning.

How this plays for mortgage rates: a hot PCE plus strong GDP would likely push the market to price more hikes after December, which would lift the 2-year and drag the 10-year up with it. A softer PCE would help the case that one more hike might be the last. Bessent's comments do not change the Fed's vote, but they show the political pressure will grow if the Fed keeps going.

What it means for you: do not tell clients rates will fall because the Treasury wants them to. The Fed is in charge of short rates, and it is signaling more work to do. Frame it as a two-way risk this week, with Wednesday morning as the moment of truth. Borrowers who need certainty before the holiday should lock before PCE, not after.

5) ResiClub on D.R. Horton: orders down 7%, starts cut 10%, unsold finished homes back up to 8,800 even with 1.5-point rate buydowns

Wed show

So-what: America's biggest builder says demand got worse as rates rose and it is not counting on rate relief in 2027. Expect builder incentives to stay rich, which is a competitive threat and a buyer opportunity.

1-minute read

Lance Lambert's ResiClub PRO report broke down D.R. Horton's fiscal Q4 2026 results for the quarter ended Sept. 30. Net orders fell 7.3% year over year to 18,619. Cancellations rose to 23% from 20%. Gross margin fell to 19.5% from 20.7% the prior quarter, below the builder's own target, and it guided to 18.0% to 18.5% next quarter.

Unsold completed homes jumped to 8,800 from 5,500 six months earlier. In response, D.R. Horton started just 13,100 homes, down 45% from the prior quarter and 10% from a year ago.

The builder is still buying down rates about 1.5 points below market, after averaging 1.6 points last quarter at rates of 4.99% to 5.50%. As market rates rose, it raised the buydown rate rather than widening the gap. High-80% to low-90% of its mortgage customers use a buydown. CEO Paul Romanowski said October looked like a soft September, and management is not counting on rate relief.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is what America's largest homebuilder is telling us about the market, through ResiClub's read of its earnings.

Demand: CEO Paul Romanowski said demand "did weaken as the quarter progressed" as rates rose, and that October looked similar to a soft September, with traffic and sales "a little lower than what we would expect on a normal seasonal basis." Net sales orders fell 7.3% year over year to 18,619. ResiClub's history shows that is still above pre-pandemic levels (13,130 in Q4 2019) but below the last three years. The cancellation rate rose to 23% from 20%.

Margins: gross margin on home sales was 19.5%, down 120 bps from 20.7% in the June quarter and below expectations. D.R. Horton said 80 bps came from tougher market conditions that limited its ability to pass higher costs to buyers, and 40 bps from higher litigation reserves. It guided to 18.0% to 18.5% for fiscal Q1 2027 because of lower average prices and higher incentive costs at current rates, and said incentives will stay elevated through fiscal 2027.

Inventory: unsold completed homes, which hit a cycle high of 10,400 in Q1 2025, had been worked down to 5,500 by fiscal Q2 2026. They are back up to 8,800. Most of it is fresh, with only 600 finished and unsold for more than six months. Adjusted for its much larger size, ResiClub says the inventory is still in the normal range relative to sales. The builder cut starts to 13,100, down 45% from the prior quarter and 10% from a year ago, and said fast cycle times let it run with fewer homes in inventory.

Buydowns: D.R. Horton is still buying rates down about 1.5 percentage points below the prevailing market. Last quarter the average was 160 bps, with offered rates "pretty consistently" between 4.99% and 5.50%. As market rates climbed, it raised the offered rate instead of spending more to keep it low. Roughly high-80% to low-90% of buyers using its in-house lender get a buydown. It is also shrinking homes: average square footage is down 4% over five years, and attached homes are now about 16% of closings, up from 10% a year ago.

Costs: the easy cost relief is reversing. Stick-and-brick costs rose 1% per square foot from the prior quarter, though they were down 4% for the year. Lot costs rose 4% for the quarter and 6% for the year, while revenue per square foot fell 2% for the year. Management cited earlier lumber price increases and some labor inflation tied to immigration enforcement in certain markets.

Where it is holding up: smaller and secondary metros with less inventory buildup are selling closer to plan. The harder markets are larger ones with lots of new and resale competition. Its rental arm also took $91.4 million in impairments on nine multifamily projects as higher rates cut what investors will pay.

What it means for you: when you compete against a builder's in-house lender, the client is often comparing your quote to a rate 1.5 points below market. Help buyers compare total cost: price, the incentive's value, and what the same incentive could do as a price cut or a permanent buydown on your loan. In inventory-heavy markets, buyers have leverage, so encourage them to negotiate.

6) Dream Finders follows Lennar in selling finished new homes straight to mom-and-pop investors, with a 5.99% builder buydown on the site

So-what: Builders with too many finished spec homes are hunting for any buyer, including small landlords. That is DSCR and investor-loan opportunity, and it shows how much negotiating room buyers have in soft Sun Belt markets.

1-minute read

ResiClub reports that Dream Finders Homes, now the No. 7 U.S. builder after buying Beazer Homes, launched Dream Finders Marketplace on Nov. 18. It is a free site that pitches "investment-ready homes" to individual investors in 22 cities and communities, including D.C., Raleigh, Charlotte, Atlanta suburbs, Jacksonville, Sarasota, Austin, Dallas, Houston, San Antonio, suburban Nashville and Denver.

Investors can screen homes by rent comps, school scores and projected return. The site also shows current deals, such as a 5.99% rate made possible by a builder-paid forward commitment. Lennar launched a similar investor marketplace in August 2025.

Why now: Dream Finders' Q3 net sales fell 15% year over year to 1,712 homes, but excluding bulk build-to-rent deals they rose 6%. Institutional buyers pulled back, so the builder is going after small landlords one house at a time. Dream Finders (14.2%) and Beazer (13.6%) posted two of the lowest gross margins among the 15 big builders ResiClub tracks in Q2.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is why a builder investor portal matters for originators.

The move: On Nov. 18, Dream Finders Homes launched Dream Finders Marketplace, a free website that gives individual investors access to what it calls "investment-ready homes." At launch it lists ready-to-rent homes in 22 cities and communities across its footprint: the Washington, D.C., metro; Raleigh, Charlotte and Wilmington in North Carolina; Myrtle Beach, Greenville and Spartanburg in South Carolina; suburban Atlanta and the I-85 corridor; Jacksonville, Winter Haven, Dade City and Sarasota in Florida; and Austin, Dallas, Houston, San Antonio, suburban Nashville and Denver, among others.

The company says investors can evaluate homes using rental comparisons, school district scores, occupancy mix or projected return on investment, all before touring. The site also advertises current incentives, such as a 5.99% interest rate made possible by a builder-paid forward commitment that buys down the market rate.

The context: Dream Finders recently bought Beazer Homes. Dream Finders closed 8,608 homes in 2025 and Beazer closed 4,427, for a combined 13,035, which makes it the nation's No. 7 builder on a pro forma basis. Before the deal, Dream Finders was heaviest in Florida, with a big presence in the Carolinas, Texas, Tennessee, Colorado and Georgia. Many of those are exactly the markets where builders are struggling most to move finished spec homes.

The pressure: among the 15 major builders ResiClub tracks, Dream Finders (14.2%) and Beazer (13.6%) had the second- and third-lowest homebuilding gross margins in Q2 2026. Only K. Hovnanian was lower at 11.8%. CEO Patrick Zalupski said in the Q3 release, "Margins remain under pressure, and we continue to carefully balance sales pace and price." Q3 net sales fell 15% year over year to 1,712 homes. Excluding built-for-rent activity, net sales rose 6%. In other words, the drop came from bulk buyers who buy whole rental communities pulling back.

The bigger picture: ResiClub's Finished Unsold New Homes Supply Index, which compares unsold completed homes to sales, has drifted back toward the historically normal range but is still on the high side. The slack is concentrated in parts of the Southeast, Southwest and Mountain West, especially Texas, Colorado and Florida, where active inventory is still well above 2019 levels. Lambert lists the builder's levers: cut prices, add incentives like buydowns, slow starts, or find new buyers. Lennar and Dream Finders are now pulling that last lever with small landlords.

What it means for you: this is a direct opening for DSCR and investor lending. A small investor buying a new spec home through a builder portal still needs financing, and the builder's affiliated lender may not offer the investor-loan flexibility a broker can. Lambert's warning applies to your clients too: run the numbers with your own rent, vacancy and expense assumptions, not only the builder's. In soft markets, investors and owner-occupants alike have leverage. Ask what the builder will pay toward a permanent buydown on a loan you arrange, and compare that to the advertised rate.

7) Newrez survey: 95% of originators say small-business owners don't know non-QM could get them qualified, and 8 in 10 could buy sooner than they think

Wed show

So-what: Self-employed buyers are a big, under-served market, and the gap is education, not product. That is exactly the content lane for the show and for referral partners.

1-minute read

Newrez surveyed 99 originators who work with self-employed borrowers, Scotsman Guide reports. Its headline finding: more than 8 in 10 prospective self-employed buyers could likely qualify sooner than they realize.

Nearly three-quarters of originators said small-business owners assume they will not qualify before they ever talk to a lender. About 95% said these borrowers are often unaware non-QM products could help. And 99% said non-QM has become a more important tool for small-business owners over the past few years.

About three-quarters of originators said more than half their small-business clients choose non-QM. Roughly 7 in 10 said self-employed borrowers are more anxious about the process than W-2 borrowers. About 70% said around half their self-employed clients are first-time buyers. Among originators who serve this group, small-business owners make up a median 25% of loan volume.

Newrez's Bob Johnson put it simply: their income "isn't always fully captured by traditional documentation," because write-offs shrink taxable income.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is what the Newrez study says and how to use it.

The survey: Newrez, the national lender and servicer, queried 99 loan originators who work with self-employed borrowers. Bob Johnson, its head of originations, framed the goal: "Building a business of your own should help you buy a home, not stand in your way."

The education gap: Nearly three-quarters of originators said small-business owners assume they will not qualify for a mortgage before speaking with a broker or lender. Around 95% said small-business owners "are often unaware that non-QM products could help them qualify." Newrez concluded that more than 8 in 10 prospective self-employed buyers could likely qualify sooner than they think.

The anxiety factor: roughly 7 in 10 originators said self-employed borrowers feel more anxious about the loan process than borrowers with W-2 wages. About 70% said around half of their self-employed clients are first-time buyers. That matters because first-time buyers have already been squeezed hardest by affordability. NAR puts first-time buyer share around 20% this year, near historic lows.

Why they get stuck: Johnson said small-business owners "have income that isn't always fully captured by traditional documentation." The study pointed to tax write-offs and deductions. They lower taxable income, which helps at tax time but hurts under Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac guidelines that lean on tax returns.

The product side: Scotsman Guide notes non-QM has grown rapidly since the pandemic and has become a key production channel for lenders in a slow market. Non-QM loans let borrowers qualify with profit-and-loss statements, bank statements or assets held in reserve. About three-quarters of originators surveyed said more than half of their small-business clients choose a non-QM loan. Nearly all (99%) said non-QM has become a more important tool for this group over the past few years.

The business case: among originators who work with small-business owners, these borrowers make up a median 25% of their loan volume, and 40% said they make up more than a quarter. In what Scotsman calls a prolonged low-production market, that is a lot of business left on the table when owners never ask.

Caveat: this is a lender survey with a business interest in non-QM, and 99 originators is a small sample. Treat the percentages as directional, not as hard market data.

What it means for you: the barrier is awareness, not availability. Talk directly to business owners about bank statement, P&L and asset-based options, and explain the trade-off. Non-QM rates usually run higher than conforming, so the conversation should cover cost now versus waiting two years to show more taxable income. For referral partners, a simple message works: self-employed buyers should talk to a broker before assuming no. This fits a Wednesday show segment on self-employed buyers.

8) MBA tells Treasury its rule limiting big investors' home purchases misreads the ROAD Act's build-to-rent exception; comments closed Tuesday

So-what: How Treasury writes this rule decides which rental homes big investors can still buy. Brokers who do investor and DSCR lending should watch how 'single-family home' and 'build-to-rent' get defined.

1-minute read

The Mortgage Bankers Association filed a 12-page comment letter on Treasury's proposed rule under the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, Scotsman Guide reports. The rule would bar investors that control at least 350 single-family homes from buying more, unless a purchase fits an exception. About 434 public comments were posted as of Tuesday evening, ahead of the deadline.

MBA's main objection: Treasury limits the build-to-rent exception to future "horizontal multifamily" rental communities. MBA SVPs Jamie Woodwell and Pete Mills argue the law's text covers homes that have been managed as rentals since they were newly built, no matter how long ago that was.

Here is the fight over the investor-purchase rule and why it matters.

Background: the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act was enacted in July. One piece restricts large institutional investors from buying single-family homes. Treasury's proposed rule would prohibit investors that control at least 350 single-family homes from buying additional homes, unless the purchase falls under one of several exceptions. As of 6:30 PM ET Tuesday, 434 public comments were visible.

The MBA letter: written by senior vice presidents Jamie Woodwell and Pete Mills, the 12-page letter says the proposal "misinterprets the Act's text and intent." Their main target is the build-to-rent (BTR) exception. Treasury limits it to prospective "horizontal multifamily" rental communities, meaning new rental neighborhoods yet to be built.

MBA disagreed with Treasury's statement that a "newly constructed single-family home" could "include older homes that have been renter-occupied for potentially decades." They argued that in the statute, "newly constructed" modifies "to be managed as a rental property." Their reading: "The language, while not as clear as one might hope, focuses the exception on homes that, since newly constructed, have been managed as rental properties, regardless of how long ago the construction of the rental property took place."

MBA also said Treasury leaned on wording "never used in the Act." It even pointed out the law's own grammar problems, noting a reference to "a newly constructed single-family homes" [sic]. It cited President Trump's January 2026 executive order, "Stopping Wall Street From Competing With Main Street Homebuyers," and statements from Senate Banking Chair Tim Scott and House Financial Services Chair French Hill. MBA said none supports limiting the exception to future BTR. "The President and Congressional leaders have consistently focused on the form and function of BTR, not on whether the property is prospective or already in existence," the letter said.

MBA's asks: eliminate the BTR program definition, or replace it with a more detailed two-part definition. It also wants "essential clarification" on what counts as a single-family home, tied to Fannie and Freddie eligibility rules for homes with two or fewer units.

Why it matters: MBA said the law "has the potential to significantly change the structure of the housing market. Whether it does so positively or negatively will depend on many of the specifics of its implementation." A narrow exception could force large investors to stop buying existing rental homes. That could free some inventory for owner-occupants. It could also slow capital flowing into rental housing. Builders who depend on bulk BTR sales, like Dream Finders, already show what happens when institutional demand pulls back.

What it means for you: most of your investor clients own far fewer than 350 homes, so this rule is not aimed at them. It may even help small investors compete for homes the big players can no longer buy. The details to watch are the final definitions of "single-family home" and "build-to-rent." Those will shape how much the rule reshapes local markets. Expect a final rule sometime after Treasury reviews the comments.
