Here is the latest product-by-product rate check and where it points.

The table. Mortgage News Daily's daily index, last updated Nov. 25, showed: 30-year fixed 7.26% (down 0.06 on the day, 0.20 in a week, 0.07 in a month, up 1.06 from a year ago). 15-year fixed 6.82% (down 0.05 on the day, 0.16 in a week). 30-year FHA 6.95% (flat on the day, down 0.09 in a week). 30-year jumbo 7.40% (down 0.02 on the day, 0.10 in a week). 7/6 SOFR ARM 6.60% (down 0.10 on the day, 0.47 in a week). 30-year VA 6.96%. The 52-week range for the 30-year fixed runs from 5.99% to 7.61%.

The recent path. MND had the 30-year at 7.45% on Nov. 19, 7.33% on Nov. 20, 7.34% on Nov. 23, 7.32% on Nov. 24 and 7.26% on Nov. 25. So the 30-year has fallen about 19 bps in four trading days. The ARM fell from 6.83% on Nov. 23 to 6.60% in two days.

Why the ARM moved more. ARM pricing leans on shorter-term yields and investor demand for adjustable product. The fixed rate has to carry more prepayment and duration risk, which keeps its spread wide when markets are jumpy. When rates drop fast, lenders often pass the gain through on ARMs first.

What the gap is worth. At 7.26% versus 6.60%, the monthly principal and interest on a $700,000 loan is about $4,780 versus $4,471, a difference of roughly $310 a month. Over seven years that is about $26,000 before any refinance. On a jumbo it scales up.

California context. A WFG Title Insurance Orange County update this week says California leads all states with 31% of home loans being ARMs. It lists the Orange County median at $1,220,000. In that price range, an ARM discount is a meaningful affordability lever.

Risks to explain. A 7/6 ARM is fixed for seven years, then adjusts every six months off SOFR. The Fed is hiking now, so borrowers should understand the adjustment caps and the worst case. The pitch is not "rates will be lower later." It is "you save now, and you have seven years of fixed payments to sell, refinance or absorb a change."

Freddie Mac's weekly survey, which lags and averages, had the 30-year at 7.28% in its Nov. 20 reading, per the WFG note. MND's daily index is the better real-time read, but the two roughly agree that top-tier rates are in the mid 7.2% range.

FHA and VA. Government rates sit about 30 bps under conventional, at 6.95% and 6.96%. For lower down payment buyers, FHA is still the cheaper monthly path in many cases even with mortgage insurance.

How to use it. Run side-by-side ARM and fixed quotes on every jumbo and high-balance file this week.

6) USTR proposes new Section 301 tariffs on 14 economies: China up to 20%, Vietnam 35%, India 25%, EU and Japan 15%. Comments due Jan. 5

So-what: Nothing takes effect yet, but more tariffs mean more inflation pressure in 2027, which is the wrong direction for rates.

1-minute read

On Nov. 25, U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer found that 14 economies have unfair policies that create "structural excess capacity" in manufacturing. He proposed new tariffs on each one.

The list: China, the European Union, Japan, Korea, Taiwan, Vietnam, India, Singapore, Switzerland, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand and Bangladesh. Mexico and Norway were not decided yet.

Proposed rates per USTR and its summary: China up to 20% on most goods and up to 10% on a separate list, Vietnam up to 35%, India up to 25%, Singapore up to 20%, several Southeast Asian countries up to 18%, and the EU, Japan, Korea, Switzerland and Taiwan up to 15%. There are product exemptions.

These are proposals. Public comments are due Jan. 5, 2027, and hearings start Jan. 25. The WSJ ran two pieces today warning the move could undermine the U.S.-China trade truce and that this is a bad time for new tariffs.

Plain English: tariffs raise prices on imported goods, including building materials and appliances. That keeps inflation sticky, which keeps the Fed hiking and mortgage rates higher.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is what USTR actually proposed and why a mortgage shop should care.

The finding. USTR's Nov. 25 press release says Greer determined under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 that the acts, policies and practices of 14 economies tied to structural excess capacity are unreasonable and burden U.S. commerce. That makes them actionable. USTR also published a report on each economy. The economies are China, the EU, Singapore, Switzerland, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan, Bangladesh, Japan and India. USTR said it has not made determinations on Mexico or Norway.

The proposed tariffs. For China, USTR proposes tariffs of up to 20% on goods not listed in the annexes and up to 10% on goods in Annex A, with exemptions for an Annex B list tied to the U.S.-China Board of Trade. USTR says the China proposal accounts for the 20% rate and commitments in the Kuala Lumpur arrangement between Presidents Trump and Xi from October 2025. For the EU, it proposes a 15% rate net of existing duties. Bangladesh and Cambodia are up to 18%. Per USTR's summary, Vietnam is up to 35%, India up to 25%, Singapore up to 20%, Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand up to 18%, and Japan, Korea, Switzerland and Taiwan up to 15%. Most rates are net of existing tariffs and come with product exemptions.

Timeline. These are proposals open for public comment. Comments and hearing requests are due Jan. 5, 2027, and hearings begin Jan. 25. So nothing changes on prices this holiday season.

Reaction. The WSJ's page A2 today has "Why This Is a Terrible Time for New Tariffs" and "New Tariffs on China Risk Undermining Trade Truce With U.S." The editorial page calls it a "post-election message: more tariffs." India's trade minister said a bilateral deal could still be signed before year end, which could lower India's rate.

Why it matters for rates. The Fed is hiking because inflation is running hot, with October core PCE at 3.3%. Broad new tariffs would push up goods prices in 2027 just as the Fed is trying to cool them. Bond traders tend to price that in early through higher inflation expectations. The same logic hits housing costs directly. Lumber, steel, fixtures and appliances come through these trade channels, and builders are already cutting prices and leaning on incentives to move homes.

What could soften it. Deals. Several countries are already in talks, and USTR's own text says some proposals reflect ongoing consultations. Exemptions and negotiated rates could shrink the final package a lot.

How to use it. This is a rate-channel story, not a politics story. The simple client line: when costs for goods go up, inflation stays high, and that keeps mortgage rates from falling as fast as people hope.

In a HousingWire column, real estate coach Darryl Davis weighs in on Open Real Estate, a new national listing platform backed by a coalition of large brokerages.

He agrees with the goal. Listings are moving into private and exclusive channels that never hit the open market. That hurts buyers, who see less, and agents, who compete on access instead of skill.

Here is the argument and why it reaches the mortgage side.

The trigger. A coalition of large brokerages announced Open Real Estate, a shared national listing platform. Darryl Davis, a national speaker and author of How to Become a Power Agent in Real Estate, wrote the column for HousingWire. He says he has written a white paper of more than 70 pages on why a broker-controlled national MLS is the wrong design. HousingWire notes the column does not necessarily reflect its own editorial view.

Credit where due. Davis says the coalition is responding to a real problem: listings moving into private and exclusive channels. When inventory goes into walled gardens, buyers see less, agents compete on access instead of expertise, and consumers are quietly disadvantaged.

The practical problem. The founders say the platform is not a national MLS and will not replace local MLSs. Davis says it functions as national listing infrastructure anyway. It includes a participant view option, where a listing can be seen only by agents at participating brokerages and cannot be publicly marketed. His conclusion: the model does not stop private listings. It gives them a larger, better organized home.

The structural problem. Today there are hundreds of local MLSs. People complain about the fragmentation, but Davis says it has a hidden benefit. Because data lives in many independent systems, no single company controls the national market. Collapse that into one system run by brokers, and the firms with the deepest pockets set the rules for everyone else, including their competitors. He calls that a tollgate owned by a few.

The proposed model. Davis is not against unifying. He says a national MLS could give agents nationwide access and one set of rules, forms and fields in every state. His model is Visa and Mastercard: a neutral entity runs the shared technology and standards, while banks still compete. He wants the same for listings: a broker-independent operator with rules that treat the smallest independent and the largest franchise the same.

His test. Judge every new national platform on governance, not features. Ask who owns it, who sits on the board, how rules change and what recourse a small brokerage has.

Why it matters to a lender. Agent referrals depend on agents having listings to show. If big brokerages keep more inventory inside their own walls, smaller agents lose deals, and so do the lenders those agents refer. It also pushes buyers toward brokerages with in-house lending and affiliated mortgage shops. Because your team lends in many states, this plays out in every market you serve, not just California.

How to use it: a good conversation starter with agent partners. Ask whether their brokerage plans to join Open Real Estate, how they feel about private listings, and whether they have lost a buyer to a listing they could not see.
