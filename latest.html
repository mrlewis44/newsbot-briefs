So-what: Rates can fall two ways: lower Treasury yields or tighter mortgage spreads. Buyers do not need to wait for both to act.

1-minute read

HousingWire's weekend tracker says mortgage rates fell for a third straight week and housing demand steadied. Freddie Mac's 30-year averaged 7.18% on Thursday, and HousingWire put the rate flat at 7.17% on Friday.

Logan Mohtashami says the 10-year yield topped at 5.35% in early October and has since dropped toward 4.82%. He would not get excited until it breaks under 4.70% with follow-through bond buying.

The spread between the 10-year and mortgage rates rose to 2.27% last week from 2.23%. Historically that gap ran 1.60% to 1.80%. His point: if spreads were at those normal levels, rates would already be under 7%.

Weekly active inventory slipped to 902,740. Pending sales stabilized, which he calls stabilization rather than growth. His read is that demand grows when rates are below 6.64% and heading toward 6%, and mostly does not above 7%.

Separately, Tom Lawler at Calculated Risk reports the current-coupon MBS yield fell to 5.93% on Friday, the lowest since September 9 and 50 basis points below the October 5 high. Spreads to Treasuries tightened 6 to 7 bp on the week but stay wider than most of 2026.

For clients: rates have improved without a crash in demand. A move under 7% needs help from either yields or spreads.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is the full picture from HousingWire's Monday Daily Update, which leads with Logan Mohtashami's weekend housing tracker.

The rate story. Mortgage rates have fallen three weeks in a row. Freddie Mac's survey showed 7.18% for the 30-year on Thursday. HousingWire put the rate flat at 7.17% on Friday even after the strong jobs report. Mohtashami calls the move in the 10-year yield a nice reversal. The 10-year topped at 5.35% in early October, a level that has now held as the ceiling, and dropped toward 4.82%. He says the head-and-shoulders pattern in the 10-year chart is still somewhat intact, and a fight is happening over whether it breaks lower.

His line in the sand is 4.70%. Until the 10-year closes under that level and gets follow-through buying, he does not want to get too excited about rates. That is a useful number for clients who ask when rates will really drop.

Spreads. This is the part most people miss. The gap between the 10-year yield and the mortgage rate rose to 2.27% last week from 2.23%. In normal markets that gap has been 1.60% to 1.80%. Spreads have worsened recently, which he says is typical after a big reversal in yields because lenders and investors try to compress mortgage-rate volatility. But he also notes spreads are still near multiyear lows for this year. If they were not, he says, rates could not be below 7.25% and demand would not have stabilized. His math is that rates would be sub-7% if spreads returned to this year's best levels.

Lawler's note at Calculated Risk supports that. The current-coupon MBS yield fell to 5.93% Friday, its lowest since September 9, and 50 basis points under this year's October 5 high. Its low for 2026 was 4.81% at the end of February. Spreads to Treasuries fell by 6 to 7 bp last week but remain wider than during most of the year. Implied interest-rate volatility also dipped but is still higher than most of this year. Lower volatility is what lets spreads tighten.

Demand. Weekly pending sales, which take 30 to 60 days to show up in closed data, stabilized for three weeks. Mohtashami is clear this is not growth. It is stabilization from the slowdown when rates approached 7.60%. His observation from nearly four years of data is that demand grows when rates are under 6.64% and heading toward 6%. Above 7%, there is not much growth beyond seasonal demand. Purchase applications, which look 30 to 90 days out, softened when rates went above 6.64% and had a small weekly decline last week. Year-over-year comparisons will stay hard because last year at this time rates were heading toward 6%.

Inventory and listings. Weekly active inventory fell to 902,740. Growth in inventory has been tame this year, with some weeks down year over year, but it picked up when rates rose toward 7.60%. New listings are in their normal seasonal decline. 2026 has been the healthiest new-listings year since 2022, with more than 80,000 new listings a week a few times. His worry that high rates would freeze sellers, as happened in the second half of 2022, has not happened in any meaningful way.

Politics. He adds that the midterms changed control of the House and Senate and add a variable to rates, and he discussed what Congress can do to help lower mortgage rates on the HousingWire Daily podcast with Sarah Wheeler.

How to use this. For buyers, the message is practical. Do not hold out for sub-7% if the home works now. Rates can be refinanced later, and the bond market is not offering a sure thing. For the show, the spread story is a good teaching moment: two numbers, the 10-year and the spread, explain most of the rate you see.

It matters for mortgage pricing because Fed independence is one of the things that supports long-term bond demand.

Right now the 10-year is following the AI trade, and the Fed odds are following the same thing. So the cleanest message for clients is that rates are being driven by a market storyline we cannot predict, and that we price the loan from live sheets, not forecasts.

For the show. A good segment: the Fed hiked in September, inflation is 3.6% on the Fed's preferred measure, and jobs are strong, yet mortgage rates fell three weeks in a row. Explain why. It is a way to cut through the idea that a hot economy always means higher rates in the short run.
