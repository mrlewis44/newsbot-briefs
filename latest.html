So-what: A December hike is fully priced, so it will not move mortgage rates much. What matters is whether the Fed keeps hiking into 2027, and that debate just got louder.

1-minute read

CNBC reports that Fed Chair Kevin Warsh has avoided signaling where rates are headed in his first six months. His colleagues have filled the gap. Bank of America counted more Fedspeak in the past two weeks than any similar stretch since early 2025. BofA's Aditya Bhave said Warsh's silence may be pushing the rest of the committee to speak "more often and more forcefully."

Markets read it as hawkish. CME FedWatch implies a 100% chance of at least a quarter-point hike in December, with a small chance of a half point, and high odds of another hike in January. But five-year forward inflation expectations from the Treasury market are at their lowest since April.

Peter Boockvar pushed back this morning. With the AI trade selling off and metals and oil prices falling, he does not see how the Fed hikes again after December. He noted the data center build is about half of GDP growth and drives about 75% of S&P earnings growth. If he is right, short-term rates fall, while long-term rates could face a deficit problem.

This is the policy backdrop behind every rate sheet right now.

The CNBC piece lays out a Fed without a clear lead voice. Warsh has argued forward guidance "handcuffs" policymakers before they need to decide. He calls the internal debate a "family fight." Meanwhile, Governor Christopher Waller and St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem have floated faster hikes. Cleveland's Beth Hammack, who dissented in October for a hike, prefaced her outlook by saying, "Those are not promises... They're not forward guidance." BofA's Bhave said the October pause "increasingly looks like a mistake" and warned the public back-and-forth is causing "undue rates volatility."

What is priced: a December hike is essentially certain, with a follow-up in January likely and perhaps one more before the end of 2027. Current inflation runs above 3%. That is why a hike next month will not, by itself, push mortgage rates higher. It is already in the price.

Boockvar's case, from his Friday Boock Report, is the other side. The AI selloff is now spreading into industrial metals (Bloomberg's index hit its lowest since last December, with copper down about 18% from September) and crude. He wrote that if the data center build were not about half of GDP growth, about half the S&P 500 by market value and about 75% of earnings growth, and if upper-income consumers did not depend so much on stock market wealth, the Fed could look past it. In plain terms, a weaker economy helps the Fed cut, but bigger deficits could keep the 10-year from falling as much as short rates.

For the Wednesday show and client calls, the simple message is this: the Fed controls short-term rates, and markets control mortgage rates. This week proved it.

How to use it with clients: avoid predicting the Fed. Explain that mortgage rates follow the 10-year and MBS, not the Fed's overnight rate. Today shows that clearly. Stocks fell, the 10-year dropped under 5%, and mortgage pricing improved even with a December hike fully expected.

3) Mortgage delinquencies rise to 4.42% in Q3, and FHA foreclosures hit 1.89%, the highest since early 2017

So-what: Credit stress is building in government loans. Expect tighter overlays on low-score FHA and VA files, and more distressed listings in markets where prices are already slipping.

1-minute read

MBA's National Delinquency Survey put the delinquency rate on 1-4 unit homes at 4.42% at the end of the third quarter. That is up 5 bps from Q2 and 43 bps from a year ago. Loans 90-plus days late or in foreclosure rose for the fifth straight quarter and are 51 bps higher than a year ago.

Government loans are driving it. The FHA foreclosure rate of 1.89% is the highest since Q1 2017. VA's 1.06% is the highest since Q4 2016. FHA's delinquency rate is 11.74%, up 96 bps from a year ago. VA is at 5.00%, up 50 bps. Conventional is 2.73%, up only 11 bps.

MBA's Marina Walsh tied it to wages lagging inflation, falling savings, rising credit card, auto and student debt, and the end of COVID-era relief options. She also pointed to recent buyers who borrowed at higher rates. Unemployment was 4.1% in October.

For originators, the takeaway is to expect more scrutiny on thin FHA and VA files, and to talk about reserves early.

Expand 3-minute read

This is primary data, and it matters for both underwriting and the listing market.

Headline numbers from MBA (released Thursday, Nov. 19): the seasonally adjusted delinquency rate was 4.42% of loans outstanding. By stage, 30-day delinquencies rose 4 bps to 2.25%, 60-day rose 1 bp to 0.74%, and 90-day was flat at 1.43%. Foreclosure starts rose 1 bp to 0.21%. Loans in the foreclosure process were 0.71%, up 4 bps from Q2 and 21 bps from a year ago.

The seriously delinquent rate, loans 90 days late or in foreclosure, was 2.12% (not seasonally adjusted). It rose 6 bps from Q2 and 51 bps from a year ago. By loan type versus a year ago, serious delinquency rose 13 bps for conventional, 197 bps for FHA and 60 bps for VA. That FHA number is the one to remember. Nearly two percentage points more FHA borrowers are seriously behind than a year ago.

By loan type, total delinquency: conventional 2.73% (+1 bp from Q2), FHA 11.74% (down 5 bps from Q2 but up 96 bps from a year ago), and VA 5.00% (+11 bps from Q2, +50 bps from a year ago). The five states with the biggest quarterly jumps were Wyoming (+72 bps), West Virginia, Louisiana, Arkansas (+31) and Kentucky (+27).

Walsh's explanation is worth repeating to clients and agents. Delinquencies track unemployment, which is 4.1%. The job market is holding up, but wage growth lags inflation, the savings rate is falling and non-housing debt is rising. Borrowers who bought recently at higher rates have thinner cushions. And the removal of COVID-era relief options means fewer ways to catch up before foreclosure.

What this means for your business. First, investors and wholesale lenders tend to respond to rising FHA losses with tighter credit overlays, especially on low scores and high debt-to-income files. Watch for changes. Second, rising foreclosures matter more where prices are already falling, like parts of Florida and Texas. That is where distressed supply can add pressure. Third, the FHA insurance fund is still strong at a 10.29% capital ratio, so this is not a crisis. But it gives HUD a reason to be cautious about the industry's push to cut FHA premiums.

A note on the HousingWire view. A separate HousingWire analysis this week argued that, unlike 2008, rising delinquencies are not yet pushing national prices down, because the distressed pipeline is much smaller. Foreclosures are still far below 2008 to 2010 levels. The risk is local. Where prices fall and equity is thin, the two can start feeding each other.

In California, strong equity means most struggling owners can sell rather than face foreclosure. That keeps distressed supply low here even as the national numbers rise.

For buyers, the practical point is simple. Payment shock is real, and these numbers show it. Build reserves into the plan, and do not stretch to the max approval just because the AUS says yes.

4) ResiClub and ICE: 2.1% of U.S. mortgages are underwater, up from 1.0% in April 2025, with Cape Coral at 11.2% and Austin at 8.4%

So-what: Negative equity is still rare nationally and almost nonexistent in coastal California. But it is climbing fast among 2022 to 2026 buyers, especially low-down-payment FHA and VA loans in the Sun Belt.

1-minute read

Lance Lambert's ResiClub got new ICE Mortgage Technology data. At the end of September, 2.1% of outstanding U.S. mortgages had negative equity. That compares with 1.5% in May and 1.0% in April 2025. At the 2009 peak, it was 23.0%.

The worst metros are in Florida and Texas: Cape Coral 11.2%, Lakeland 8.9%, Austin 8.4%, San Antonio 7.4% and Tampa 6.1%. Phoenix and Tucson are at 3.7% and 3.5%.

California is at the other end. Los Angeles is 0.3%, San Jose 0.2%, San Francisco 0.7%, Oxnard 0.7% and San Diego 0.9%. Lambert says the damage is concentrated in the 2022 through 2026 loan vintages and in thin-equity FHA and VA borrowers who put as little as 3.5% down. Nearly half of all borrowers, 49.9%, still have rates under 4%.

For California clients, the takeaway is that equity is still strong here, which supports HELOC and cash-out conversations. In Sun Belt states we serve, thin-equity buyers need a frank talk about price risk.

Expand 3-minute read

This is a useful counterpoint to doom headlines, and a warning at the same time.

Why so few borrowers are underwater nationally, per Lambert. First, national existing-home prices are still close to all-time highs even though parts of the West, Southwest and Southeast have fallen from their 2022 peaks. Second, borrowers who locked in 2% to 3% rates have been paying down principal faster, because more of each payment goes to principal at low rates. As of Q1 2026, 49.9% of mortgage holders still had rates below 4%. Third, few people in correction markets actually bought at the very top in spring 2022.

The trend is still up. ICE's apples-to-apples series went from 1.0% in April 2025 to 1.5% in May 2026 to 2.1% in September 2026. And the price corrections in some places are large. On the Zillow Home Value Index, Cape Coral-Fort Myers is down 19.5% from its peak and Austin 28.8%.

The top 15 underwater metros: Cape Coral 11.2%, Lakeland 8.9%, Austin 8.4%, San Antonio 7.4%, Tampa 6.1%, Colorado Springs 5.7%, North Port 5.4%, Baton Rouge 4.8%, Jacksonville 4.8%, Dallas 4.0%, Orlando 3.7%, Palm Bay 3.7%, Phoenix 3.7%, Tucson 3.5% and New Orleans 3.5%.

The 15 lowest include several California markets: San Jose 0.2%, Los Angeles 0.3%, Oxnard 0.7%, San Francisco 0.7% and San Diego 0.9%, alongside Bridgeport, New York, Boston and Madison.

For context, in September 2009, 68% of Nevada borrowers, 48% in Arizona and 45% in Florida were underwater. Today's worst metro, Cape Coral, is far from that.

Lambert's key point is about who is affected. In down markets, it is mainly the 2022 to 2026 vintages, and especially FHA and VA borrowers who put down as little as 3.5%. That links directly to the MBA delinquency data above. Thin equity plus rising delinquency is the combination that turns a soft market into a distressed one.

Lambert also stresses that being underwater is not the same as being in distress. A borrower who owes more than the home is worth but can still make the payment usually just keeps paying. Trouble starts when negative equity meets a job loss, a divorce or a payment shock. That is why the combination with today's MBA delinquency numbers matters more than either one alone.

For agents in those Sun Belt markets, underwater sellers often cannot list without bringing cash to closing. That limits resale supply in some places and can push sellers toward short sales or renting the home out instead.

For our clients: in California, equity remains a major cushion, which supports HELOCs and cash-out conversations. In Arizona, Texas, Florida and other states we serve, low-down-payment buyers need a clear-eyed talk about what happens if prices drop 5% to 10%. A larger down payment, or a plan to stay longer, lowers that risk. This also makes a solid Wednesday show segment on what "underwater" means and why it is rare.

5) New-home purchase applications fell 13.6% from a year ago in October, the fourth straight annual drop, even with builder incentives

So-what: Builders are cutting prices and buying down rates, and buyers are still waiting. That keeps builder financing deals on the table for clients who are ready.

1-minute read

MBA's Builder Application Survey showed new-home purchase mortgage applications down 13.6% from October 2025 and down 7% from September, before seasonal adjustment. It was the fourth straight month of annual declines.

MBA's Joel Kan said the 30-year rate rose about 60 bps over two months to 7.5%, and many buyers continue to "hold off on their purchases" despite incentives. MBA estimated new single-family sales at a 592,000 annual pace in October, up 1.4% from September but still the weakest two-month stretch since July 2022.

The loan mix leans government: conventional 47.9%, FHA 36.9%, VA 14.1% and USDA 1.0%. The average new-home loan fell to $368,934 from $374,724. This lines up with Tuesday's NAHB index at 25, the lowest since April 2012, with 46% of builders cutting prices and 68% using incentives.

For buyers shopping new construction right now, the leverage is on their side. Builders need sales, and many will negotiate on price, closing costs and rate together.

Expand 3-minute read

This is the builder channel's version of the demand problem we have seen all week.

The numbers, from MBA via Mortgage Professional America: new-home purchase applications were down 13.6% year over year in October and down 7% from September, unadjusted. MBA estimated new single-family home sales at a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 592,000, up from 584,000 in September. On an unadjusted basis, about 44,000 new homes sold in October, down 6.4% from 47,000. August had already marked the 2026 low for new-home applications.

Kan pinned it on rates. The 30-year fixed rose roughly 60 bps in two months to around 7.5%. Buyers who could qualify in September are now short, or simply choosing to wait.

Loan type matters here. Nearly 37% of builder-channel applications were FHA and another 14% were VA. That is a heavier government share than the resale market, and it ties back to today's MBA delinquency report showing FHA and VA stress. The average new-home loan size also dropped to about $369,000, which suggests buyers are going smaller or builders are cutting prices.

NAHB's November index fell four points to 25, the lowest since April 2012. 46% of builders cut prices, the highest share since the pandemic, and 68% used sales incentives. NAHB's Robert Dietz said rates above 7% and an uncertain economy are "holding buyers back."

Separately, Scotsman Guide reported that builder-financed buyers continue to get below-market rates. Builders and lenders use Fannie and Freddie forward commitments, where each side takes a haircut to offer discounted rates on new homes. That gives builders an edge over resale sellers.

Not everyone likes it. Aequitas Mortgage CEO Darshit Chokshi has argued builder incentives mask the real problem: "New build homes are still priced very high."

One practical point on FHA and VA in the builder channel. With government loans making up more than half of new-home applications, a buyer using a builder's lender should compare the full cost, including upfront MI or the VA funding fee, with an outside quote. Builder buydowns can look great on the rate but carry a higher price that affects the appraisal and the long-term cost.

And with new-home sales at the weakest two-month pace since July 2022, buyers have more choice and more room to negotiate than in most resale markets.

For our clients: if a buyer is shopping new construction, compare the builder's rate buydown against a price cut used for a permanent buydown with an outside lender. Check whether the builder rate requires their lender and whether the price is inflated to pay for it. In a market this soft, a buyer can often negotiate both a price cut and closing-cost help.

6) Broker Action Coalition wants lender-paid broker comp pulled out of the QM 3% points-and-fees cap, the same way retail LO pay is treated

So-what: This is a direct fix for brokers. Today, wholesale comp counts toward the cap and retail LO pay does not, which can knock smaller broker loans out of QM.

1-minute read

The Broker Action Coalition released a white paper asking Congress and the CFPB to exclude lender-paid broker compensation from the Qualified Mortgage points-and-fees test. Under the rule, points and fees generally cannot exceed 3% on loans of $137,958 or more in 2026, with tighter limits on smaller loans.

Right now, comp a wholesale lender pays a broker counts toward that cap. Comp a retail lender pays its own employee LO does not. BAC says both are funded through the rate and look the same to the borrower. The gap leaves brokers less room for other fees and can push otherwise good loans out of QM, losing its liability protections.

BAC says small-balance and rural loans are hit hardest. It cites research showing purchase loans under $70,000 fell from more than 11% of the market in 2011 to under 4% in 2020. The fix would apply only to rate-funded comp paid under a compliant LO comp agreement.

For our shop, the practical step today is to watch points and fees closely on smaller loans, where the cap bites first.

Expand 3-minute read

This one is squarely in our lane as a broker shop.

The problem, per HousingWire's summary of the white paper: the CFPB's 2013 Ability-to-Repay/QM rule set the points-and-fees cap. For 2026, the limit is 3% of the loan amount for loans of $137,958 or more, with graduated percentage and dollar limits below that. When a wholesale lender pays a broker, that comp counts toward the 3%. When a retail lender pays its own loan officer, it does not.

BAC argues there is no real difference from the borrower's side. Both are built into the rate. But the broker version eats into the cap, leaving less room for other fees that count, like certain title or affiliate charges. On a smaller loan, a few fixed-dollar charges plus broker comp can push the file over the line. If a loan fails points and fees, it loses QM status and the legal safe harbor that comes with it. Most lenders will not fund it.

That is why BAC focuses on small-balance and rural lending. Fixed charges take up a bigger share of a small loan's cap. It cites Pew, Urban Institute and HUD research showing the share of home purchase loans under $70,000 dropped from more than 11% in 2011 to under 4% in 2020.

The CFPB's original reason for the distinction was steering. In 2013 it said counting broker comp could reduce the risk of brokers pushing borrowers into pricier loans. BAC acknowledges that, but says later rules already handle it. LO comp cannot vary based on the rate or loan terms, and a separate rule bans steering borrowers toward loans that pay the originator more.

The proposal has two tracks. The main one is an amendment to the Truth in Lending Act that would exclude rate-funded, creditor-to-broker comp from points and fees when it is paid under a compliant LO comp agreement. The parallel track asks the CFPB to make the same change in Regulation Z. The exclusion would apply only when comp is funded through the rate, not paid directly by the borrower or financed into the loan.

BAC's white paper says the change would "remove an inequitable regulatory constraint" without hurting wholesale's pricing edge.

Why it matters beyond small loans: any time a broker file runs close to the 3% cap, the broker has to cut its comp, cut fees or restructure the loan. Retail lenders do not face that tradeoff on LO pay. Removing that gap would let brokers compete on equal terms on every loan size, which is the core argument for the wholesale channel's growth.

What to watch: whether NAMB, AIME and wholesale lenders like UWM line up behind it, and whether any member of Congress files bill language. With Congress in a lame-duck session, this is more likely a 2027 effort. For now, keep tracking points and fees closely on loans under about $150,000, where the cap bites first.

7) Industry pushes FHA to cut premiums to 0.35% or bring back MI cancellation at 78%, as the Senate panel advances Matt Jones for FHA commissioner 13-11

So-what: A 20 bp cut would save about $58 a month on a $350,000 FHA loan. Nothing is decided, but it is a live talking point for first-time buyers weighing FHA versus conventional.

1-minute read

CNBC reports a fresh industry push after HUD's report showed the FHA insurance fund at a 10.29% capital ratio, down from 11.47% but more than five times the 2% minimum. MBA suggested cutting the annual premium 20 bps, from 0.55% to 0.35%. On a $350,000 loan, that is about $160 a month dropping to $102.

The other idea is automatic MI cancellation once the balance hits 78% of the original value, like conventional loans. Today, FHA borrowers with under 10% down pay MI for the life of the loan. NAMB's Michael Farrell backs cancellation and says premiums could restart if the fund falls to 2%.

Separately, the Senate Banking Committee voted 13-11 Thursday to advance Matt Jones as HUD assistant secretary for housing and FHA commissioner. Jones runs FHA single-family now and is a former MBA staffer. MBA wants him confirmed before Congress adjourns.

In plain terms: FHA is likely to stay strong, and any premium relief is a maybe, not a promise.

Expand 3-minute read

Two FHA stories that fit together.

First, the premium debate. HUD's annual report to Congress, released Monday, showed the Mutual Mortgage Insurance Fund at a 10.29% capital ratio as of Sept. 30, down from 11.47% a year earlier. That is still more than five times the 2% legal minimum. MBA CEO Bob Broeksmit said the fund's strength is "an opportunity for targeted changes that improve affordability." CNBC notes about 83% of new FHA purchase loans in the last fiscal year went to first-time buyers, and FHA insured more than 8.4 million mortgages.

How FHA MI works today: borrowers pay an upfront premium, usually 1.75% of the base loan, plus an annual premium, typically 0.55% for most 30-year loans. Under the 2013 rules, borrowers with less than 10% down pay the annual premium for the life of the loan. Those with 10% or more down pay it for 11 years. Conventional borrowers can drop PMI at 20% equity, and it ends automatically at 78% of the original value.

The two proposals. One is a rate cut. MBA suggested 20 bps, from 0.55% to 0.35%. CNBC's math on a $350,000 loan: $1,925 a year (about $160 a month) at 0.55%, versus $1,225 (about $102 a month) at 0.35%, a $58 monthly difference. FHA last cut the rate in 2023, from 0.85%. HUD's new report said that cut reduced the fund's revenue and contributed to the lower capital ratio. The second proposal is auto-cancellation at 78%, which FHA had from 2001 to 2013. NAMB's Farrell said, "Cancellation prevents the squeeze for borrowers who have paid down their loans and shouldn't be paying for coverage they have outgrown."

Whether either happens is uncertain. HUD did not respond to CNBC. And today's MBA data showing FHA foreclosures at a nine-year high gives HUD a reason to go slow.

Second, leadership. The Senate Banking Committee advanced Matt Jones 13-11 in a split vote. He would replace Frank Cassidy, who left in June, and succeed acting commissioner Joseph Gormley. Jones is HUD's deputy assistant secretary for single-family housing and previously worked at MBA, Amerifirst Home Mortgage and on Senate committee staff. Chair Tim Scott said Jones would help implement the 21st Century Road to Housing Act. Ranking member Elizabeth Warren opposed him, saying his office has "cut resources for first-time homebuyers." FHA insures roughly $2 trillion in mortgages.

One more angle for the Wednesday show. Over 80% of FHA purchase loans go to first-time buyers, and NAR says the median first-time buyer is now 40. Explaining how FHA MI works, and how to get out of it later, is exactly the kind of practical content our audience needs.

For clients: do not promise a premium cut. But when comparing FHA to conventional, remind buyers that life-of-loan FHA MI is why a refinance to conventional later is often part of the plan.

8) Five trade groups, including NAMB, ask FHFA to push the condo reserve jump from 10% to 15% back a year, to Jan. 4, 2028

So-what: If FHFA says no, some condo projects could lose Fannie and Freddie eligibility in January. Check project status early on any condo deal closing in 2027.

1-minute read

In a Nov. 19 letter to FHFA Director Bill Pulte, five groups asked FHFA to tell Fannie and Freddie to delay a planned increase in condo reserve requirements. The rule would raise minimum reserves for project reviews from 10% to 15% of the HOA's annual budgeted assessment income, starting Jan. 4, 2027.

The signers are the Community Associations Institute, Community Association Roundtable, Community Home Lenders of America, Housing Policy Council and NAMB. They support funded reserves but say HOAs run on annual budgets and cannot change midyear. Projects that miss the new bar could lose conventional GSE financing.

Fannie estimates the change would add about $13 to $14 a month to the average owner's costs. The groups said condo eligibility has already shrunk since Fannie retired limited reviews on Aug. 3.

For Southern California condo buyers, this is worth flagging early on every deal.

If FHFA keeps the January date, lenders will start checking the new reserve level on project reviews right away.

Expand 3-minute read

Condos are a big share of entry-level buying in Southern California, so this one matters locally.

What changes: for applicable project reviews, the minimum reserve allocation would rise from 10% to 15% of the association's annual budgeted assessment income. The effective date is Jan. 4, 2027. The trade groups want it moved to Jan. 4, 2028.

Their three concerns. First, timing. Many associations set budgets once a year and may need new reserve studies, board votes or owner input before changing contributions. There are also open questions about how the rule applies to existing budgets, recent reserve studies, big capital projects and alternative funding setups. Lenders, servicers and management companies need time to update systems and underwriting.

Second, eligibility. Associations that cannot meet 15%, or qualify under an alternative reserve study provision, could lose GSE eligibility. That shrinks the buyer pool, makes selling or refinancing harder for current owners and can pressure resale values. The groups said older buildings, small communities and associations with more modest-income owners would be hit hardest. Those projects would lean on non-agency or portfolio loans, which are usually pricier and harder to get.

Third, cost. Higher reserves add to monthly HOA dues at a time when owners already face higher payments, taxes, insurance and fees. Fannie's estimate is about $13 to $14 a month on average, but it varies by building.

Context: NAMB asked in June for a 12-month delay on earlier condo review and insurance standards. Fannie still retired its limited review process on Aug. 3. The groups wrote that "condominium project eligibility has already declined" since then. They want FHFA to convene stakeholders to study the impact before moving ahead.

In Orange County and Los Angeles, many older condo complexes run tight budgets. Those are the buildings most likely to miss a 15% reserve line. If the delay is denied, expect some projects to flip from warrantable to non-warrantable in early 2027, which changes the loan options, down payment and rate a buyer can get.

This is also a good topic for buyer education. A condo with weak reserves can mean special assessments later, even if the loan works today.

Timing matters for pipelines. A buyer who goes into contract in December could close after Jan. 4, when the new standard applies. Ask the lender how it will treat project approvals done before that date.

What to do now: on any condo purchase or refi likely to close after early January, ask for the HOA budget and reserve study up front. If a project looks like it will miss 15%, line up a non-QM or portfolio condo option before you need it. Non-warrantable condo programs are a real niche where brokers with broad lender access can win deals that retail shops lose.

Expand 3-minute read

This is a supply-side story, so think in years, not weeks.

What passed. The Senate approved the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, or BAAJA, 62-33. Senators Sheldon Whitehouse (D-RI), Martin Heinrich (D-NM), Shelley Moore Capito (R-WV) and Mike Lee (R-UT) introduced it. It has been attached to a larger, more popular bill and revised over several weeks. The latest version changed language to address concerns about environmental protections and Native American rights.

What it does. It streamlines permitting and limits the scope of federal reviews under NEPA and the Clean Water Act. The main target is big energy projects, including wind, solar, transmission, gas pipelines and nuclear. But housing groups say faster federal reviews also help larger housing and community projects that touch federal permits. The WSJ's version of the story described it as designed to speed up energy projects and lower consumer costs.

The numbers behind it. A White House policy paper estimates federal permitting for major infrastructure takes 4.2 years and 1,700 pages of paperwork. It projects reforms would add $82 billion to $164 billion to GDP and $14 billion to $28 billion in tax revenue, and cut average household electric bills by $178 to $251 a year. Those are administration estimates, not independent ones.

Supporters include NAHB, YIMBY Action, the Niskanen Center, Associated Builders and Contractors and the National Community Development Association. NAR's chief advocacy officer Shannon McGahn said "America's housing shortage demands solutions that make it easier and more affordable to build." President Trump called it "a BIG WIN." Sen. Lee said the bill could make "everything you buy" cheaper over time.

Opponents include the Association on American Indian Affairs and the Endangered Species Coalition. Rep. Melanie Stansbury (D-NM) said, "This is NOT the deal, NOT the time." The bill now goes to the House.

One link to watch: data centers are among the projects that would move faster. With the AI selloff calling data center spending into question, the energy and grid side of this bill may matter more than the housing side. Lower power costs over time would help household budgets, which feeds into what buyers can afford.

For the show, the clean framing is that Washington is starting to treat permitting as a cost problem. That is good news for supply over the next decade, but buyers should not wait on it.

Context from this week's data: single-family permits are near their lowest since May 2020 and NAHB confidence is at a 2012 low. Builders' main problems right now are rates and demand, not federal permits. Most housing permits are local, which this bill does not change. So this is a modest, long-run positive for supply and utility costs. It is not a fix for affordability this year.
