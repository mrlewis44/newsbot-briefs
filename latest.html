5) Freedom, Rocket and Redwood CEOs: liquidity is the top risk if rates stay higher for longer

So-what: Cash, not credit losses, sinks lenders in rising-rate cycles. Check the balance sheets of the wholesale lenders you send loans to, especially for FHA and VA.

Three big mortgage CEOs agree on the main danger right now: running out of cash.

Freedom Mortgage CEO Stan Middleman said liquidity risk should be a top concern across the industry. "Rising interest rates create a whole different set of issues when it comes to liquidity than falling interest rates," he said on a Housing Finance Strategies webinar. He said many firms failed in past cycles not from credit losses but because they "didn't have cash on hand."

The pressure points are warehouse lines, repo funding and the cost of financing mortgage bonds. Ginnie Mae servicers face extra strain because FHA and VA loans have higher delinquencies. When borrowers miss payments, servicers must advance the money to bondholders.

Rocket CEO Jay Bray said liquidity is "something we should all be thinking about." Rocket is investing heavily in AI and expects rates to stay elevated longer. "The world is going to consolidate," Bray said. Redwood CEO Chris Abate said his firm has done 35 securitizations this year, more than ever.

All three expect more consolidation and plan to buy portfolios or companies.

So what: Strong players will grow. Weaker ones may pull back from FHA and VA or disappear.

HousingWire covered a Housing Finance Strategies webinar Tuesday where three large mortgage CEOs talked frankly about risk. Their shared message: liquidity, meaning cash on hand and access to funding, is the biggest threat if rates stay high.

Freedom Mortgage. Founder and CEO Stan Middleman said liquidity should be a top concern for the whole sector, especially when rates are rising. "Rising interest rates create a whole different set of issues when it comes to liquidity than falling interest rates," he said. He told a story from the financial crisis, when his JPMorgan Chase representative asked him not to close loans at quarter end because the bank needed liquidity. "When liquidity at JPMorgan is tight, that makes the rest of us have a really good reason to worry," he said, "and that truth remains so today."

Middleman listed the pressure points. They go beyond servicing advances to warehouse lines, which fund loans before they are sold, and repo facilities, which are short-term loans backed by mortgage assets. They also include the cost of financing mortgage-backed securities. He said many lenders that failed in past cycles did so because they "didn't have cash on hand to meet their requirements." He is preparing his team for a lower origination environment and plans to buy assets or companies as they become available.

Why FHA and VA matter here. Ginnie Mae servicers must advance payments to bondholders when borrowers fall behind. Government loans have higher delinquency rates, so those advances rise and strain operating cash. Industry group Community Home Lenders of America has proposed a Ginnie Mae liquidity backstop, called G-TALF, to address this risk.

Redwood Trust. CEO Chris Abate called the backdrop "challenging" for liquidity and capital, but said it creates room for well-capitalized players. Redwood has done 35 securitizations this year, its most ever. That matters for non-QM and jumbo, since securitization is how many of those loans get funded.

Rocket. CEO Jay Bray said 2026 brought market share gains after the Redfin and Mr. Cooper deals, but Rocket is now planning for rates to stay high longer than many expected. "Liquidity is something we should all be thinking about, and probably talking less about some of these other issues and focus more on that, candidly," he said. Rocket is investing heavily in AI to cut costs and compete.

All three described the same playbook: protect the balance sheet, diversify funding, invest in technology, and buy portfolios or companies when they come up for sale.

So what for a broker: Your wholesale lenders are only as reliable as their funding. Watch for warning signs like sudden pricing jumps on FHA and VA, slower funding times, or overlay changes. Spread your government loans across more than one strong lender. If a lender exits, you want your pipeline to have a home.

6) Ex-FHFA head Sandra Thompson questions putting VantageScore and FICO on one pricing grid: a 720 is not always a 720

So-what: If investors cannot see the risk clearly, they price it in, and borrowers pay through higher rates. Credit score rules may keep shifting.

1-minute read

Former FHFA Director Sandra Thompson is pushing back on how the agency now prices loans by credit score.

Some background. Under Director Bill Pulte, FHFA approved VantageScore 4.0 for all loans sold to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. It first kept separate pricing grids for Classic FICO and VantageScore, then moved to one shared grid based on Classic FICO. Pulte said separate grids made "zero sense."

Thompson disagrees. "A 740 classic FICO isn't necessarily the same risk as a 740 VantageScore loan," she said on a Housing Finance Strategies webinar. She said she wants to see the analysis behind the common grid.

Former Ginnie Mae President Alanna McCargo agreed. "If the loan carries a VantageScore 720 doesn't mean the same thing as a FICO 720," she said. When investors cannot see risk clearly, "you price it in, and then the borrowers are paying for it through a higher rate."

Thompson also said she would have made bi-merge credit, two bureaus instead of three, an option rather than a full replacement.

So what: For now, lenders can pick the score that helps a borrower most. Critics say that could get revisited, so do not assume today's score rules are permanent.

Expand 3-minute read

This story is about how loans are priced by credit score, which affects nearly every conventional file. Here is the background and the debate, in plain English.

What changed. For decades, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac relied on Classic FICO. Under former Director Sandra Thompson, FHFA began studying new score models and planned to move away from the old FICO and from the tri-merge credit report, which pulls all three bureaus. Implementation was delayed. Under Director Bill Pulte, FHFA approved VantageScore 4.0 for all single-family loans sold to the GSEs and signaled future acceptance of FICO 10T.

The pricing grid. Loan-level price adjustments, or LLPAs, are the fees Fannie and Freddie charge based on credit score, down payment and other risk factors. FHFA first kept separate LLPA grids for Classic FICO and VantageScore 4.0. Then it moved to one unified grid based on Classic FICO. Pulte said keeping separate grids "makes zero sense" and pointed to feedback from lenders and consumers.

Thompson's concern. Speaking on a Housing Finance Strategies webinar Tuesday, Thompson said getting risk pricing right is the most important part of credit score modernization. "A 740 classic FICO isn't necessarily the same risk as a 740 VantageScore loan," she said. "Different models can rank and measure borrowers differently, and we just need to know whether the same numerical score warrants the same price." She added, "I'd want to see the analysis behind the common grid, and that's the one thing that's upsetting me." When lenders can choose the model, she asked whether anyone is measuring the effect of those choices across the GSE portfolios. Fannie and Freddie, she noted, "are on the hook for any losses."

A second voice. Alanna McCargo, former Ginnie Mae president, said the core issue is borrower creditworthiness, not just adding more score products. "If the loan carries a VantageScore 720 doesn't mean the same thing as a FICO 720; investors need to understand what that means underneath, and when you can't see the risk clearly, you price it in, and then the borrowers are paying for it through a higher rate," she said.

Bi-merge. FHFA has also signaled a move from tri-merge to a bi-merge option, pulling two bureaus instead of three, to cut costs. Thompson said under her plan bi-merge would have been an option, not a full replacement, so lenders could still pull three reports when they wanted.

How this connects to recent news. Earlier this week we covered FICO's $10 score deal, which requires lenders to pull FICO on every application. Score choice, score cost and score pricing are all moving at once.

So what for originators: Today, the unified grid can help some borrowers whose VantageScore is higher than their FICO. That is a real tool for first-time buyers with thin files. But critics with influence are questioning it, and investor pricing of MBS could reflect any doubt about score quality. Keep running both scores where allowed, document why you chose one, and do not promise clients that the current rules will last.

First American Data and Analytics published its first report on home equity and rate lock-in Wednesday. It puts numbers on something every loan officer feels: owners are rich in equity and stuck on rate.

The headline number. Accessible home equity reached a record $13.2 trillion in the second quarter of 2026. That is up 1.9% from the first quarter and 1.5% from a year earlier. First American defines accessible equity as the amount an owner could borrow while keeping the combined loan-to-value at or below 80%.

The lock-in. About $11.6 trillion of that equity, roughly 88%, is held by owners whose first mortgage rate is below the national average. First American used 6.49% as the average 30-year rate at the end of June. HousingWire pointed out that rates have risen nearly a full percentage point since then, so the lock-in today is likely deeper. First American also found that 87% of locked-in equity falls in its "severe" or "extreme" categories, and 83% is "extreme," meaning the owner's rate is more than 1.5 percentage points below market.

Senior economist Genaro Villa explained why. Many owners got record-low rates in 2020 and 2021 and then watched their equity surge during the pandemic price boom. First American said that mix explains thin listing inventory, muted cash-out refinance volume and growing interest in products that unlock equity without disturbing a low first-lien rate.

State by state. Every state has at least 74% of its accessible equity locked in. The lowest shares are Connecticut at 74.6%, Massachusetts at 76.7% and Rhode Island at 77.7%. Florida (82%) and Georgia (82.6%) are also among the 10 lowest. The highest is South Dakota at 98%, followed by Washington at 93.3%, Wisconsin at 92.4%, Colorado at 92% and Minnesota at 91.9%. California sits at 91.7%, tied with Hawaii, North Carolina and Montana.

The report says high lock-in states tilt toward HELOCs, closed-end seconds or renovation loans that preserve the first lien. Lower lock-in states may see more willingness to list, move or do a cash-out refinance.

Reverse mortgages. Traditional FHA-insured reverse loans are slumping. The National Reverse Mortgage Lenders Association says HECM endorsements were 24,182 in fiscal 2026, the lowest since 2003. Private, non-FHA reverse loans are filling the gap. New View Advisors estimated $3.5 billion in proprietary reverse volume in the first three quarters of 2026, up 46%, versus $2.7 billion in HECM volume. Proprietary loans took 60% of the market in September. Fairway's Christine Jensen suggested FHA could cut the 2% upfront premium, which she says keeps many seniors away. Seniors 62 and older held a record $15.34 trillion in home equity, per NRMLA and RiskSpan.

So what for a broker: The equity story is the best lead source right now. In California, more than nine in ten dollars of borrowable equity sit behind a rate that is below market. A cash-out refi asks those owners to give up that rate. A HELOC or closed-end second does not. For clients 55 and older, a proprietary reverse loan may be worth a conversation, especially in high-value markets.

8) Sellers cut prices: 21% of September listings had a reduction, a four-year high; median list price fell 1.2% to $419,250

So-what: Buyers have leverage again. Pair price cuts with seller credits for rate buydowns to make payments work at 7%-plus rates.

1-minute read

Sellers are losing leverage, and the numbers show it.

Realtor.com says the national median list price fell 1.2% from a year earlier in September to $419,250. That is the 11th straight monthly decline. About 21% of listings had a price cut, the most in four years, per the AP story in the LA Times.

Redfin found about 63% more sellers than buyers across the 50 largest metros last month. Buyers numbered roughly 929,000, the fewest in records going back to 2013. Sellers hit their highest count since 2020.
