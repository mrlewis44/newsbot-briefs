Wed show

So-what: Pricing is steady into Wednesday. Floating now carries two-way risk, and the Fed statement at 11 AM PT is the next big swing.

1-minute read

Mortgage rates went nowhere on Monday, and that was a small win. The Mortgage News Daily index held at 7.33% for a 30-year fixed, just a hair above the lowest level in more than a month.

Bonds had a reason to sell off and didn't. Tech stocks bounced to their best close since last Wednesday. Lately, a tech rebound has meant money leaving bonds, which pushes rates up. This time bonds held. UMBS 6.0, the mortgage bond that drives most 30-year pricing, closed Monday at 99.50, up 0.09.

Tuesday morning is a touch softer. At 7:07 AM PT, UMBS 6.0 was 99.45, down 0.02, and the 10-year Treasury yield was about 5.00%, up roughly 1 basis point (0.01%). A stronger durable goods report gave yields a small bump.

So what: Rate sheets today should look a lot like Monday's. Clients close to locking should decide now how much Wednesday risk they want to carry.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is the simple version of where rates stand. The average 30-year fixed is 7.33% on the Mortgage News Daily index, unchanged Monday. The 52-week range on that index runs from 5.99% to 7.61%, so we are much closer to the top than the bottom. Freddie Mac's weekly survey from last Thursday was 7.32%. Other MND products on Monday: 15-year 6.99%, FHA 6.96%, VA 6.98%, jumbo 7.60%, and the 7/6 SOFR ARM at 6.82%, up 0.07.

Why did rates hold Monday? Matt Graham at MBS Live called it an exceptionally uneventful day with no major data. The one thing to watch was tech. Tech stocks bounced to their best close since last Wednesday, and bond yields for CoreWeave, a company that rents out AI computing power, moved slightly lower. Graham wrote that on most recent occasions that would have meant upward pressure on rates. Instead, bonds went sideways. His words: "today's sideways performance was a victory."

The tech link matters, so here it is in plain terms. In mid-October, worries about AI companies sent investors out of those stocks and risky AI-related bonds and into safe Treasuries and mortgage bonds. More demand for mortgage bonds means lower rates. Mortgage News Daily adds a twist: tech corporate bonds had been pulling money away from mortgage bonds, so when those tech bonds fell out of favor, mortgage bonds benefited.

On Tuesday morning MBS Live introduced a new term: "neocloud." That is shorthand for the group of AI cloud companies like CoreWeave. Their slide leveled off last Thursday, which is the same day bond yields bottomed. This morning those stocks are up sharply and their bond yields are falling. That rebound is a mild headwind for mortgage rates. At 7:07 AM PT, UMBS 6.0 was 99.45, down 0.02, and the 10-year yield was 5.001%, up 0.014. The 2-year was 4.742%.

MBS Live's lock or float view from Monday: rates held despite some healing in tech, which bodes well for the range holding ahead of Wednesday. Floors sit at 5.07%, 5.00%, 4.93% and 4.83%. We are sitting right on the 5.00% line.

The historical normal is about 1.60% to 1.80%. A wide spread means mortgage rates carry extra cushion that could shrink later, but it is not shrinking yet.

2) Fed decision Wednesday 11 AM PT: about 90% odds of a hold, but a December hike is priced near 82%

Wed show

So-what: A hold is expected. The rate story for buyers is the tone: firm talk about December could lift rates, patient talk could help.

1-minute read

The Fed began its two-day meeting this morning. The decision comes Wednesday at 11 AM PT, with Chair Kevin Warsh's press conference at 11:30 AM PT.

Markets expect no change. CME FedWatch, which turns futures prices into odds, shows about a 90% chance the Fed holds its key rate at 3.75% to 4.00%. That leaves roughly a 10% to 11% chance of a hike this week. The bigger number is December. Reuters, citing FedWatch, put the odds of a December hike at about 82%.

Why a hold if inflation is firm? The Wall Street Journal says officials have called for patience, and the midterm elections are next Tuesday. That makes a surprise hike hard to pull off. Some economists still say the data support a hike. BMO expects at least one dissent, and TD Securities analysts expect three.

Keep in mind, the Fed sets a short-term rate, not mortgage rates. Mortgage rates follow longer-term bond yields, which react to the Fed's message about the future.

So what: Wednesday is about tone. Tell clients the headline will likely be "no change," but rates can still move that afternoon.

Expand 3-minute read

The setup for Wednesday is unusual. The Fed raised rates by a quarter point in September, a unanimous 12 to 0 vote and its first hike since July 2023. That put the federal funds range at 3.75% to 4.00%. Since then, inflation has stayed warm. September consumer prices rose 3.7% from a year earlier, per the Labor Department. Core prices, which strip out food and energy, rose 2.6%. Hiring was weak, with just 29,000 jobs added in September, and unemployment at 4.2%. But layoffs remain low. Weekly jobless claims were 194,000 for the week ending October 17.

That mix is why the market expects a pause now and more pressure later. CME FedWatch put hold odds near 90% on Tuesday morning, per CNBC. Polymarket bettors put no change at 94%. Reuters, as cited by see.news, had odds of an October hike near 11% and a December hike near 82%.

The Wall Street Journal's Nick Timiraos laid out why a hold is likely even though there is a case for raising. Officials have called for patience. The midterms are November 3. A hike just days before a national election would invite claims that the Fed is playing politics in one direction or the other. Several officials, including New York Fed President John Williams and Vice Chair Philip Jefferson, have signaled a wait-and-see approach.

Not everyone agrees. Kathy Bostjancic, chief economist at Nationwide, said, "The data, in my opinion, do support a rate hike." BMO's Scott Anderson expects at least one dissent, likely from Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, and he expects two more hikes. Semafor reported that TD Securities analysts predict three dissents in favor of hiking. A split vote would be a hawkish signal, meaning it would tilt toward higher rates.

There is also a style question. The Journal notes Warsh has said he wants the Fed to stop previewing its moves. That means the statement could be short, and investors will lean hard on every word in the press conference. If Warsh keeps December alive with firm language, bond yields could rise. If he sounds patient, yields could ease.

Here is the part clients often get wrong. The Fed does not set mortgage rates. It sets an overnight rate banks charge each other. Mortgage rates track the 10-year Treasury and mortgage bond prices, which move on what investors expect over years, not days. A hike can even lower long rates if markets think it will crush inflation faster. A hold can raise long rates if markets think the Fed is behind.

What comes next: Thursday at 5:30 AM PT brings September PCE inflation, the Fed's preferred measure, with forecasts near 3.6% from 3.4%, plus the first read on third-quarter GDP. The October jobs report follows on November 6.

So what: For the Wednesday show, frame the Fed as the headline but not the whole story. The likely outcome is no change. The real question is whether Warsh signals December. That tone, plus Thursday's inflation data, will decide whether this month's rate relief holds.

3) New home sales 676,000 in September; median price $400,400, down 3.9% from a year ago

Wed show

So-what: Builders still have 8.4 months of supply and lower prices than last year. That means room to negotiate incentives for first-time and move-up buyers.

1-minute read

New home sales came in a bit better than expected this morning. The Census Bureau said sales of new single-family homes ran at a 676,000 annual pace in September. That is down 0.6% from August's revised 680,000 and down 5.3% from a year ago. Forecasters expected about 650,000.

The price side is the part buyers will care about. The median new home sold for $400,400. That is up 3.9% from August but down 3.9% from September 2025, when it was $416,800. The average price was $472,800, down 6.7% from a year ago.

Supply is still heavy. Builders had 476,000 new homes for sale at the end of September. At the current pace, that is 8.4 months of supply. Around six months is usually considered balanced, so builders have more homes than buyers.

One caution: this report swings a lot. The Census Bureau's margin of error on the monthly change is plus or minus 13.8%, so the small dip is basically flat.

So what: Builders are sitting on inventory with rates above 7%. Buyers who are open to new construction should ask about rate buydowns and closing cost credits.

Expand 3-minute read

Here are the September numbers in one place. New single-family home sales ran at a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 676,000. Seasonally adjusted means the figure is smoothed for normal seasonal swings, and annual rate means it shows what a full year would look like at this month's pace. August was revised down to 680,000 from the 684,000 first reported. Compared with September 2025's 714,000, sales were down 5.3%. The forecast going in, per the MBS Live calendar, was about 650,000, so this beat expectations.

Prices tell a softer story. The median sale price, the middle of all sales, was $400,400. That is up 3.9% from August's $385,400 but down 3.9% from a year earlier. The average price was $472,800, down 6.7% from $506,800. When the average falls more than the median, it often means fewer expensive homes are selling, or builders are shrinking homes and cutting prices at the top end.

Inventory is the key to bargaining power. Builders had 476,000 new homes for sale at the end of September, down 1.0% from August's 481,000 and down 2.3% from a year ago. At the current sales pace, that is 8.4 months of supply, compared with 8.5 months in August and 8.2 months a year ago. Six months is the rough line between a balanced market and a buyer's market. Above that, sellers usually have to compete harder.

Builders compete differently than resale sellers. Instead of cutting the list price, many offer to pay down the buyer's mortgage rate or cover closing costs. Last week we covered PulteGroup spending 10.4% of the home price on incentives. Large builders with in-house lenders can buy a rate down by a point or more, which can matter more for the monthly payment than a price cut of the same cost.

The context: the Mortgage News Daily average for a 30-year fixed was 7.33% on Monday. In a 7% world, a builder buydown that gets a buyer into the 5s or low 6s for the first years can be the difference between qualifying and not.

A caution on reading this report. New home sales are one of the noisiest government releases. The Census Bureau's margin of error on the monthly change is plus or minus 13.8%, and on the yearly change plus or minus 12.5%. The monthly change is not statistically meaningful. Revisions are common, so the trend over several months is more reliable than any single print. New home sales are also counted when a contract is signed, not at closing, so they react to rate changes faster than existing home sales do.

The October report is due November 25. It will capture contracts signed after rates jumped above 7% in September and the partial relief in mid-October.

So what: For first-time and move-up buyers, new construction may offer the best deals right now because builders carry too much inventory. Compare builder incentives against what an outside lender can offer, and run the numbers on a temporary versus permanent buydown. 4) Home prices up 2.1% nationally on Case-Shiller and 2.4% on FHFA, but still trailing inflation

Wed show

So-what: Prices are not crashing, they are slowly losing ground to inflation. Waiting for a big price drop is not a strategy the data supports.

1-minute read

Two home price reports came out this morning, and both said the same thing: prices are still rising, just slower than everything else.

The S&P Cotality Case-Shiller national index rose 2.1% from a year earlier in August, up from 1.9% in July. The 20-city index rose 2.7%, a bit above forecasts. The FHFA index, which tracks homes with Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac loans, rose 2.4% from a year earlier and 0.3% from July.

With inflation at 3.4%, that means homes lost value in real terms, after inflation, for the 15th straight month. That is a slow squeeze, not a crash.

The gap between markets is huge. Chicago led at 6.7%, followed by New York at 6.2%. Seattle fell 2.0%, Las Vegas 1.3% and Denver 0.8%. S&P said the summer slowdown may be hiding some underlying strength.

Keep in mind Case-Shiller is old news by design. August data reflects deals signed in early summer.

So what: Clients waiting for prices to crash are waiting on something these numbers do not show. The better question is whether their local market is a Chicago or a Seattle.

Expand 3-minute read

The headline numbers first. The S&P Cotality Case-Shiller U.S. National Home Price Index rose 2.1% from August 2025 to August 2026, up from 1.9% in July. The 10-city composite rose 3.6%, from 3.4%. The 20-city composite rose 2.7%, from 2.5%. Forecasts had the 20-city near 2.5% to 2.6%, so it came in slightly hot. Trading Economics called it the strongest 20-city annual gain since May 2025.

The FHFA House Price Index rose 0.3% from July to August and 2.4% from a year earlier. July was revised down to a 0.2% gain. FHFA uses only purchase loans backed by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, so it leaves out most jumbo homes and cash deals. That makes it a decent read on the conforming market where most first-time buyers shop.

The inflation angle is the real story. Rebecca Kaufman at S&P Dow Jones Indices said U.S. home prices declined in real terms for the 15th consecutive month. Consumer prices rose 3.4% from a year earlier in August, so a 2.1% home price gain lost about 1.3 percentage points to inflation. In plain terms, a home bought a year ago is worth more in dollars but buys a bit less in everything else.

Monthly numbers look confusing, so here is the short version. Before seasonal adjustment, the national index fell 0.2% in August, and the 10-city and 20-city indexes each fell 0.4%. After adjusting for the normal end-of-summer slowdown, they rose 0.3%, 0.4% and 0.3%. Kaufman said the divergence suggests the typical post-summer slowdown is obscuring some underlying strength.

Regional splits are wide. Chicago led all metros for the sixth straight month at 6.7%, then New York at 6.2% and Cleveland at 4.3%. Seattle had the largest decline for the third straight month at 2.0%, followed by Las Vegas at 1.3% and Denver at 0.8%. That is a gap of nearly 9 percentage points between the best and worst market. FHFA's regional data showed the same pattern. Yearly gains ranged from 0.5% in the Mountain states to 4.4% in the East North Central states around the Great Lakes.

Odeta Kushi, deputy chief economist at First American, told Mortgage Professional America earlier this month: "It's not a house price crash, right? It's been a gradual decline." She noted that former pandemic hot spots are seeing moderation, while Chicago keeps gaining, and that price declines do help bring affordability into better balance.

One note on the S&P commentary. It describes mortgage rates in the mid-to-high 6% range. Current trackers are higher: Freddie Mac's weekly average was 7.32% last Thursday and Mortgage News Daily was 7.33% Monday.

Remember the lag. Case-Shiller is a three-month moving average of closed sales, published two months later. August data mostly reflects contracts signed in late spring and early summer, before September's rate jump. The September report comes November 24.

So what: For the show, the message is simple. Prices nationally are flat to slightly up, and falling after inflation. Buyers waiting for a crash will likely keep waiting. Local data matters more than national headlines.

5) Orange County: price it right or sit; only 68% of homes sold without a price cut, and pending sales hit an October low

Wed show

So-what: In your backyard, overpriced listings are sitting 81 days and selling $100K-plus below original asks. Buyers have real leverage on stale listings.

1-minute read

Steven Thomas's latest Orange County Housing Report is about one thing: price.

In September, 68% of Orange County home sales closed with no price cut. Those homes went pending in a median of 11 days and sold at about 100% of list. Back in March, 79% sold with no cut.

The other 32% paid for starting too high. Sellers who cut 1% to 4% waited a median 56 days and got 95.7% of their original price. Sellers who cut 5% or more waited 81 days and got just 89.4%. In one example, a home listed at $1,094,000 sold for $978,000, or $116,000 below the original ask.

Today, 45% of active listings have been reduced at least once. Another 1,042 homes were pulled off the market in September, up 37% from a year ago.

Demand is weak. Pending sales fell to 1,219, the lowest October reading since 2007. The time it would take to sell everything on the market rose to 122 days, the highest since January 2019.

So what: Buyers in Orange County can push hard on listings with price cuts. Well-priced homes still sell fast, so act quickly when one fits.

Expand 3-minute read

Steven Thomas of Reports on Housing published his Orange County update on October 26, titled "The Power of Pricing." It is a useful local lens on what national numbers miss.

Start with pricing outcomes for September closed sales. 68% of homes sold without a price reduction. Those homes went pending in a median of 11 days and sold at about 100% of their list price. In March, 79% sold without a reduction, so the share of sellers getting it right on day one has fallen as rates climbed.

14% of sales had a cut of 1% to 4%. Those homes sold at 98.4% of the reduced price after a median 56 days. Measured against the original price, they got 95.7%. Thomas's example: a home listed at $1,029,000 was cut to $1,000,000 and sold for $984,000, $45,000 below the first price.

18% of sales needed cuts of 5% or more. Those sold at 97.8% of the final list price after a median 81 days, or 89.4% of the original. His example: listed at $1,094,000, cut to $1,000,000, and sold at $978,000, which is $116,000 under the original ask.

The pattern goes beyond closed sales. 45% of active listings have been reduced at least once. In September, 1,042 sellers gave up and pulled their homes, up 37% from a year ago and 90% higher than September 2024.

Supply is high by recent standards. Active inventory is 4,948 homes, up 24 in two weeks. It peaked at 5,054 in mid-August and has barely dropped since. Last year it fell 723 homes over the same stretch. Inventory is 660 homes higher than a year ago, though still below the pre-COVID average of 6,010. Through September, 27% fewer homes came on the market than the 2017 to 2019 average.

Demand is the weak link. Pending sales over the prior month fell to 1,219, down 2% in two weeks and the lowest October reading since 2007. A year ago there were 1,546. Thomas pins it on rates stuck above 7% since September 11. He notes the three-quarter-point jump in September was the largest four-week climb since September 2022.

The Expected Market Time, meaning how many days it would take to sell every listing at the current pace, rose from 118 to 122 days. That is the highest reading since January 2019 and the highest October since 2010. Detached homes are at 115 days, condos and townhomes at 131. A year ago the county was at 83 days. Luxury homes above $2.5 million are at 192 days.

Closed sales were 1,729 in September, down 4% from a year earlier. Distressed sales remain tiny. Short sales and foreclosures were only 0.4% of listings, so this is not a forced-selling market.

So what: For California buyers, this is the most usable data point today. The right home at the right price still moves in under two weeks. Stale listings with cuts are where negotiation lives. Pair that with a strong pre-approval and buyers can ask for credits or a lower price without losing the deal. The same playbook applies in every state BuyWise serves.

6) Consumer confidence jumps 7.5 points to 91.7 as gas prices ease, but home-buying plans slip

Wed show

So-what: Households feel better, but buying plans for homes dipped and most still expect higher rates. Confidence alone will not unlock purchase demand.

1-minute read

Consumers felt noticeably better in October. The Conference Board's Consumer Confidence Index rose 7.5 points to 91.7, back to June levels. September was revised up to 84.2.

Both parts improved. The Present Situation Index, which measures how people see business and jobs today, rose 8.6 points to 120.4. The Expectations Index, which measures the outlook for the next six months, rose 6.9 points to 72.6. That only partly reverses three straight monthly drops.

The Conference Board tied the bounce to retail gas prices easing from September to October. It also said confidence remains on a long-term downward trend.

Two details matter for housing. Plans to buy a home over the next six months declined slightly on a six-month average. And consumers still expect higher interest rates over the next year.

Inflation expectations eased but stayed high. People expect prices to rise 5.8% over the next 12 months, on average, down from 6.1% in September.

The survey ran October 1 to 21, so it mostly missed last week's rate dip.

So what: Better mood is a good sign, but buyers still need a payment that works. Lead with numbers, not sentiment.

Expand 3-minute read

The October Consumer Confidence report from The Conference Board came out at 7 AM PT. The headline index rose 7.5 points to 91.7, using 1985 as the base year of 100. September was revised up to 84.2 from 81.9. The forecast in the MBS Live calendar was 81, so this beat by a lot.

The Present Situation Index rose 8.6 points to 120.4. That piece asks how people feel about business and job conditions right now. The Expectations Index rose 6.9 points to 72.6. That piece asks about the next six months for income, business and jobs. The Conference Board notes that a reading below 80 on expectations has historically signaled recession risk, and this is still under that line.

Why the jump? Fergus McCormick, senior economist at The Conference Board, said the recovery lined up with retail gasoline prices easing from September to October. He also said confidence remained on the long-term downward trend of the past few years. Consumers planned less spending on services and on durable goods over the next six months, likely because of higher price levels and persistent inflation.

What people wrote in is telling. Complaints about prices, food and groceries, and oil and diesel rose. Mentions of war and conflict eased for a second month but stayed elevated. Concern about jobs ticked up slightly.

On the job market, 26.7% said jobs were plentiful, up from 24.3%. 21.4% said jobs were hard to get, unchanged. The gap between those two, called the labor market differential, rose 2.4 points to positive 5.3%. Expectations for income improved, with 18.7% expecting their income to rise and 12.0% expecting it to fall.

Now the housing pieces. On a six-month moving average, plans to buy a home declined slightly. Plans to buy autos also declined slightly. Consumers still expected higher interest rates over the next 12 months. Average inflation expectations for the next year fell to 5.8% from 6.1% in September, but that is still well above the Fed's 2% target.

There is also a generational split. Only consumers under 35 and households earning $100,000 or more showed rising confidence on a six-month average. Gen Z and Millennials remain far more upbeat than Gen X and Baby Boomers. That fits the first-time buyer audience: younger people are more confident, yet the data earlier this month showed the typical first-time buyer is now 40.

Keep the timing in mind. The survey cutoff was October 21. Most of last week's rate improvement came after that, when the 10-year yield fell about 20 basis points on October 22. November's survey will show whether that relief helped.

For the bond market, a strong confidence number is a slight negative for rates, since it supports spending and inflation. It is a minor factor compared with the Fed and Thursday's PCE inflation report.

So what: Sentiment is better, but the share planning to buy a home slipped, and most people expect rates to rise. That is a gap you can fill on the show. Many buyers assume rates only go up from here. A clear explanation of what moves rates, and how buydowns and refinancing options work, can bring back some of those sidelined buyers.

7) Hidden costs of owning a home top $16,500 a year; about $22,900 in Los Angeles

Wed show

So-what: Insurance, taxes and upkeep now make up about 42% of a typical monthly housing cost. Budget for them up front, not after closing.

1-minute read

The mortgage payment is only part of the bill. A new Zillow and Thumbtack analysis says homeowners now spend more than $16,500 a year on maintenance, homeowners insurance and property taxes. That is up nearly 32% in five years.

The good news is the pace is slowing. Those costs rose 3% over the past year.

Here is how it stacks up monthly. The average mortgage payment is $1,922. On top of that, the average owner pays $1,380 a month for costs outside principal and interest. That is nearly 42% of the total monthly cost of owning.

Maintenance is the biggest piece at 67.4%. Property taxes are 18.9%, and insurance is 13.7%.

California is near the top. Los Angeles averages $22,915 a year and San Francisco $24,383. New York leads at $25,111. Even Phoenix, the cheapest metro in the study, runs $14,187.

Zillow economist Kara Ng put it plainly: "Many households focus so much on winning the home and underestimate what it costs to live in it."

So what: First-time buyers should plan a monthly budget that includes upkeep, not just the payment on the pre-approval letter.

Expand 3-minute read

This study lands right in the middle of the affordability conversation. Zillow and Thumbtack, a home services marketplace, looked at three costs that come with owning a home beyond the loan itself: maintenance, homeowners insurance and property taxes. Together they now average more than $16,500 a year nationally. That is up nearly 32% over the past five years.

The growth is slowing. The typical homeowner saw those costs rise 3% over the past year. Thumbtack's Kendra Sinclair called the slowdown in maintenance costs "welcome news for homeowners managing a lot of competing expenses."

The monthly math is what clients need to see. The average U.S. mortgage payment is $1,922 a month. The average owner adds $1,380 a month for costs other than principal and interest. That brings the all-in figure to about $3,300, and the non-loan part is nearly 42% of it.

Here is the breakdown of those extra costs. Maintenance is 67.4%. That includes routine upkeep and repairs, from HVAC service to roofs and water heaters. Property taxes are 18.9%. Insurance is 13.7%. Note one thing: a lender's payment estimate usually includes taxes and insurance but not maintenance, which is the largest piece. That is where new owners get surprised.

Coastal metros carry the heaviest load. New York averages $25,111 a year, San Francisco $24,383, Los Angeles $22,915 and Boston $22,096. Phoenix was the lowest metro in the analysis, and owners there still spend $14,187 a year. These figures exclude the mortgage payment.

For California buyers, this lines up with two stories from recent weeks. Insurance has become a bigger budget item than property tax in a lot of places, and the state's FAIR Plan and carrier pullbacks keep premiums high. Proposition 13 limits on property tax increases help long-time owners, but new buyers pay taxes on today's purchase price.

Kara Ng, a senior economist at Zillow, said, "Many households focus so much on winning the home and underestimate what it costs to live in it." She added that because costs vary by market, home shoppers should "run the numbers and understand what they can truly afford before they make an offer."

How to use this with clients. First, show the full monthly cost, not just principal and interest. Second, suggest a maintenance reserve, often a set amount each month in a separate savings account. Third, shop insurance before writing an offer, especially in fire-risk or coastal areas. Fourth, for move-up buyers, compare what they pay now in upkeep on an older home to what a newer home might cost. A newer home can carry lower maintenance even at a higher price.

There is also a qualifying angle. Debt-to-income ratios count taxes, insurance and HOA dues, so a high insurance quote can shrink the loan amount a buyer qualifies for. Getting a quote early avoids a surprise in underwriting.

So what: This is a strong segment for the Wednesday show. Buyers who plan for the full cost of ownership make better offers and are less likely to feel house poor. You and Jeb can walk through a sample monthly budget for a California home and for buyers in any of the other states BuyWise serves.

8) Treasury proposes first rule for the ROAD to Housing Act's limits on big investors buying single-family homes

Wed show

So-what: Starting January 7, 2027, investors with 350 or more homes face purchase limits, with carve-outs. Comments are due November 23.

1-minute read

The government took its first concrete step on the investor limits in this summer's housing law. Treasury released a 96-page proposed rule for the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, which Congress passed in July.

The core rule: investors that own 350 or more single-family homes cannot buy more, with exceptions. The law takes effect January 7, 2027. It is often called an investor ban, but it is really a cap on the largest investors with several carve-outs.

Key pieces of the proposal:

Large investors get an 18-month window after January 7 to keep buying newly built rental homes that were never owner-occupied.

Investors that renovate homes to rent can still buy if they spend at least 15% of the purchase price on improvements. That blocks cosmetic flips from qualifying.

Violations could bring a civil penalty of up to $1 million or three times the purchase price.

Public comments are due November 23, and Treasury says more rules will follow.

So what: For first-time buyers, fewer big-investor bids on entry-level homes could help over time. The effect is not immediate, and smaller investors are not covered.

Expand 3-minute read

Background first. The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act passed in July with bipartisan support. It is the most ambitious housing bill since the 1990s, with provisions meant to boost supply and expand access to mortgage financing. One of its best-known pieces limits purchases of single-family homes by the largest investors. The law takes effect January 7, 2027, but many parts need regulations and pilot programs before they work, which experts say could take years.

What Treasury proposed. Last Thursday, Treasury released a 96-page proposed rule covering what it calls the first phase. Treasury said it focused on issues that would be beneficial to address as soon as possible to provide clarity and minimize market disruptions.

Who is covered. The statute says investors owning 350 or more single-family homes cannot buy additional properties, subject to exceptions. The proposal asks for feedback on how to count ownership. It separates direct, indirect and passive investment, which decides whether a fund or partner counts toward the 350-home line.

Build-to-rent. Treasury proposed an 18-month grace window after January 7 for new build-to-rent homes. During that window, large investors could still buy homes built to rent that had never been owner-occupied. Builders with projects in the pipeline can finish and sell them. But homes completed in that window and held past it could not later be sold to large investors unless another exemption applies. Treasury asked whether 18 months is enough and whether a certificate of occupancy should mark when a home counts as newly built. Existing build-to-rent communities are not part of that window, though they could still change hands between large investors if a separate exemption applies.

Renovation. The rehab-to-rent exemption stays, but investors must spend at least 15% of the purchase price on renovations and improvements. That keeps paint-and-carpet jobs from qualifying.

Penalties. Violations could bring a civil penalty of up to $1 million or three times the property's purchase price.

Next steps. Public comments are due November 23. Treasury says more rulemaking will follow for other parts of the investor rules. Senator Elizabeth Warren, the ranking Democrat on the Senate Banking Committee, said final implementation is "far from done" and that Congress will be watching for loopholes.

What it means for buyers. The largest institutional landlords concentrated purchases in certain Sun Belt metros and entry-level price ranges. Limiting their purchases could reduce competition for starter homes in those places. It will not change much in markets where big investors were never a large share of buyers. Most investor purchases nationally come from small investors who own a handful of homes and are not covered by the 350-home threshold.

What it means for lenders. DSCR and investor-loan clients with small portfolios are not the target here. Larger operators who use agency or non-QM financing should watch how the ownership-counting rules come out, since affiliates and funds could be grouped together.

So what: This is a slow-moving help for first-time buyers, not a quick fix. On the show, it is a good example of the difference between a headline and the actual rule. The real limits apply only to the biggest players, with exceptions, starting in January.

So what: Do not promise clients that credit report fees will fall in 2027. Budget for flat to higher costs per file until the dust settles, and ask your vendors now how they plan to handle the Preferred Program.
