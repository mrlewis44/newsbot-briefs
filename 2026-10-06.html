3) Pennymac rolls out VantageScore 4.0 in all channels, including broker direct

Wed show

So-what: One of the largest correspondent and wholesale lenders is now live on VS4, so brokers can start testing it on real files.

1-minute read

Pennymac said Monday afternoon it has finished rolling out VantageScore 4.0 across its whole mortgage production platform. That includes consumer direct, broker direct, and correspondent.

For brokers, broker direct is the key part. If you send loans to Pennymac, VS4 is now an option on those files. Correspondent matters too. Pennymac is one of the largest correspondent aggregators, buying loans from community lenders, credit unions, and regional banks. Its adoption could pull a lot of smaller lenders onto VS4.

The rollout follows April moves by FHFA and HUD that opened conventional and FHA lending to competing credit score models. VantageScore 4.0 uses trended credit data. That means it looks at how a borrower has handled credit over as long as 24 months, not just a single snapshot.

CEO David Spector said credit scoring has never had a competitive market and borrowers have paid for that in closing costs. He thanked FHFA Director Pulte and HUD Secretary Turner.

For buyers, this is real progress on the credit score story. More lenders offering VS4 means more ways to price a file. Josh should ask Pennymac reps how pricing compares to Classic FICO.

Expand 3-minute read

HousingWire reported Monday afternoon that PennyMac Financial Services completed its rollout of VantageScore 4.0 across its mortgage production platform. The deployment covers three channels: consumer direct, broker direct, and correspondent.

Why this matters for a broker shop. Pennymac is a major wholesale lender. With broker direct live, independent brokers sending files to Pennymac can now use VantageScore 4.0 on conventional loans. Pennymac is also one of the largest correspondent aggregators in the country. It buys closed loans from community lenders, credit unions, and regional banks. When an aggregator of that size supports a new score, its sellers have a strong reason to adopt it too. Pennymac said its scale across all three channels could help speed up adoption, especially through correspondent.

The policy backdrop. In April, FHFA and HUD opened the conventional and FHA markets to competition among credit scoring models. Since then, lenders have been sorting out how to use VS4 in practice. Recent briefs covered the single LLPA grid that removed a 20-point VS4 downward adjustment, and Rocket's stated preference for VS4 in its direct channel. Pennymac's move adds a large wholesale and correspondent player to the list.

What VS4 does differently. VantageScore 4.0 uses trended credit data. Instead of only a single snapshot of the credit file, it looks at how a borrower has managed credit over as much as 24 months. In plain words, a borrower who has been steadily paying down balances can look different than one who has been running them up, even if today's balances are the same. That can help thin-file and recovering-credit borrowers, but it can also hurt borrowers who recently added debt. Brokers should not assume VS4 is always the higher score.

What Pennymac's CEO said. David Spector said the company intends to be at the vanguard of credit score modernization because it believes it will lower costs and support homeownership. He thanked FHFA Director Pulte and HUD Secretary Turner for moving this forward. He said credit scoring has never had a competitive market, and borrowers have paid for that through their closing costs. He added that multiple score options give lenders a basis to compare predictive accuracy, coverage, and cost, and that borrowers should feel the benefits of that competition directly. Pennymac's statement did not include specific pricing numbers or cost savings per loan, so treat the savings claim as a direction, not a figure.

What to do with it. Ask your Pennymac account executive three things. First, how VS4 pricing compares to Classic FICO on the same file. Second, whether VS4 can be used on every conventional product or only some. Third, how they handle files where VS4 and FICO tell different stories. Then run a few live files both ways.

Show angle. Credit scores are a top first-time buyer topic. The plain-English message for Emily and Jake: lenders are starting to compete on which credit score they use, and a newer model looks at your payment trend over two years, not just today. Paying down cards ahead of applying can matter more than it used to.

4) CRMLS sues Compass first, on the eve of Compass's Oct 6 MLS deadline

Wed show

So-what: The biggest MLS in the country, the one most Southern California agents use, is now in federal court over off-MLS listings; buyers' access to listings is the stake.

1-minute read

CRMLS filed suit late Monday in federal court in New York. It asks a judge to declare that its listing cooperation rules are legal under state and federal antitrust law. It also asks for an injunction that would permanently block Compass from suing over those rules.

Timing matters. The filing came on the eve of Compass's deadline for MLSs to accept its demands or face legal action. Compass sent CRMLS a demand letter on Sept. 8. Over the weekend, CEO Robert Reffkin posted on LinkedIn that Compass is preparing a federal antitrust suit against CRMLS to stop it from fining agents for public marketing.

The fight is about homes that are marketed publicly but kept off the MLS. CRMLS says once a home is advertised to the public, every cooperating broker and their buyers should see it on the MLS. Its filing says Compass wants to free-ride on other brokers' listings while hoarding its own.

Compass says MLSs covering more than 350,000 agents in 12 states already allow public marketing of any listing. It calls the CRMLS suit without merit and says it will file its own soon.

For SoCal buyers, the risk is fewer homes visible in one place.

Expand 3-minute read

Monday's brief covered MLS leaders saying lawfare is now normal as Compass's Oct 6 demands approached. The new development: CRMLS did not wait to be sued. It moved first.

What CRMLS filed. HousingWire reports CRMLS filed a complaint late Monday in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York. It seeks a declaratory judgment that its listing cooperation policies are fair, procompetitive, and legally sound. It also asks for an injunction permanently barring Compass from bringing any claim that the cooperation rules violate federal or state antitrust laws. In plain words, CRMLS wants a judge to bless its rules before Compass can attack them.

The core dispute. The fight is over listings that are publicly marketed but not entered in the MLS. CRMLS says sellers already have options if they do not want a home marketed publicly. But once a home is advertised to the public, CRMLS says cooperating brokers and their clients deserve equal access to it through the MLS. The filing says Compass wants to control for-sale inventory as a competitive weapon, marketing homes publicly to attract buyers while withholding those same homes from the MLS whose data Compass uses for its own clients. The complaint calls that free-riding on other brokerages' listings while hoarding Compass's own inventory.

How we got here. Compass sent CRMLS a demand letter on Sept. 8, alleging its rules violate antitrust law. CRMLS rejected the demands. At the Council of MLS Open House conference last week, Reffkin threatened to sue any MLS that refused similar changes. On Sunday, Reffkin posted on LinkedIn that Compass is preparing a federal antitrust lawsuit against CRMLS, the largest MLS in the country, to stop it from fining agents for public marketing. He wrote that the suit would hit in the next few weeks. He also posted what he says is a 2018 CRMLS form that let sellers market a property without submitting it to the MLS until a set date. He argues MLSs started blocking public marketing after a 2019 industry report flagged off-MLS marketing as a major challenge. CRMLS has also announced an MLS Cooperation Legal Defense Fund.

What each side says. CRMLS CEO Art Carter said the case is about protecting a marketplace that works for all real estate professionals and consumers. A Compass spokesperson said homeowners should be able to publicly market any listing, including office exclusives, without their agent facing thousands of dollars in fines. Compass said MLSs supporting more than 350,000 agents across 12 states already allow this. It called the CRMLS suit without merit and said it plans to file its own lawsuit soon.

Why a mortgage broker in Huntington Beach should care. CRMLS is the MLS most Southern California agents and buyers rely on. If more listings move into private or office-exclusive channels, buyers working with smaller brokerages could see fewer homes, and later. That can affect pre-approval strategy and how fast a buyer needs to move.

Show angle. For Emily and Jake, the plain takeaway: not every home for sale may show up on the big search sites. Ask your agent how they find off-MLS and office-exclusive listings, and do not assume the portal shows everything.

5) Realtor.com: older owners will release 13.9 million homes over 10 years, but few starter homes

Wed show

So-what: More move-up inventory is coming from boomers, but only about 38,000 starter homes a year, so entry-level supply stays tight.

1-minute read

A Realtor.com analysis published Monday projects that baby boomer and Silent Generation owners will release 13.9 million homes from 2026 to 2036. That is about 1.39 million a year. The prior decade released roughly 8 million, so this is a big step up.

The catch is the mix. Only 0.38 million of those homes are starter homes with 0 to 2 bedrooms. That is about 38,000 a year, just 3.2% of current annual listings on Realtor.com. Family homes with 3 to 4 bedrooms make up 9.9 million, or 71.2%. Large homes with 5 or more bedrooms add 3.6 million.

Why so few starter homes? Many older owners of small homes own them free and clear. Among starter home owners ages 70 to 79, nearly three-quarters have no mortgage. With no payment pressure, they stay put.

The pace builds over time, from about 1.27 million homes in 2027 to 1.52 million by 2036. Turnover is fastest in the Rust Belt and Midwest, not expensive coastal markets.

So-what: good news for move-up buyers, not much help for first-time buyers in places like Orange County. It will not erase a roughly 4 million home shortage.

Expand 3-minute read

The silver tsunami gets talked about a lot. Realtor.com put numbers on it. HousingWire covered the analysis, published Monday.

The headline. Owner-occupied homes held by boomers and the Silent Generation are projected to fall from an estimated 36.7 million in 2026 to 22.8 million by 2036. That net decline of 13.9 million homes is what gets released as older owners die, move into care, combine households, or move into rentals. It averages about 1.39 million homes a year. Realtor.com estimates these households released roughly 8 million homes over the past decade. So the next decade means 5.9 million more homes, which Realtor.com describes as a 74% increase in turnover. HousingWire's summary also describes it as a 34% increase from the past 10 years, so the exact comparison depends on how it is measured.

The timing. This is a slow handoff, not a flood. The annual pace is projected to rise from about 1.27 million homes in 2027 to 1.52 million in 2036. The Silent Generation is the main source today. Boomers take over around 2029 as they move into their 80s. Boomer releases are still climbing in 2036, so the peak likely comes after the forecast window.

The mix is the real story. Starter homes, 0 to 2 bedrooms: 0.38 million over 10 years, about 38,000 a year, or 3.2% of current annual Realtor.com listings. Family homes, 3 to 4 bedrooms: 9.9 million, 71.2% of the total, nearly 1 million a year, equal to 24.7% of recent listings. Large homes, 5 or more bedrooms: 3.6 million, about 360,000 a year, equal to 67.2% of recent large-home listings.

Boomers and the Silent Generation own an estimated 1.33 million starter homes, about 51.3% of that segment, but are expected to release less than one-third of them. The reason is paid-off mortgages. Among starter home owners ages 70 to 79, nearly three-quarters own free and clear. That compares to 65.1% for family-size home owners and 58.9% for large-home owners. No mortgage payment means less pressure to sell.

Move-up buyers win. If even half of the family homes released reach the market, Realtor.com says family-home listings could rise about 12.3% from recent levels. Large homes see the biggest relative impact. More mid-tier and upper-tier supply could ease prices for trade-up buyers and may free some starter homes as current owners move up.

Location matters. Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies projects slower household growth ahead, averaging about 859,000 a year. NAHB research finds turnover from older households is happening fastest in the Rust Belt and Midwest, not expensive coastal markets. So expect local softening in family and large homes in slower-growing markets, not a national price drop. Realtor.com senior economist Jiayi Xu said the handoff is a welcome relief but not big enough to solve the housing shortage alone, which HousingWire puts at roughly 4 million homes.

Show angle. For Emily and Jake: waiting for boomers to sell is not a plan for a first home. For move-up buyers, though, the coming decade likely brings more 3 and 4 bedroom choices. In coastal California, where turnover is slower, the effect will be smaller.

6) NextGen buyer study: 66% fear their money won't last, 43% say a mortgage feels like a trap

Wed show

So-what: Many Gen Z and millennial buyers are qualified but frozen by anxiety and myths, which is exactly the audience the Wednesday show serves.

1-minute read

National MI and FirstHome IQ released a new NextGen Homebuyer report on how Gen Z and millennials feel about money. The findings point to emotion, not just credit and income, as a big barrier to buying.

The numbers: 66% worry their finances will not last. That includes a majority of people with strong savings habits and incomes above $100,000. 60% say the amount of homebuying information out there is overwhelming. Nearly 9 in 10 report some level of shame about their finances. More than half are anxious about their financial future and skeptical of experts.

There is a hopeful side. 6 in 10 say owning a home would make them feel more secure than anything else they could do with their money. More than half believe they can reach a financial goal once they set one. But 43% say a mortgage feels like a trap.

National MI's Ralph Remy said many would-be buyers are financially qualified but still hesitate because of overwhelm, distrust, or myths. A big one: many still believe they need 20% down.

So-what: education and trust win this buyer. Simple, calm explanations of low down payment options can move people who already qualify.

Expand 3-minute read

HousingWire covered a new study from National MI and FirstHome IQ titled How You're Built to Buy: A NextGen Homebuyer Report. Kristin Messerli, executive director of FirstHome IQ, led it. She has run the NextGen Homebuyer research series with National MI since 2020.

What is different about this study. Most readiness research looks at savings, credit, and income. This one looks at emotions: financial anxiety, shame, self-belief, and uncertainty, and how they affect whether people even start the homebuying process.

The key numbers. More than half of respondents said they are anxious about their financial future and skeptical of experts, regardless of income or savings habits. 66% worry their finances will not last. That includes a majority of people who report strong savings habits and annual incomes above $100,000. In plain words, this is not just a low-income problem. 60% said the volume of homebuying information is overwhelming. Nearly 9 in 10 reported some level of shame about their finances.

The hopeful numbers. More than half said that if they set a financial goal, they believe they can find a way to reach it. 6 in 10 said owning a home would make them feel more secure than anything else they could do with their money. So the desire is there.

The tension. 43% said a mortgage feels like a trap. That is a big share of potential buyers who see the product itself as the danger.

What the researchers said. Messerli said money is emotional, and that more than half of the total market is anxious or avoidant with their finances regardless of income and savings, which creates an opportunity for those who learn to reach and serve them. Bri Lees, a NextGen marketing expert and new FirstHome IQ board member, said these buyers want to own a home and are waiting on someone they trust. Ralph Remy, senior director of client digital experience at National MI, said the industry has traditionally judged readiness by income, assets, credit, and savings, but many potential buyers are financially qualified yet still hesitate because of overwhelm, distrust, or misconceptions.

The 20% myth. Remy pointed to a continuing knowledge gap on down payments. National MI said many consumers still believe they need 20% down, even though lower down payment options with private mortgage insurance are available. The report ends with seven practices for housing professionals. HousingWire's summary did not list them, so check the full report before quoting them.

How to use this. For BuyWise, the lesson is about the first conversation. Lead with one clear next step, not a wall of options. Normalize the anxiety. Show the real monthly payment with 3% or 5% down and PMI next to a 20% down scenario, so the buyer sees the tradeoff instead of a myth.

Show angle. This study practically describes Emily and Jake. A segment idea: three money feelings that stop qualified buyers, and what to do about each. Address the mortgage feels like a trap fear directly with fixed-rate payment stability, and bust the 20% down myth with real numbers. Keep the tone calm and non-salesy, since more than half are skeptical of experts.

7) California condo special assessments hit $26,000 to $49,000, squeezing a key entry-level option

Wed show

So-what: Surprise HOA bills are another hidden cost for condo buyers in Orange County, so HOA reserve and assessment review belongs in every condo pre-approval talk.

1-minute read

The LA Times reports that big emergency HOA assessments are becoming more common in California. Aging condos, the insurance crisis, and new balcony safety rules are driving them.

The lead example is in San Clemente. Owners at a 198-unit complex built in the 1990s got a one-time $26,000 emergency assessment to replace roofs, on top of monthly fees of more than $500. The total roof and fire suppression project cost $5.2 million. Many residents are paying through a plan of about $2,000 a month. The HOA sent lien letters warning missed payments could escalate as far as foreclosure. The Times also cited a $49,000 fee at a Torrance condo community.

An HOA attorney said assessments over $20,000 per unit are increasingly common. In 2017, an $18,000 one in Orange County was almost unheard of.

The Times says the risk of these fees may be cooling buyer interest in condos, long an affordable entry point, while new condo construction lags.

For lenders, this is a DTI and condo review issue. A $2,000 monthly assessment can sink a qualifying ratio. Check HOA budgets, reserves, and pending assessments early, before a buyer falls in love with a unit.

Expand 3-minute read

The LA Times story, published Oct 2, is about a growing problem in California condos: huge one-time emergency assessments that owners did not see coming.

The San Clemente case. Megan Blanda, 42, lives at Vilamoura at Rancho San Clemente, a 198-unit complex built in the 1990s. Three-bedroom units there have recently listed for about $1 million. Monthly HOA fees are more than $500. This year, the HOA told owners they owed a one-time $26,000 emergency assessment to replace the roofs. The HOA's attorney, James McCormick, said the roof project and fire suppression repairs total $5.2 million. He acknowledged roof maintenance should have been planned and done in prior years, but said an independent expert report found the roofs would keep leaking and could cause catastrophic interior damage, especially with a strong El Nino expected this winter. Residents have started paying, many through a plan of about $2,000 a month on top of regular fees. The HOA sent lien letters warning that missed payments could escalate as far as foreclosure. An 81-year-old resident on a fixed income told the Times she does not have the $26,000. Some owners have launched a recall election of the board.

It is not just one complex. The Times cited a $49,000 fee at a Torrance condo community. Michael Kushner, an HOA attorney who usually represents homeowners, said he has heard of more emergency assessments over $20,000 per unit in the last few years. In 2017, he worked with owners in another Orange County community facing an $18,000 assessment, which he said was almost unheard of at the time.

Why it is happening. Many California condos were built in the early 2000s boom and are now aging. The state's insurance crisis is pushing up HOA insurance costs as carriers pull back. State law requiring balcony inspections, passed after a 2015 Berkeley balcony collapse killed six people, has led to expensive repairs. HOA document reviewer Jacquie Berry said boards often avoid raising monthly fees because it is unpopular, so reserves fall behind.

The housing market link. The Times reports that the risk of these fees may be a factor in buyers' waning interest in condos, which have long been a relatively affordable option. At the same time, new condo construction in California has fallen off sharply since the early 2000s, limiting the entry-level market. First-time buyers are also being squeezed by higher rates.

Mortgage implications. A special assessment can change a loan in a few ways. It can add to the monthly obligation if it is paid on a plan, which hits DTI. It can show up on condo questionnaires and affect project review. And a seller may need to pay it off or credit it at closing, which becomes a negotiation point. The safest path is to request the HOA budget, reserve study, and any notice of pending assessments at the start, and to ask whether balcony inspection work is finished.

Show angle. For Emily and Jake shopping condos in Orange County: the monthly HOA fee is not the whole story. Ask for the reserve study, the last two years of board minutes, and any planned assessments before you write an offer. Berry's advice: look at the budget, go to the meeting, ask questions.

8) The Loan Store is now Averra Financial; non-QM volume up 68% year to date

So-what: A top 4 wholesale lender is leaning harder into non-QM, a broker HELOC, and a complex-income tool, all useful for self-employed borrowers.

1-minute read

The Loan Store changed its name to Averra Financial, effective Oct. 1. The company bought Homepoint's wholesale business in 2023. It now says it is one of the four largest wholesale lenders in the U.S. and has one of the largest non-QM platforms across channels.

The key number: non-QM volume is up 68% year to date through August versus the same period in 2025. That is a strong sign that alternative-documentation lending is growing even with high rates.

Two products stand out for brokers. First, a HELOC partnership with Aven, which funded its first loan in May. It gives brokers a digital way to tap equity without refinancing a low-rate first mortgage. The Fresno State loss was ugly on that side of the ball. WSU scored only on two Jack Stevens field goals, including a career-long 50-yarder before halftime. They went 3 of 14 on third down, and their season rate is now 27 percent, near the bottom of the country. Tailbacks Maxwell Woods, Kirby Vorhees, and Leo Pulalasi combined for 45 yards on 12 carries. "When 11 of your 14 third downs are third-and-long plus, the percentages on conversion there are pretty low," Moore said.

The defense was the bright spot. End Matyus McLain had two tackles for loss and a half sack, and WSU stayed within one score for three quarters. Fresno's trick plays, including a reverse for a third-quarter touchdown, were the main slip.
