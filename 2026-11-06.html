1) Strong October jobs (+165k) barely move bonds: UMBS only -0.04 after the release as the 10-year sits near 4.86%

So-what: A hot payroll number did not wreck rate sheets. Lock talk stays about this week's gains, not a forced selloff.

1-minute read

October hiring was much stronger than expected, and mortgage bonds barely blinked.

The Labor Department said payrolls rose 165,000. Wall Street had been looking for about 60,000. Unemployment slipped to 4.1%. Average hourly earnings rose 0.4% on the month and 3.1% from a year ago. August and September payrolls were revised up by a combined 84,000.

Matthew Graham at MBS Live called it a recipe for a bond selloff. Instead, after a quick knee-jerk move, yields were back near pre-release levels within about eight minutes. Around 6:26 AM PT, UMBS 6.0 was 100.31, down only 0.04. The 10-year was about 4.863% on MBS Live and 4.868% on CNBC.

Graham's read: bonds have been taking cues from the AI tech selloff more than from labor data. CME FedWatch still prices roughly a 77% chance of a December hike.

For clients: today's print did not erase this week's better pricing. Get a fresh quote, and do not treat the jobs miss as a reason rates must jump.

Expand 3-minute read

The big number landed, and the bond market shrugged. That is the story for mortgage pricing this morning.

So the headline is strong, with a household-survey caveat on slack work.

Why bonds did not sell off hard. MBS Live's morning note said a report this strong is usually a recipe for higher yields. After an initial knee-jerk, yields returned to pre-NFP levels within about eight minutes and stayed there. Fed odds. Markets still lean toward a December hike. Snapshot figure: CME FedWatch near 77%. That is opinion-sensitive and can move intraday.

Client takeaway. A strong jobs print did not force a big giveback in mortgage pricing by mid-morning. This week's improvement is still largely intact. Borrowers who like today's payment should get locked-or-float advice with a same-day rate sheet, not wait for a theoretical post-jobs selloff that has not shown up yet.

Why the muted reaction matters for locks. In a normal playbook, a 165,000 print with upward revisions and 0.4% wage growth would push mortgage bonds lower and force lenders to worsen rates. That did not stick this morning. The early UMBS reading near 100.31 left most of this week's improvement intact. Later MND headers showed a bit more weakness at 100.22, so sheets can still drift through the day. The right client message is not that jobs were soft. Jobs were strong. Bonds just cared more about the tech channel. Pair every conversation with a live price and a lock trigger on the 10-year if the borrower floats.

CrossCountry Mortgage is turning non-solicit clauses into real demand letters, and the industry is split on whether that is smart or heavy-handed.

Expand 3-minute read

This is an industry-structure story with direct recruiting risk.

What CCM did. HousingWire reports CrossCountry Mortgage sent cease-and-desist letters to at least 25 former loan officers. The letters invoke two-year non-solicitation provisions and demand thousands of dollars under employment agreements. CCM declined to comment on the follow-up story. An earlier HousingWire exclusive covered the same campaign.

What 75 basis points means. CCM's letters seek repayment calculated at 75 bps per loan for loans the former LOs originated that allegedly violated the agreements. Attorneys told HousingWire that number likely approximates the economics CCM would have kept if the loan stayed in-house. James Brody of Brody Gapp warned that liquidated-damages clauses must reflect a reasonable estimate of loss. If they look like a pure penalty, courts often strike them.

Why enforcement is rising. Non-solicits that bar poaching clients, referral partners, or staff have long been in mortgage contracts. Many smaller lenders do not enforce them because lawsuits are costly and proof is hard. With higher-for-longer rates compressing volume, that calculation is shifting. Alan Pezeshkian of House America said every company has a version, but he has not enforced his. He called enforcement both a message and a way to become "a villain."

Who owns the customer. Brendan McKay of McKay Mortgage argues originators hold the relationship they personally earned, especially when the borrower reaches out. Several CCM letters specifically reference refinance business from lender databases. Attorneys draw a line: active LO outreach is easier to police than a borrower who follows the LO. Barring referral partners from choosing an LO can start to look like a non-compete and draws more scrutiny.

Practical checklist. 1) Before joining a lender, negotiate carve-outs for personally generated leads. 2) Before leaving, get counsel on what you can solicit. 3) Before hiring a producer, ask what the prior agreement covers and what data they can legally bring. 4) Tell borrowers upfront if a contract could limit who can help them later. In a tight market, the shops that handle this cleanly will recruit better and avoid nasty letter wars.

What BuyWise teams should do operationally. Review offer letters and branch agreements for non-solicit scope, duration, and liquidated-damages math. If recruiting, ask candidates what letters they have already received and whether refi database language is included. If an LO is leaving, freeze any unclear outreach until counsel reviews the file. CCM's 75 bps ask makes the dollars vivid, but enforceability still depends on state law and how contact started. Treat this as a process risk, not just industry gossip.

7) CFPB closes 86% of open supervisory actions and cuts scheduled exams about in half

So-what: Compliance priorities are shifting in Washington, but today's ATR, QM, and servicing rules still govern every file.

1-minute read

The CFPB is narrowing supervision, and originators should read that as a priority shift, not a green light to loosen process.

In its semiannual report covering activity through June 2026, the bureau said it closed 1,327 of 1,552 supervisory actions that were open as of July 1, 2025, about 86%. Scheduled exams were cut roughly 50%. About 40% of pending investigations were closed. Mortgage origination and servicing remain listed among supervisory priorities.

The bureau is reviewing ability-to-repay and qualified mortgage rules under Regulation Z and considering a streamlined servicing rule under Regulation X. Those are reviews, not final borrower-qualification relief.

It also finalized Section 1071 small-business data changes, including a lower revenue threshold of $1 million and a Jan. 1, 2028 compliance date. Complaints still poured in: about 8.27 million over the year, with companies responding to 99.6% of those sent for review.

So what: keep current disclosures and ATR docs until rules actually change.

Expand 3-minute read

This is a regulatory posture story. The rules on your rate sheets and disclosure stack have not flipped overnight.

What closed. HousingWire's account of the CFPB semiannual report, released Oct. 27, says that during the broader July 2025 through June 2026 window the bureau closed 1,327 of 1,552 supervisory actions open as of July 1, 2025, or about 86%. Closures reflected either institutions fixing issues or findings that no longer matched revised priorities. The bureau reassessed 80 open examinations and closed 66 that did not fit. Fourteen continued, focused on correcting violations, compliance weaknesses, and consumer relief. Scheduled examinations fell about 50%. Roughly 40% of pending investigations were closed. Focus shifted toward depository institutions, clear fraud, and areas inside statutory authority. Mortgage origination and servicing remain on the priority list even as the overall exam calendar shrinks.

Mortgage rules under review. The bureau is reviewing ability-to-repay requirements and qualified mortgage definitions under Regulation Z, tied to the administration's push on mortgage credit access. It is also considering a final rule to streamline mortgage servicing under Regulation X, building on a July 2024 proposal meant to simplify loss-mitigation review. After the report period, it issued a July 2026 request for information on ways to promote mortgage credit access. None of that equals a finished rewrite of borrower qualification standards. Until a final rule publishes and takes effect, lenders still underwrite to current ATR/QM language, and brokers should not market "easier qualification" as if it is already live.

Fair lending and 1071. The bureau said it did not cite fair lending violations during the period and made no DOJ pattern-or-practice referrals, focusing instead on intentional discrimination with identifiable victims. It amended Regulation B in April, including changes around disparate-impact language and Special Purpose Credit Programs. A May rule revised Section 1071 small-business lending data collection: the annual revenue threshold moved from $5 million to $1 million, some data elements were removed, and compliance was pushed to Jan. 1, 2028. That date matters for shops that also do business-purpose or small-business related lending conversations.

Complaints. About 8.27 million consumer complaints arrived between July 1, 2025 and June 30, 2026. About 7.5 million went to companies, and companies responded to 99.6%. Separately, the bureau has limited public posting of unverified complaint narratives while saying it still collects complaints and shares information with regulators.

Client and shop takeaway. 1) Do not tell borrowers rules are already looser. 2) Watch ATR/QM and servicing proposals for real effective dates. 3) Nonbank and bank partners may face different exam intensity, but file quality still matters for buybacks and investor diligence. 4) For California and every state we serve, run today's checklists until the text of the rule changes.
