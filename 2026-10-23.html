3) ATTOM Q3: owning a median home takes about 32% of typical wages, up from 25% in Q1, and California has 12 of the 25 least affordable counties

So-what: Prices softened and wages grew, but higher rates wiped out the gain. In most of California, payment math, not price, is still the main barrier for buyers.

1-minute read

Lower prices were not enough to help buyers in the third quarter.

ATTOM's Q3 report, published Thursday, says major ownership costs on a median-priced home took about 32% of typical wages nationally. That is up from about 25% in the first quarter. Those costs include the mortgage payment, mortgage insurance, property taxes and homeowners insurance.

Costs were above historic norms in 570 of 583 counties. That compares with 560 in the first quarter.

In more than 3 in 10 counties, costs would take more than 43% of typical wages. ATTOM calls that seriously unaffordable.

There was some good news. Wages grew faster than home prices in almost 6 in 10 counties, including Los Angeles, San Diego, Phoenix and Miami. But rates climbed from about 6% early in the year to near 7.5% in early October, and that erased the gain.

California had 12 of the top 25 counties where ownership costs take the biggest share of wages.

For buyers, this explains why lower list prices still feel out of reach. A small rate drop matters more than a small price cut.

ATTOM's third quarter affordability report shows how rates cancelled out the good news on prices and wages.

The data, published Thursday and covered by Scotsman Guide, looks at the major costs of owning a median-priced single-family home or condo. That includes the mortgage payment, mortgage insurance, property taxes and homeowners insurance. ATTOM compares those costs with typical local wages.

Nationally, those costs took about 32% of typical wages in the third quarter. That is well above about 25% in the first quarter. For a buyer, that is a big jump in a short time.

The problem is broad. Costs were above historic norms in 570 of the 583 counties ATTOM studied. That compares with 560 counties in the first quarter. In more than 3 in 10 counties, the costs on a median-priced home would take more than 43% of typical wages. ATTOM labels those areas seriously unaffordable.

Some of the underlying trends were actually positive. Home prices declined nationally in the quarter. Wages grew faster than home prices in almost 6 in 10 counties. That included big markets like Los Angeles, Phoenix, San Diego and Miami. ATTOM CEO Rob Barber said those gains were not enough to meaningfully improve affordability across much of the country.

The reason is rates. Scotsman Guide notes mortgage rates rose from around 6% at the start of 2026 to recent highs near 7.5% in early October. It points to the inflation effects of the Iran war and AI spending, plus higher long-term government borrowing costs. Property taxes and insurance have also jumped in recent years, which keeps pressure on monthly payments.

California stands out. The state had 12 of the top 25 counties where homeownership costs take the greatest share of typical wages. New York had six, New Jersey had three and Hawaii had two.

What does that mean for buyers? In high-cost areas, a small price cut barely moves the payment. A change in rate moves it much more. That is why this week's drop in rates matters more than another few thousand dollars off a list price. It is also why insurance and property tax quotes belong in the first conversation, not at the end.

For first-time buyers, there are still tools. Rate buydowns, seller credits, and a careful look at loan type and down payment can close part of the gap. Buyers outside California face the same math in many markets. The core point holds across the country: affordability is mostly a payment problem right now.

For agents and referral partners, this is a useful framing. Price reductions get attention, but the payment is what decides whether a buyer can move. Pairing a price cut with a seller credit toward a lower rate often does more for the buyer than a bigger cut alone.

This week's NMHC survey is a commercial real estate story, but it runs on the same engine as the mortgage market: the 10-year Treasury.

The National Multifamily Housing Council surveys apartment owners, developers and lenders every quarter. Bill McBride posted the October results at Calculated Risk on Thursday morning under the headline "Apartment Market Conditions Weaken."

Here are the four indexes. For each one, 50 is the breakeven line.

Market Tightness fell to 47 from 57 in July. Twenty-six percent said conditions were looser than three months ago, 21% said tighter and 53% said unchanged.

Sales Volume came in at 22. Sixty-one percent saw fewer deals, and only 4% saw more.

Equity Financing fell to 17. Two-thirds said equity money became harder to find, and just 1% said easier.

Debt Financing came in at 10. Eighty-one percent said it is a worse time to borrow, and only 2% said better.

NMHC chief economist Chris Bruen tied it to rates. "Higher rates of inflation have caused longer-term interest rates to spike in recent months, leading to an increase in the cost of both debt and equity capital," he said. Buyers now "demand lower valuations to make their deals pencil." The 10-year averaged 5.23% so far in October, up 63 basis points from July and 117 from a year earlier.

The refinance question is the part to remember. Many owners borrowed in 2020 and 2021 at very low rates and now must refinance at much higher rates or ask lenders for modifications. A majority, 53%, said they cannot refinance the full maturing balance without exceeding lenders' debt service or loan-to-value limits.

NMHC broke it down further. Twenty-two percent have few loans maturing now. Of the other 78%, 61% said debt service or loan-to-value limits block a full refinance. Forty-five percent will inject more equity. Thirty-nine percent said lenders are allowing modifications and extensions for now. Sixteen percent will turn to mezzanine debt or preferred equity. Seventeen percent said they may face foreclosure on some properties.

McBride's read: this index has been an excellent leading indicator for rents and vacancy rates, so asking rents and vacancies should stay under some pressure, even though monthly rent data show early signs that asking rents are near a bottom. Together with the architecture billings index, it also points to further weakness in multifamily starts.

Why it matters to our audience: softer rents can make renting look cheaper than buying in the short run. But fewer apartments being started means less new supply later, and the same rate move that froze apartment refinancing is what keeps buyer payments high. When the 10-year comes down, both sides get relief. This week's drop toward 5% helps, but the survey shows how much damage a few months above 5.2% can do to anyone who has to borrow.

Thursday's L.A. Times business section asked whether California's economy is in trouble. Friday's real estate story points to one long-running reason housing is so tight: the region stopped building.

Jack Flemming's story draws on a USC Lusk Center for Real Estate study of "naturally occurring affordable homes," meaning rentals that are cheap without a government subsidy.

The age numbers stand out. The average L.A. County home is 58 years old. That is 11 years older than the California average and 16 years older than the national average. L.A. is a young city, but its housing is old because of a 20th century building boom that has since slowed sharply. The county added more than 70,000 housing units in the 1950s and fewer than 15,000 in the 2010s.

"It underscores a massive slowdown in construction over the past several decades," said USC research professor Jared Schachner, a co-author. "When you're not bringing in a large influx of units, that's the result: a large share of old buildings with a higher probability of being dilapidated."

The study found the most affordable apartments tend to be in five- to 19-unit buildings built before 2000. They make up 43% of the county's multifamily stock with five or more units. Koreatown-Mid-City has the highest share of these naturally affordable rentals at 62%, followed by Hollywood-Studio City at 61% and Burbank-Glendale at 57%. The oldest neighborhoods, such as Cypress Park, Vermont Square, South Park and Harvard Park, average at least 90 years.

The problem is money. Repairs cost more since the pandemic, and private owners want higher rents to pay for them. Donovan Rypkema of PlaceEconomics said preservation should come first and pointed to San Antonio's Under 1 Roof program, which gives homeowners grants of up to $19,000 for roof repairs.

Azeen Khanmalek of Abundant Housing L.A. said 75% of the city's residential land is zoned for single-family homes, which pushes development pressure onto older neighborhoods like Echo Park and Silver Lake. He backs building more under SB 79, the state law raising density near transit. L.A. delayed it until 2030 but will allow up to 16 units in 55 single-family zones.

The city's main tool is Measure ULA, the so-called mansion tax, which has raised more than $1.4 billion since 2023. A $466.6 million funding round is open through Dec. 4, and the Small NOAH program will have $102 million over four years.

Why it matters to buyers: older homes often come with deferred maintenance, so inspections, insurance and repair budgets matter as much as the price. And the lack of new building is a big reason prices stay high even when demand cools. The same supply story shows up in many of the other states we lend in. For first-time buyers, a newer condo or townhome can cost more up front but less in surprise repairs, so it is worth comparing total monthly cost, not just price.

6) Trump signs the housing cost executive order: MI cancellation pilot, permanent title waiver program, cheaper refinances and lower USDA and VA direct loan rates

Wed show

So-what: The order is now official, but most of it needs FHFA, Fannie, Freddie, USDA and VA to write the rules. The near-term wins are mostly for refinances, not purchases.

President Trump signed "Decreasing Housing Costs for the American People" on Thursday. Scotsman Guide says the signed version closely matches the draft it reported Tuesday.

HousingWire lists the main pieces. FHFA will create a pilot to proactively cancel mortgage insurance for borrowers who have enough equity based on current values. FHFA's title insurance waiver pilot becomes a permanent, larger program. USDA and VA are told to cut rates on their single-family loans. A new task force, co-chaired by HUD's Scott Turner and FHFA's Bill Pulte, will work on manufactured and modular housing.

Scotsman Guide says some steps can move quickly, like lower loan-level price adjustments on eligible refinances. Others, like waiving appraisals and income reverification on certain cash-out refinances, need industry input.

MBA, CHLA and the Broker Action Coalition praised it. The title industry urged caution. KBW analysts expect limited impact because the title waiver applies only to refinances, about 7% of title premiums.

For buyers, the clearest near-term benefit is cheaper mortgage insurance removal later. Purchase pricing does not change today.

Expand 3-minute read

Thursday's brief said the housing order was still unsigned. That changed Thursday afternoon.

President Trump signed an executive order titled "Decreasing Housing Costs for the American People." A White House spokesperson confirmed the signing, according to Scotsman Guide, and the signed version closely matched the draft it reported on Tuesday, apart from some revised section headings. The order says it is administration policy "to reduce regulatory barriers to mortgage origination and costs impeding homeownership."

Here is what it does, based on HousingWire's review of the White House fact sheet and Scotsman Guide's reading of the order.

First, mortgage insurance. FHFA is directed to create a pilot program that proactively cancels mortgage insurance for borrowers who meet equity requirements based on current property values. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are told to review their books for borrowers eligible for proactive cancellation of excessive mortgage insurance.

Second, title insurance. FHFA's title insurance waiver pilot, launched in 2024, becomes permanent and expands. That lets more refinance borrowers use alternatives to traditional title insurance.

Third, refinance costs. Scotsman Guide says faster steps include lower loan-level pricing fees on eligible refinances. Slower steps include waiving appraisals and income reverification for certain cash-out refinances.

Fourth, government loans. USDA and VA are told to lower rates on the single-family loans they make and guarantee.

Fifth, manufactured housing. A Task Force on Manufactured Housing Innovation, co-chaired by HUD Secretary Scott Turner and FHFA Director Bill Pulte, will recommend ways to align and streamline federal standards for factory-built homes.

Reaction was mostly positive. MBA's Bob Broeksmit welcomed the refinance and manufactured housing steps and promised to work on a timely rollout. CHLA said the actions have long been its priorities. Brendan McKay of the Broker Action Coalition called it a big deal and said streamlined refinances and title waivers could cut closing costs by thousands of dollars. Pulte called it the greatest housing executive order in history.

Not everyone is convinced. ALTA, the title industry group, warned that expanding waivers should be done with caution so financial risks do not shift onto families. KBW analysts wrote that the market impact is likely limited. The title pilot works only in a few regions and only for eligible refinances, which are about 7% of title premiums. If it ever expands to purchases, title insurers could face more risk.

What it means for brokers: watch for FHFA and agency guidance before telling clients anything is available. The biggest near-term value is on refinances, and only once rates give people a reason to refinance.

What it means for buyers: purchase pricing does not change today. The mortgage insurance piece is the most useful part for first-time buyers with small down payments, because it could make removing MI easier once their home gains value.

7) Deephaven: a non-QM rate that beats everyone else may not survive a market move, as unhedged lenders sell loans near 98 and double extension fees

So-what: For self-employed and investor borrowers, the cheapest non-QM quote can turn into the most expensive if the lender cannot fund or changes lock terms mid-pipeline.

1-minute read

A low non-QM rate can be a warning sign.

In an MPA interview, Deephaven Mortgage chief sales officer Tom Davis says some newer non-QM lenders came in over the past six months with aggressive pricing to win market share. Then rates moved, and many were not hedged.

Loans they expected to sell at 102 or 103 are now selling at about 98, he says.

Davis has seen lenders change lock policies, stop honoring some terms, and double extension costs overnight on existing pipelines. Losses can break warehouse line covenants and trigger margin calls. That can cut a lender's ability to fund loans.

Non-agency lending is now about 20% of the market, Davis says. Optimal Blue's September data showed non-QM locks up 8% from August while total volume fell 2%.

More volume may be coming. Fannie Mae retired limited condo reviews in August, and condo reserve requirements rise from 10% to 15% of the annual budget on January 4, 2027. Davis expects many condo loans to shift to private label.

For BuyWise, the takeaway is to check a non-QM lender's track record, lock policy and extension costs before chasing the lowest rate.

Expand 3-minute read

Non-QM is growing, and that is pulling in new lenders. A Deephaven executive says some of them are pricing loans in a way they cannot sustain.

In an MPA interview published October 21, Deephaven Mortgage chief sales officer Tom Davis said brokers should look closely at how a non-QM lender has handled rate swings before trusting a rate that looks too good. He said you want an investor with a proven track record that has managed volatility with discipline.

Here is the problem. Over the past six months, Davis said, a couple of new entrants tried to grab market share with lower note rates. Then the market moved. They were holding loans at low rates and were not hedged. "Now they're selling loans that they thought they were going to make 102, 103. Now they're selling them at like 98," he said. Hedging non-QM is harder than hedging agency loans, he added, because lenders must manage spread risk as well as duration.

There is history here. Sprout Mortgage shut down abruptly in July 2022 after rising rates left it with loans it could not sell at par. First Guaranty Mortgage closed weeks earlier.

Davis also described behavior brokers should watch for now. He has seen lenders change lock policies, stop honoring certain terms, and double extension costs overnight on loans already in the pipeline. Some non-QM lenders were collecting loans without locking them and then got caught when they went to sell.

The damage can spread. If a lender takes losses or cannot sell loans, it can break warehouse covenants. That leads to margin calls, a cut credit line, or losing the ability to fund. For a borrower, that can mean a delayed or failed closing.

Why non-QM matters more now: Davis said non-agency lending is about 20% of the market. Optimal Blue's September data showed non-QM lock volume up 8% from August while total lock volume fell 2%. Condo rules are one driver. Fannie Mae retired its limited condo reviews in August, so many established buildings now need a full review. Reserve requirements for those reviews rise from 10% to 15% of the annual budget on January 4, 2027. Davis expects that to push many condo loans into private label lending.

He also expects more consolidation across the industry, including among non-QM lenders.

What it means for BuyWise: for self-employed bank statement borrowers, DSCR investors and condo buyers, lender choice matters as much as the rate. Before locking, confirm the lender's lock policy and extension costs in writing. Ask how long they have been in non-QM, and how they handled the rate spike in 2022. A quarter point saved is not worth a closing that falls apart.

For clients, the simple message is that the lowest non-QM quote is not always the best deal. Reliable funding on the closing date is part of the price.

The appraisal overhaul starts November 2, and it is going to be uneven. That creates new questions for brokers and their clients.

Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac have kept the November 2 mandate for UAD 3.6, the new appraisal dataset and report format. But they gave lenders that are not ready a temporary exception. Lenders granted relief can keep submitting the older UAD 2.6 reports through May 19, 2027. Starting March 1, loans backed by those older reports lose collateral rep and warranty relief for value, and the agencies' collateral review tools stop returning standard risk scores. The agencies say the exception is one-time and will not be extended.

In an MPA interview published October 21, NAMB president Michael Farrell said brokers need to know which format each lender uses. Because the exception is granted to lenders, different wholesale partners may be on different formats, and the client's cost and timeline can depend on which one handles the loan.

The cost gap is real. Farrell said that in the last two weeks, an appraisal management company offered his shop a 2.6 appraisal for $555 or a 3.6 appraisal for $850. The reason for the roughly $300 difference, he said, is simple supply and demand: not enough field appraisers are ready to accept 3.6 orders. Many want to switch but are waiting on their software.

He expects two things. Prices go up, and timelines stretch. NAMB expects a $200 to $300 cost difference and a 3 to 5 day delay in getting orders finalized. The consumer pays for both.

There is also a workforce problem. Farrell said the Appraisal Foundation and the Appraisal Institute expect 23% to 25% of field appraisers to leave the field rather than adopt the new format. The average field appraiser is over 62. He worries the six-month reprieve lets some delay the decision.

Some lenders may stay on 2.6 as long as they can, because it is cheaper for the consumer and faster. That could become a competitive edge in the short run.

Farrell's practical advice for brokers is clear. When disclosing appraisal costs, disclose the highest possible outcome if the lender might use 3.6. Educate agents early. Agents writing 30-day contracts may need longer timelines to allow for slower appraisals. Sellers should also expect longer inspections, because the 3.6 report asks for more data points.

What it means for BuyWise: add the appraisal format to the lender comparison along with rate and fees. Build extra days into lock periods for loans that will use 3.6. Tell agents now, before November contracts are written. Farrell's point is that brokers who explain this early can become the go-to expert for their referral partners.

For buyers, the message is short. Appraisals may cost a few hundred dollars more and take a few extra days this winter. Plan for it in the contract timeline, so it does not become a surprise.
