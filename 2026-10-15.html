1) Paradoxical rally: UMBS 6.0 jumps to 99.19 (+0.59) and the 10-year falls to 5.054% even after a hot PPI

So-what: This is the best pricing of the week, but it is coming from fear money leaving AI stocks, not from cooler inflation. Lock conversations should treat it as a window that can close quickly.

1-minute read

Bonds are rallying hard this morning even though wholesale inflation came in hot.

By MBS Live's Morning update at 6:42 AM PT, UMBS 6.0 was 99.19, up 0.59. The 10-year Treasury was 5.054%, down about 13 basis points. The 2-year was 4.715% and the 30-year was 5.397%. Earlier alerts showed the climb: 98.98 at 5:04 AM, 99.09 at 5:13 AM and 99.26 at 5:43 AM.

This sits on top of Wednesday's comeback. UMBS 6.0 traded near 97.7 right after CPI and recovered to roughly 98.60 by the close.

Matt Graham's read is that core PPI at 0.7% versus 0.3% expected would normally send yields higher. Instead, traders saw that PPI should add less than 0.1% to the Fed's preferred PCE inflation gauge in two weeks. The bigger driver is worry about an AI debt bubble, with investors moving money from stocks into Treasuries. Yields near multi-decade highs last week also looked like a bargain to buyers.

Graham's caution: past 30 basis point corrections have often given way to more selling. Freddie Mac's PMMS lands around 9 AM PT.

Expand 3-minute read

For two days in a row, the bond market has done the opposite of what the inflation headlines suggested. That matters for anyone holding a lock decision this morning.

Start with the numbers. MBS Live's Morning update, sent at 6:42 AM PT, had UMBS 6.0 at 99.19, up 0.59 from Wednesday's close. UMBS 6.5 was 101.42, up 0.52. GNMA 6.0 was 99.44, up 0.53. The 15-year UMBS 5.0 was 97.77, up 0.57. On the Treasury side, the 10-year was 5.054%, down about 13 basis points. The 2-year was 4.715%, the 5-year 4.863% and the 30-year 5.397%. MBS Live's 4:30 AM PT pre-market alert already had the 10-year at 5.083%, so a big chunk of this move happened overnight, before any data hit.

Now the context. On Wednesday morning, hot September CPI knocked UMBS 6.0 down to about 97.7 at the lows. By the close it had recovered to roughly 98.60. Today's gain stacks on top of that. In plain English, the price of the 6.0 coupon has climbed about a point and a half from Wednesday's worst level. Higher MBS prices generally mean better rate sheets, so pricing this morning should beat anything seen earlier this week.

Why is this happening with inflation running hot? Matt Graham at MBS Live laid out three reasons. First, core PPI was 0.7% for September versus 0.3% expected. Normally that alone would push yields up. But traders mapped the PPI components into the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, the PCE price index, and found it should add less than 0.1% to the PCE reading due in about two weeks. So the hot headline did less damage than it looked.

Second, and most important, worry about an AI debt bubble is driving a flight to safety. Wednesday was the worst day for stocks since July, with the S&P 500 down 1.11% and the Nasdaq down 1.88%, led by AI infrastructure names. When portfolio managers trim stocks, some of that money goes into Treasuries and mortgage bonds. That buying pushes yields down.

Third, yields hit multi-decade highs last week. At those levels, some investors simply saw a good entry point.

Graham was clear that this could be the start of a bigger correction, but it is not guaranteed. He noted the market has seen plenty of 30 basis point corrections that later gave way to more selling. If AI fears calm down, the bid for bonds could fade quickly. That is not a forecast, it is the reason to keep this move in perspective.

Other data out at 5:30 AM PT did not change the picture much. Retail sales rose 0.8%, the control group cooled, and jobless claims were 199,000. Those numbers are covered in the next item.

What to do with this today: pull fresh pricing rather than relying on Wednesday's sheets. For clients who were waiting for a better day, this is a better day, and it came without any good inflation news to support it. That is worth saying out loud. Freddie Mac's weekly PMMS comes out around 9 AM PT. Its survey mostly reflects applications from earlier in the week, so it will not fully capture this rally. Watch afternoon reprices if the stock market stabilizes.

2) Hot PPI and a gas-heavy shopping month: wholesale prices +1.3% (6.3% y/y), retail sales +0.8%, claims 199,000

So-what: Inflation is spreading from fuel into freight and services, while shoppers outside the gas pump are slowing down. That mix keeps Fed hike talk alive even when bonds rally.

1-minute read

Three reports hit at 5:30 AM PT, and the inflation one was the loudest.

The Producer Price Index rose 1.3% in September and 6.3% from a year ago, per the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Economists polled by Dow Jones expected 0.6% and 5.5%. CNBC says those are the biggest monthly and annual gains since March 2022 and December 2022. Core PPI, which strips out food and energy, rose 0.7% for the month and 5.0% for the year.

Energy did most of the damage as the Iran war squeezed oil supply. The BLS said almost half the rise in processed goods for immediate demand came from diesel. Brent settled near $106 on Wednesday as two more ships sank near the Strait of Hormuz.

Retail sales rose 0.8%, beating forecasts near 0.3% to 0.4%, after a revised 1.3% in August. But excluding autos and gas stations, sales rose just 0.25%, down from about 1.1%. A lot of spending went to the pump.

Initial jobless claims were 199,000, up 1,000. Continuing claims were 1.71 million. Layoffs are still low. For borrowers, the takeaway is higher everyday costs with jobs still holding.

Expand 3-minute read

Thursday's data batch tells a story borrowers already feel: prices are climbing, fuel is a big reason, and people are cutting back elsewhere to keep up.

Producer prices first. The Bureau of Labor Statistics said the Producer Price Index for final demand rose 1.3% in September after a 0.4% to 0.5% gain in August, depending on revisions. That compares with a 0.6% consensus from economists polled by Dow Jones. On a 12-month basis, PPI rose 6.3%, well above the 5.5% expected and up from 5.4% in August. CNBC noted the monthly gain was the largest since March 2022 and the annual gain the largest since December 2022. Core PPI, excluding food and energy, rose 0.7% for the month and 5.0% for the year.

Peter Boockvar added detail in his morning note. Core goods rose 0.3% for the month and 5.1% over the year. Services rose 0.8% for the month and 4.9% over the year. Truck freight jumped 3.8% in a single month and is up 19.3% from a year ago. That freight number is the clearest sign of fuel costs feeding into the price of moving goods.

The energy link is direct. CNBC tied the surge to the U.S. war with Iran, which has cut oil supply. The BLS said almost half of September's increase in processed goods for immediate demand traced back to diesel prices. Overnight, Reuters reported a container ship and a bulk carrier sank near the Strait of Hormuz after being hit by projectiles. Iran says it is stepping up strikes in response to the U.S. blockade of its ports. Brent settled near $106 a barrel on Wednesday. That keeps fuel and shipping costs high, which is the main route from geopolitics into mortgage rates.

Retail sales came in at plus 0.8% for September per the Commerce Department, beating forecasts that ranged from 0.3% to 0.4%. August was revised to 1.3%. Look underneath and it is weaker. Excluding autos and gas stations, sales rose about 0.25%, down from roughly 1.1% the month before. AP's headline said a big chunk of spending went to gas bills. Boockvar flagged core retail at 0.1% versus 0.3% expected, with building materials down. He described a split consumer, with higher-income households still spending and others pulling back.

Jobless claims stayed calm. Initial claims were 199,000 for the week ending October 10, up 1,000 from a revised 198,000. The four-week average was 198,500. Continuing claims, a measure of people still collecting benefits, were 1.71 million. Truflation's research team said the report does not show a layoff wave, though stable claims can coexist with slow hiring.

Regional factory surveys were firm too. Boockvar listed the October Empire State index at 25.5 and the Philly Fed at 20.9.

Here is why this matters for borrowers. Lenders qualify people on debt-to-income ratios, and higher costs for gas, groceries and insurance squeeze the budget even when the ratio looks fine on paper. A client who qualifies at 45% DTI may feel stretched sooner than last year. Talk through the full monthly budget, not just the payment. On the rate side, CNBC said fed funds futures now show about a 34% chance of a hike on October 28, up from under 18% a week ago, and about 90% odds of at least one hike by year end. Bonds are rallying anyway, which is the paradox in the previous item.

The Fed's own officials moved markets about as much as the CPI report did this week. Here is what they said and why it matters for rate conversations.

San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly told Reuters further rate increases are more likely than not to be needed, but she did not feel any urgency and preferred a thoughtful decision over reacting to limited information. She attributed much of the headline jump to gasoline and said core inflation remains sticky, as expected. How to use this with clients: the Fed sets short-term rates, and mortgage rates follow the 10-year Treasury and MBS more closely. A Fed hike can coexist with lower mortgage rates if bond investors are scared about growth or stocks, which is exactly what is happening this morning. Keep the explanation simple. The Fed is worried about inflation, bond investors are worried about AI debt, and mortgage pricing is caught in the middle.

Expand 3-minute read

Mortgage rates move with the 10-year Treasury and MBS, and right now the strongest outside force on both is fear about how much debt the AI build-out is carrying.

Start with CoreWeave, one of the largest AI data center operators. Peter Boockvar's Thursday note said CoreWeave's five-year credit default swaps reached 1,117 basis points. A credit default swap is essentially insurance against a borrower defaulting. The higher the price, the more investors are paying to protect themselves. Boockvar said the swaps first crossed 1,000 basis points in July, fell back below 700, and are now at new highs. He said that pricing implies more than a 50% chance of default. CoreWeave's five-year bond trades around 80 cents on the dollar, for a yield near 15.7%. He also noted the company has roughly $16 billion in long-term lease obligations.

CNBC reported CoreWeave stock sank 15.5% on Wednesday as its bond yields pushed toward 15%. The damage was broader than one company. The S&P 500 fell 1.11% to 7,718.94 and the Nasdaq dropped 1.88% to 26,692.87, the worst one-day declines since July. Oracle fell about 9% and Dell about 11%, and chipmakers declined too. The Wall Street Journal's headline summed it up as AI jitters and rate-hike fears combining for the worst day for stocks since July.

Boockvar added a big-picture number from Gartner: more than $3.5 trillion of AI infrastructure spending could be impaired by 2030. He cited about $1 trillion of AI capital spending in 2026 and another $6 trillion planned from 2027 to 2030. By his math, that spending needs about $8.5 trillion in revenue to pay off, versus forecasts at or below $3.6 trillion. He also said he thinks global bond yields have topped in the short term. That is his view.

Here is the link to mortgages. Matt Graham at MBS Live wrote this morning that concerns over an AI debt bubble, and big losses in AI stocks, are leading investors to move money back into Treasuries and portfolio managers to trim stocks in favor of bonds. That buying is why the 10-year fell to about 5.05% and UMBS 6.0 rose to 99.19 even with hot PPI. In other words, borrowers are getting help from a scare in a completely different corner of the market.

Two cautions. First, a fear-driven bond rally depends on the fear. Graham said things could reverse surprisingly quickly if the market finds a reason to calm down on AI. Second, credit stress can tighten lending more broadly. If investors get nervous about risky debt, spreads on non-QM securities and other private credit can widen, which can show up as higher pricing on DSCR and bank-statement loans even while agency rates improve. Watch for that in wholesale non-QM rate sheets.

For clients, keep it plain: stocks tied to AI had a rough day, investors ran to the safety of government bonds, and that pushed mortgage rates down a bit. It is a real improvement in pricing. It is not a sign that inflation is solved.

Fannie's Economic and Strategic Research Group now projects the 30-year fixed rate averaging 7.4% in the fourth quarter and 7.5% in every quarter of 2027. Last month it had 6.8% this quarter and 6.7% for 2027, so the 2027 number moved up 0.8 percentage points. Expand 3-minute read

Fannie Mae publishes a housing and economic forecast every month. October's version is worth a close read because it removes most of the rate relief that had been built into next year.

The rate path. Fannie's Economic and Strategic Research Group now expects the 30-year fixed mortgage rate to average 7.4% in the fourth quarter of 2026 and 7.5% in every quarter of 2027. In September, it had 6.8% for the current quarter and 6.7% for 2027. Mortgage Professional America calculated the 2027 revision at 0.8 percentage points. Fannie's rate forecasts are anchored to market levels on September 30. MPA noted the 30-year fixed averaged 7.46% by October 13, so the new figures may already trail the market. This morning's bond rally cuts the other way. That is the point: these numbers are a snapshot of conditions at month end.

The Treasury assumption. Fannie's Economic Forecast holds the 10-year Treasury yield at 5.3% in every quarter from late 2026 through 2027. The same table has the federal funds rate averaging 4.0% in the fourth quarter and 4.3% to 4.4% through 2027, and unemployment near 4.2% to 4.3%. TheStreet compared that with MBA, which projects a 10-year yield of 5.1% through the end of 2026 and 5.0% for most of 2027, which explains why MBA's mortgage rate path is lower. Both groups keep 30-year rates above 7% through 2027.

The volume picture. Fannie now sees single-family originations falling from about $2.09 trillion in 2026 to $1.87 trillion in 2027, roughly an 11% drop. Purchase lending holds near $1.43 trillion both years. Refinance lending shrinks from $655 billion to $430 billion. That moves refinance share from 31% of originations to 23%. By quarter, Fannie expects originations of $448 billion in the current quarter and $377 billion in the first quarter of 2027, the seasonal low.

Housing activity. Fannie's table has total housing starts slipping from about 1.34 million in 2026 to 1.30 million in 2027. Its home price index is forecast to rise 2.7% in 2026 and only 0.6% in 2027, measured fourth quarter over fourth quarter.

Industry voices. MBA chief economist Mike Fratantoni told the MBA convention that inflation is too high, leading central banks to raise short-term rates, while budget deficits and debt are unsustainably high, leading bond investors to push up long-term rates. Cotality chief economist Selma Hepp said mortgage rates could reach 9% in a severe scenario where Treasuries hit 6% to 7%, but stressed that is not her base case.

What this means in practice. A $500,000 loan at 7.5% runs about $3,496 a month in principal and interest. At 6.7%, the old 2027 assumption, it would be about $3,226. That $270 a month gap is the refinance savings that just disappeared from Fannie's outlook. For a broker shop, the takeaway is to build 2027 plans around purchase volume, which Fannie expects to hold steady, rather than a refi rebound.

For Emily and Jake, the honest message is that nobody serious is forecasting a quick return to 6%. Buy a home because the payment works today. If rates do fall, a refinance is a bonus, not the plan. Forecasts move every month, so treat this as one data point, not a guarantee.

6) New-home sales estimate drops to 584,000, the weakest since April 2020, as builder mortgage applications fall 8% in September

So-what: First-time buyers are leaving new construction fastest, which means builders will lean harder on rate buydowns and incentives. That is leverage for buyers who can still qualify.

1-minute read

MBA's Builder Application Survey shows the September rate jump hit new-home buyers hard.

Mortgage applications for new home purchases fell 8.3% from a year ago and 8% from August. These figures are not seasonally adjusted. The survey tracks applications at mortgage companies owned by home builders.

MBA uses that data to estimate new home sales. It pegged September at a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 584,000 units, down 12% from August's 664,000 pace. Joel Kan, MBA's deputy chief economist, said it was the weakest pace since April 2020. Unadjusted, MBA estimates 47,000 new home sales in September, down 9.6% from 52,000 in August.

The mix shifted away from first-time buyers. FHA share fell to 34.7% from about 35% in August. Conventional loans were 50.2%, VA 14.1% and USDA 1.0%. Average loan size rose slightly to $374,724 from $373,194.

Expand 3-minute read

New construction has been the release valve for buyers in a market short on resale listings. September's builder data says that valve is closing, at least for now.

The headline numbers. The Mortgage Bankers Association's Builder Application Survey for September showed mortgage applications for new home purchases down 8.3% from a year ago and down 8% from August. MBA does not seasonally adjust these figures. The survey tracks application volume at the mortgage subsidiaries of home builders across the country, so it is a fast read on what is happening in builder sales offices.

The sales estimate. MBA uses the survey, plus assumptions about market coverage, to estimate new single-family home sales before the Census Bureau releases its official count. MBA said that estimate has consistently been a leading indicator of the Census report. For September, MBA estimated a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 584,000 units, down 12% from August's 664,000 pace. Joel Kan, MBA's vice president and deputy chief economist, called it the weakest sales pace since April 2020, when the pandemic shut down much of the economy. On an unadjusted basis, MBA estimated 47,000 new home sales in September, down 9.6% from 52,000 in August.

For context, August's report had shown a small bounce. HousingWire reported then that MBA's August estimate was 664,000, up 2.6% from July, but still 9% below a year earlier. September erased that gain and then some.

Who stepped back. Kan said, "The increase in mortgage rates in September had a chilling effect for new home purchase activity." He added that the FHA share of applications decreased and the average loan size increased, as higher rates had a greater impact on first-time homebuyers' purchase decisions. The numbers: FHA loans made up 34.7% of applications, down from about 35% in August. Conventional loans were 50.2%, VA 14.1% and USDA 1.0%. The average new-home loan size rose to $374,724 from $373,194.

That mix matters. FHA is the go-to product for first-time buyers with smaller down payments. When its share drops while the average loan size rises, it suggests buyers at the lower end of the budget range are the ones dropping out, while higher-budget buyers are still showing up.

What it means for builders. Builders carrying finished homes have strong reasons to keep offering rate buydowns, closing cost credits and price cuts. Earlier ResiClub research this month showed builders' buyers already carrying stretched debt-to-income ratios, so incentives are doing a lot of the work. The Census Bureau's official September new home sales report will show whether MBA's estimate holds.

How to use it. For first-time buyers who can qualify, new construction may offer more help right now than resale sellers will. Compare a builder's forward commitment buydown against a price reduction, and look at the all-in monthly payment, not just the rate. Ask whether the incentive requires the builder's in-house lender, and compare that offer side by side with an outside quote. A lower note rate can still cost more if fees and pricing are higher elsewhere in the deal. For clients on the fence, the takeaway is that fewer buyers in the builder pipeline usually means more room to negotiate.

7) Falling inventory is a false signal: pending sales down 15.4% y/y and 42.9% of listings cut, per HousingWire Data

So-what: Fewer listings is not the same as a hotter market. When buyers pull back faster than sellers, buyers gain leverage, and local pending sales tell you more than inventory counts.

1-minute read

HousingWire Data says the seasonal drop in listings is hiding weaker demand.

National active inventory fell 0.4% in the week ending October 9 to 898,139 single-family homes. That is still 4.8% above the same week in 2025. The more important number: new pending sales fell to 54,060, down 15.4% from 63,883 a year earlier. About 42.89% of active listings had a price cut, versus 41.84% a year ago.

Logan Mohtashami's tracker had mortgage rates at 7.48% and spreads at 2.12%. He has called 6.64% a key threshold for demand.

Three metros show different stories. In Dallas-Fort Worth, inventory fell 3.3% from July, but weekly pending sales dropped 32%, about 52% of listings had cuts and the median list price fell to $435,000 from $450,000. In Denver, inventory rose 11.4%, pendings fell 9.1% and 54.6% of listings were cut. San Francisco was the outlier: pending sales rose 39.3% since July and only 28.5% of listings had cuts, though pendings were still 16% below last year.

The lesson: judge a local market by pending sales and price cuts, not by inventory alone.

Expand 3-minute read

Every fall, the number of homes for sale starts to drop. This year, HousingWire Data argues that drop is misleading, because buyers are leaving faster than listings are.

The national picture. For the week ending October 9, active single-family inventory fell 0.4% to 898,139 homes. Inventory is still 4.8% higher than the same week in 2025. New pending sales, homes going under contract, fell to 54,060, down 15.4% from 63,883 a year earlier. The share of active listings with a price reduction was 42.89%, compared with 41.84% a year earlier. In plain terms, slightly fewer homes are for sale, but far fewer buyers are signing contracts, and more sellers are cutting prices.

The rate backdrop. HousingWire Lead Analyst Logan Mohtashami's latest tracker put mortgage rates at 7.48%, with the 10-year Treasury near 5.35% at that time and mortgage spreads widened to 2.12%. Mohtashami has consistently pointed to 6.64% as an important mortgage-rate threshold for housing demand. With rates well above that, purchase applications and pending sales have weakened. Note that the 10-year has since dropped toward 5.05% this morning, so the backdrop is shifting quickly.

Dallas-Fort Worth. Active inventory fell 3.3% from July 17 to October 9, from 31,296 to 30,269 homes. On its own that looks like tightening. But weekly new pending sales fell 32% over the same stretch, from 2,026 to 1,378. In July, Dallas was running 2.8% ahead of last year's pending sales. By October 9, it was 25.7% behind. About 52% of listings had a price cut, and the median list price slipped from $450,000 to $435,000. Withdrawals jumped to 1,390 in the latest week from 724 the week before, so some of the inventory decline is sellers giving up rather than homes selling.

Denver. Inventory rose 11.4%, from 8,166 to 9,100 homes. Weekly pending sales fell 9.1%, from 607 to 552, and were 14.8% below last year. The share of listings with price cuts reached 54.6%, among the highest of the 30 major metros HousingWire examined.
