Logan Mohtashami's weekend column at HousingWire makes one big point: mortgage spreads are the most important housing story right now. It is a good frame for Wednesday's show because it explains why mortgage rates feel worse than the 10-year alone would suggest.

Start with the basics. The spread is the gap between the 30-year mortgage rate and the 10-year Treasury yield. It covers prepayment risk, servicing costs, and what MBS investors demand to hold mortgages instead of Treasuries. Last week it widened to 2.12%, up from 1.98% the prior week. Over the long run it has usually sat around 1.60% to 1.80%.

Do the simple math. With the 10-year near 5.24% on Friday, a 2.12% spread lands right around Freddie Mac's 7.40% average. If spreads were back at 1.80%, the same 10-year would point to a rate closer to 7.0%. At 1.60%, closer to 6.8%. That gap is real money on a monthly payment.

Logan also notes that the 10-year held the key 5.35% line last week, despite bad news. Holding there is a positive. His line in the sand for this week is clear. He does not want to see a 10-year close above 5.35% followed by more selling. That would open the door to a new leg higher in rates.

This week has plenty that could test that level. There is a CPI report and a long list of Fed speakers. Inflation has been sticky, and the Fed has said it is willing to hike. A hot CPI print could push the 10-year back toward the top of its range.

Then there is geopolitics, which drives most of the volatility. Logan ties it to the collapse of the Iran and U.S. memorandum of understanding in July, and to President Trump saying in September there would be no deal until after the midterms. Trump has also said there will be no attack on Iran before the midterms. Markets are trying to price both the conflict and the election calendar at once, and that uncertainty shows up in wider spreads.

Logan is candid about his own forecast. His 2026 ranges called for mortgage rates of 5.75% to 6.75% and a 10-year of 3.80% to 4.60%. Both have already been exceeded. He also flags the risk of mortgage rates above 8% if the conflict worsens.

There is also a useful second view from MBA. At the MBA Annual in Chicago, chief economist Mike Fratantoni said the mortgage to Treasury spread was about 300 basis points in 2023, got as low as 200 this year, and is back around 220. He expects it to widen. Different measures, same message: spreads are moving the wrong way.

What it means for Emily and Jake: rate relief needs two things, not one. The 10-year has to calm down, and spreads have to come back toward normal. Right now neither is happening fast. Buyers should plan around today's rates, keep a refinance option in mind for later, and not count on a quick drop to the 6s.

The Mortgage Bankers Association released its updated forecast at the MBA Annual in Chicago, and HousingWire's write-up is a reality check for anyone counting on a 2027 rebound.

The Federal Housing Finance Agency just told Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac to open VantageScore 4.0 to all lenders. For years, Classic FICO was the default score for almost every GSE mortgage. That near-monopoly sat on top of a roughly $13 trillion U.S. mortgage market. FHFA's move is the clearest signal yet that the agencies want real competition on credit scores. Markets have already reacted: FICO shares are down about 60% this year as investors reprice the mortgage franchise.

Officials say dual-score choice can expand access and lower friction for borrowers who score better under VantageScore 4.0 than under Classic FICO. Younger borrowers, thin-file borrowers, and some renters with limited traditional credit history are the groups often cited. The policy pitch is simple: cheaper and easier mortgages if more people clear the automated underwriting gate. Not everyone buys that. Some bank and credit analysts warn that if the new model is less conservative, credit losses could rise later in the cycle. If investors price that risk into MBS spreads, the rate borrowers actually pay could go up even if the score itself is cheaper. That debate matters more with mortgage rates already at multi-year highs and a midterm election calendar ahead.

What changes for lenders on day to day ops is almost as big as the model debate. TransUnion launched First Look for mortgage so shops can review credit files before they buy scores. The idea is to avoid paying for scores on files that will never fund. TransUnion has also advertised VantageScore 4.0 near 99 cents through 2028 when used alongside FICO. Equifax's Mortgage Score Select lets a lender pull a mortgage file with a score or without one, then repull within 24 hours for about $1 plus the chosen score cost. Equifax has likewise talked up $1 VantageScore 4.0 pricing through 2028. Those tools are score-later products. They do not replace FHFA's policy call, but they show how the bureaus are racing to keep volume as dual scoring spreads.

For originators, the near-term work is operational: update overlays, train underwriters, pick a default model per loan, and make sure every borrower on a dual-score loan uses the same model. For Emily and Jake, ask your loan officer one plain question: which score model will you use on my file, and can you check both if I am close to a cutoff?
