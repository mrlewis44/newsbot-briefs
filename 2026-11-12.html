2) October existing home sales drop 5.5% to 3.75 million, the slowest pace since August 2010; West falls 9.7%

So-what: This is the first sales report that fully shows the 7%+ rate jump. Months of supply hit 5.1, so buyers have more leverage than they have had in years.

1-minute read

NAR reported Thursday that existing home sales fell 5.5% in October to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 3.75 million. That was down 8.8% from a year ago and well below the 3.87 million consensus that Calculated Risk cited.

NAR's Lawrence Yun said sales fell to their lowest level since August 2010, because mortgage rates climbed to their highest levels since 2000, aside from one month in 2023. NAR cited Freddie Mac's October average of 7.33%, up from 6.86% in September.

The median price was $416,700, up just 0.4% from a year ago. That keeps a 40-month streak of yearly gains alive, barely.

Inventory was 1.59 million, up 4.6% from a year ago. That is a 5.1-month supply, up from 4.7 in September and 4.4 a year ago.

The West fell hardest: down 9.7% for the month and 14.5% from a year ago, with a median price of $624,300, flat year over year.

First-time buyers were 29% of sales, down from 32% a year ago. Distressed sales were only 2%.

For clients: sellers have more competition and more time on market, 35 days median. Buyers can ask for credits.

Expand 3-minute read

This is the report a lot of people were waiting for, because October closings capture contracts signed after rates jumped above 7% in late September.

The headline. Sales fell 5.5% from September to a 3.75 million annual pace. They were down 8.8% from October 2025. NAR said monthly sales fell in the Midwest, South and West and held steady in the Northeast. All four regions were down from a year ago. Calculated Risk had flagged the consensus at 3.87 million and warned this would likely be a new cycle low below the 3.85 million of October 2023. It was.

Yun's take. "Home buying is difficult when mortgage rates have climbed to their highest levels since 2000, aside from a single month in 2023. As a result, home sales fell to their lowest level since August 2010," he said. He added that the 88 million homeowners are mostly in a comfortable position thanks to housing wealth, and that distressed sales are just 2% of transactions. His hopeful line: with steady job growth, there could be sizable pent-up demand if rates come down.

Prices. The median existing home price was $416,700, up 0.4% from $414,900 a year ago. Single-family median was $421,900, also up 0.4%. Condos were $365,100, up 0.3%. Price growth this slow is below income growth, which means homes are getting slightly cheaper in real terms.

Supply. Total inventory was 1.59 million, up 1.3% from September and 4.6% from a year ago. That is 5.1 months of supply, up from 4.7 months in September and 4.4 months last October. Around 6 months is often called a balanced market. We are getting closer.

Regions. The West was the weakest by far. Sales fell 9.7% for the month to a 650,000 pace and were down 14.5% from a year ago. The West median of $624,300 was flat. The South fell 6.5% to 1.73 million, with the median down 0.4% to $360,900. The Midwest fell 3.2% to 900,000. The Northeast held at 470,000, and its median rose 3.2% to $519,600.

Buyer mix. First-time buyers were 29%, down from 31% in September and 32% a year ago. Cash sales were 29%. Investors and second-home buyers were 20%, up from 16% a year ago. Median time on market was 35 days. NAR's affordability index was 100.7, down from 107.3.

What it means for your pipeline. November closings will reflect October contracts, when rates were highest, so next month's number could be weak too. On the plus side, buyers who can qualify today face less competition, more price cuts, and sellers who are more open to paying closing costs or buying down the rate. That is the pitch for buyers who are ready: negotiate the price and terms now, and plan to refinance if rates fall later.

3) Fed faces a case for bigger, faster hikes: WSJ says core prices rose at a 3.7% annualized pace over three months; Goolsbee calls October CPI 'borderline disturbing'

So-what: A December hike looks locked in. The open question is 25 or 50 basis points, and whether January follows. That keeps pressure on short rates and ARMs.

1-minute read

The Wall Street Journal reports the Fed is being forced to confront the case for bigger and faster rate increases. Core consumer prices rose at a 3.7% annualized rate over the three months through October, up from 1.6% for the three months through July.

Governor Christopher Waller said a 50 basis point hike should be considered in December if core CPI rose around 0.3%. It did. Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan said the Fed needs at least two more quarter-point hikes and may be able to move faster this winter.

Philadelphia Fed President Anna Paulson pushed back, saying she still prefers a gradual pace.

Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee, a voter next year, called the October CPI "borderline disturbing," per Scotsman Guide. He blamed tariffs, the Iran war, and AI-driven demand.

Governor Michelle Bowman estimated core PCE inflation rose to 3.3% in October from 3.1%.

Deutsche Bank's Matthew Luzzetti now expects quarter-point hikes at each of the next three meetings.

For clients: a Fed hike does not move 30-year rates one-for-one, but it keeps the 2-year high and makes ARMs and HELOCs pricier.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is where the Fed debate stands after a third straight firm inflation report.

The data. The WSJ's analysis, posted Tuesday night and printed in Thursday's paper, said core consumer prices rose at a 3.7% annualized rate over the three months through October. That is a broad speedup from 1.6% for the three months through July. The paper said the figures left little doubt the Fed will raise rates at its Dec. 8-9 meeting. The bigger questions are how fast and how far.

The hawks. Waller, speaking in London before the CPI release, said a 50 basis point hike should be considered if core CPI rose around 0.3%. It did. "Hiking only 25 basis points would put our credibility at risk, a risk I am not eager to take," he said. Logan, a voter this year, said the committee "needs to implement at least two more 25-basis-point rate increases" and that "it may be possible to move more rapidly this winter than the FOMC has moved so far this fall." She estimated policy is at least 50 basis points below the level that would start to bring inflation down.

The patient camp. Paulson said she still prefers raising rates gradually, so households and businesses can adjust. She would move faster only if inflation fed into wages or expectations. "Right now, that's not what I'm seeing," she said.

Goolsbee and Bowman. Goolsbee told PBS NewsHour the October CPI was "borderline disturbing." He said several cost drivers "were supposed to have gone away by now," pointing to tariffs and the Iran war. He also said the AI build-out could be "old-fashioned demand overheating." He did not fully endorse 50, but did not reject it. Bowman said inflation "has proven to be more persistent than I had anticipated" and estimated core PCE at 3.3% in October.

Two paths. The WSJ said faster tightening could come as a 50 basis point move in December, or as a signal that January will follow December. Luzzetti at Deutsche Bank thinks the second path is more likely. He expects a quarter point at each of the next three meetings, for a full percentage point including September's hike. The 2-year yield jumped 18 basis points Tuesday, its biggest daily rise since April 2025.

What it means for mortgages. The Fed sets short-term rates. Thirty-year mortgage rates track the 10-year Treasury and MBS spreads, which respond to inflation and growth expectations. A credible Fed that gets inflation down can actually help long rates over time. But in the near term, a hawkish Fed keeps the 2-year near 4.8%, which keeps ARM pricing and HELOC rates elevated. The ARM discount over fixed rates could shrink if short rates keep rising.

Talking point for clients: the Fed is fighting inflation, not trying to raise your mortgage rate. If inflation cools, long rates can fall even while the Fed is hiking.

5) ResiClub: inventory up 7.6% year over year at the end of October, four months of acceleration, and just 2.1% below 2019

So-what: Listings are piling up because buyers slowed, not because sellers are flooding in. That is buyer leverage, not distress.

1-minute read

Lance Lambert's ResiClub says national active inventory was 1,183,520 at the end of October, per Realtor.com. That is up 7.6% from a year ago and 2.1% below October 2019.

It is the fourth straight month of faster growth. Back in June, inventory was up just 1.9% year over year.

Lambert said long-term yields and mortgage rates near two-year highs have brought back the national softening.

Eighteen states are above 2018-2019 inventory levels, including Arizona, Colorado, Florida, Idaho, Nevada, Oregon, Texas, Utah, and Washington. Adjusted for household growth, the list is 10 states. California is not on either list.

Florida inventory is actually down about 10% year over year, after two years of being the weakest region.

Altos Research data in Calculated Risk shows the same trend weekly: up 7.2% from a year ago and 1.1% above 2019 as of Nov. 6. Bill McBride said buyers slowing, not new sellers, is driving it.

For clients: more choice and more time to negotiate. In tighter Midwest and Northeast markets, sellers still hold more power.

Expand 3-minute read

Two independent data sources this week point the same way: unsold homes are building.

ResiClub's numbers. Using Realtor.com active listings, Lambert counted 1,183,520 homes for sale at the end of October 2026. That compares with 1,100,001 in October 2025, 953,814 in 2024, and a low of 565,707 in October 2021. October 2019 was 1,208,311. So the U.S. is up 7.6% from a year ago and 2.1% below 2019. Inventory added 83,519 homes over the past year, fewer than the 146,187 added the year before.

The trend. This is four straight months of faster growth. In June the yearly gain was just 1.9%. A year ago it was 15.3%. Lambert wrote that with long-term yields and mortgage rates just under two-year highs, the national softening has regained momentum. He expects more states to post faster inventory growth than a year ago in the coming months.

Where. At the end of October, 18 states were above their 2018-2019 average inventory levels: Alabama, Arizona, Arkansas, Colorado, Florida, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Nebraska, Nevada, North Carolina, Oklahoma, Oregon, South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas, Utah and Washington. When adjusted for household growth, ResiClub's preferred measure, 10 states are above: Arizona, Colorado, Hawaii, Nebraska, Nevada, Oregon, Tennessee, Texas, Utah and Washington. Florida is a twist. After two years as the weakest region, its inventory is down about 10% year over year. Lambert said builders' affordability adjustments helped Florida meet the market.

The weekly read. Calculated Risk's Bill McBride posted Altos Research data showing single-family inventory up 7.2% from the same week last year and up 1.1% from 2019 as of Nov. 6. Altos also said homes for sale rose 8.8% from a year ago, the highest weekly growth since late January, climbing from about 4% in late August. McBride's key point: "The pickup is not being driven by a flood of new sellers. Instead, the slowdown in buyer activity is allowing unsold listings to accumulate." His conclusion: with 30-year rates around 7.3%, existing sales will be weak and prices will be under pressure. Today's NAR report backs that up, with 5.1 months of supply.

Big picture. Lambert says the Pandemic Housing Boom stretched prices against incomes. Now resale prices are growing slower than incomes, so real, inflation-adjusted prices are falling. Fundamentals are slowly improving, faster in boomtowns that already saw price drops.

New listings. Lambert also tracks new listings, the flow of homes coming to market each week. Calculated Risk's early local data showed new listings up just 1.0% year over year in October, while inventory in those markets rose 4.3%. That supports the same story: homes are sitting longer, not flooding in.

What it means. For buyers, more listings mean more choice, more price cuts, and more room to ask for seller credits. In Idaho, Washington, Oregon, Arizona and Texas, inventory is already above pre-pandemic norms. California remains tighter, which helps explain why prices there are holding better.

6) House passes a bill by voice vote that would nearly triple the VA IRRRL funding fee, from 0.5% to 1.42%

So-what: On a $500K VA streamline, the fee would go from $2,500 to $7,100. Takano says it will go nowhere in the Senate, but VA refi pipelines should know the risk.

1-minute read

Scotsman Guide reports that on Veterans Day, the House passed a bill that would raise the funding fee on VA interest rate reduction refinance loans (IRRRLs) from 0.5% to 1.42%.

The change is one of more than 60 provisions in the Take Care of America's Veterans Act from Rep. Mike Bost, R-Ill., who chairs the House Veterans' Affairs Committee. The fee hike would help pay for higher benefits for surviving spouses and severely disabled veterans.

Rep. Mark Takano, D-Calif., said Republicans used a voice vote while his staff was away negotiating technical fixes, so no recorded vote was possible. He called it a "shallow and hollow victory, because it will go nowhere in the Senate."

Industry reaction was cold. Trident Home Loans' Marty Medve said it would "essentially kill the program." Brendan McKay of the Broker Action Coalition said it funds benefits on the backs of other veterans. The MBA objected in June.

MBA data shows VA refis just rebounded over the past two weeks, and VA's share of applications rose to 12.4%.

For clients: no change yet. Veterans with a refi in process should keep moving.

Expand 3-minute read

Here is what passed, what it would cost, and how likely it is to become law.

The bill. Scotsman Guide's exclusive says the House advanced the Take Care of America's Veterans Act on Wednesday afternoon by voice vote. It was introduced in June by Rep. Mike Bost, who chairs the House Veterans' Affairs Committee. One of its more than 60 provisions, previously the Sharri Briley and Eric Edmundson Veterans Benefits Expansion Act, would raise the IRRRL funding fee from 0.5% to 1.42% of the loan amount. The money would pay for expanded benefits for surviving spouses and severely disabled veterans.

The math. On a $500,000 IRRRL, the fee goes from $2,500 to $7,100, an increase of $4,600. On a $750,000 loan, it goes from $3,750 to $10,650. Most veterans roll the fee into the loan, so the cost compounds over the life of the loan, as Medve pointed out. It also stretches the break-even on a streamline refi, which matters because VA requires a recoupment period.

The process fight. Rep. Mark Takano, the ranking Democrat, said Republicans "use[d] decoys" to pull floor staff away to negotiate technical changes, then "very quietly put forward a vote." That blocked a recorded vote, so members did not have to go on record. Takano predicted the bill "will go nowhere in the Senate." With the midterms just flipping power dynamics in Washington, that seems plausible, but it is not certain.

Industry views. Marty Medve, a former Navy carrier pilot and top VA originator at Trident Home Loans, called it "foolish and short-sighted." He said, "It's a huge deterrent to refinancing when you have to pay a point higher. To do this, you essentially kill the program." Brendan McKay, owner of McKay Mortgage and chief advocacy officer of the Broker Action Coalition, praised the goal of helping surviving spouses and disabled veterans but said "the way that they are funding those increased benefits is on the backs of active-duty service members and other veterans." The MBA wrote in June that the change would "significantly increase the cost of a VA loan relative to comparable products."

Timing. MBA's Joel Kan said Wednesday there was "a big rebound in VA refinance applications over the past two weeks," and VA's share of applications rose to 12.4% from 11.2%. So this lands just as some veterans were starting to look at streamlines again.

What to do. Nothing changes today. The fee is still 0.5%. If the Senate takes it up, it would likely come with an effective date, which could create a rush to close beforehand. Worth flagging to your veteran past clients and referral partners: if a streamline makes sense now, there is no reason to wait.

Rate context: GNMA 6.0, which prices VA loans, fell 0.26 this morning. A higher fee plus higher rates would make the break-even on a streamline much harder to reach for many veterans who refinanced in recent years.

Broker angle: this is the kind of policy fight where the Broker Action Coalition and MBA are on the same side, and where LO voices with real veteran examples get heard.

The widest gaps were in Portland, Maine, and San Jose. In San Jose, the current median payment is $2,659, but a new buyer's estimated payment in September was $7,039. In Portland, it was $1,270 versus $3,392.

September rates averaged just under 7.4%, per Freddie Mac.

Danielle Hale said even at 6% rates, typical payments would still be more than 60% higher than the current median.

Insurance alone made up almost 10% of the typical payment at the end of Q2, per ICE.

This report puts a dollar figure on lock-in, and California is near the top of the list.

The core number. Realtor.com chief economist Danielle Hale said, "For the typical mortgage holder, buying a home at today's median price and current rates would mean finding about $1,100 more each month for principal and interest, before property taxes and insurance." Scotsman Guide calls that roughly an 85% payment gap nationwide.

Why so wide. ICE Mortgage Technology data shows about half of outstanding first-lien mortgages had a rate below 4% at the end of September. Since 2020, home prices have risen sharply too, so buyers need bigger loans at higher rates. Realtor.com said the gap has widened through 2026 as rates climbed.

The extremes. Portland, Maine, and San Jose had the widest gaps in September, when Freddie Mac's 30-year average was just under 7.4%. In Portland, the estimated new median principal and interest payment was $3,392, compared with a current median of $1,270. In San Jose, the current median payment of $2,659 jumped to $7,039 for a new buyer. Realtor.com senior economist Hannah Jones said, "More affordable markets offer homeowners a smaller payment jump, not an escape from higher costs."

Even at 6%. Hale noted that if rates were at 6%, typical payments would still be more than 60% above the current median. That is a reminder that lower rates help, but they do not erase lock-in. Cotality research from September found 2023 borrowers paid about $911 more per month than 2020-2021 borrowers.

Other costs. Insurance, taxes, utilities and maintenance have all risen. ICE said home insurance was almost 10% of the typical monthly payment at the end of the second quarter.

California angle. San Jose is the starkest example, but the same math applies across coastal California, where prices are high and many owners hold sub-4% loans. In San Jose the gap works out to about $4,380 a month ($7,039 minus $2,659), roughly four times the national figure. For a California owner thinking about moving up, that number is the conversation.

Connect the dots with today's other data. NAR's October sales hit a 3.75 million pace, the lowest since 2010. HousingWire ran a column citing Compass economist Jonah Coste, who estimates lock-in is preventing about 870,000 sales in 2026, with existing sales near 4.2 million versus a 5 million norm. But Coste also found about 5.8% of locked-in homes get unlocked every year anyway, from death, divorce, new kids and kids leaving home.

How to use it. For move-up clients, start with the payment, not the price. Show the gap honestly, then show the tools: a 2-1 buydown paid by the seller, an ARM, or keeping the current home as a rental. (Scotsman Guide notes ARMs have priced up to 100 basis points under fixed rates since mid-September.) For first-time buyers, the gap does not apply, and they face less competition when owners stay put.

This column is a useful counterweight to doom headlines, and it lines up with today's NAR data.

The argument. The author writes that the 2026 foreclosure crisis "has failed to materialize." Headlines showing big year-over-year percentage gains in foreclosure filings started from very low levels. Using the New York Fed's Q3 Household Debt and Credit Report, he says foreclosure credit stress still has not returned to pre-COVID levels. He notes that 1% to 4% of mortgages are always delinquent, and the warning sign is when that breaks above historical norms. It has not.

The FHA moratorium myth. Many expected the end of the FHA foreclosure moratorium in October 2025 to flood the market with distressed listings. He says that is not how the process works. It takes 9 to 18 months, at minimum, from the start of foreclosure to a home hitting the market. And foreclosure data has not shown material credit stress building since 2014.

History. Since World War II there have been 13 recessions with average unemployment of 7.8%, but only one major foreclosure crisis: the run-up to 2008. That crisis took about four years of credit buildup. During 2009 to 2011, distressed sales pushed weekly new listings to 250,000 to 400,000 for years. HousingWire's weekly new listing data shows nothing like that today.

The equity cushion. In 2010, over 23% of homes were underwater, and the loan-to-value ratio hit nearly 86% in 2011. Today the figure is 44.2%. FICO scores have stayed strong, most owners have 30-year fixed loans, and underwriting is far tighter. He credits the 2005 bankruptcy reform and the Dodd-Frank qualified mortgage rules.

Today's confirmation. NAR's October release says distressed sales were 2% of transactions, unchanged from September and from October 2025. Lawrence Yun said the 88 million homeowners are "largely in a comfortable financial position due to sizable housing wealth accumulation." The New York Fed also reported credit card balances at a record $1.29 trillion in Q3, per Dow Jones, but said signs of financial stress are not widespread.

What it means. The market is slow because of rates and affordability, not because owners are being forced out. That is why inventory is rising gradually, not collapsing into a fire sale. Prices can soften, especially in the West and South, without a crash.

A caution. Rising inventory and slow sales can still mean local price declines, especially in markets that ran up hardest. A price dip is not the same as a foreclosure wave. Owners with fixed rates and big equity can wait, cut price modestly, or rent the home out instead of being forced to sell.

How to use it. When a client says they are waiting for a crash, the honest answer is: the data does not show one coming. What they can get is a better negotiating position now, with more listings and longer days on market.

This is a practical column, and the numbers behind it are worth knowing.

The research. Davis cites Compass economist Jonah Coste, who calculates that lock-in is preventing about 870,000 home sales in 2026 that would otherwise happen. Existing home sales are running near 4.2 million versus a long-run norm of 5 million. Today's NAR October pace was even lower, at 3.75 million. The hopeful part: about 5.8% of locked-in homes get unlocked each year through what economists call normal demographic and economic activity.

The four D's. Davis frames it as death, divorce, diapers and diplomas. His metaphor is a tide chart versus the weather. Rates are the weather. Life events are the tide, and they show up on schedule.

Death. Every estate with real property ends in a sale or transfer, usually handled by someone doing it for the first time. The list is public probate filings. The better list is the estate and elder law attorneys, trust administrators, liquidation companies and move managers. What to offer: a free date-of-death valuation for tax basis. Do not contact a family off an obituary.

Divorce. The house rarely fits either half of the split household. Family law attorneys, mediators and divorce financial planners know about the house about six months before the MLS does. Bring neutrality in writing: a one-page explainer both parties can read. For an LO, this is also where buyouts, refinances to remove a spouse, and new purchases for each party come from.

Diapers. A growing family outgrows a two-bedroom, whatever the rate. Start with past clients who bought small homes or condos three to five years ago. Davis warns to advertise the home and service, never who ought to live there, and to have a broker review audience targeting under Fair Housing rules.

Diplomas. When the last child leaves, parents often downsize the next spring. Graduating senior names are printed locally each June. Match surnames to homes bought 18 to 25 years ago in assessor records. Host a no-pitch empty-nest talk with a financial planner.

Why it is credible. A 5.8% yearly unlock rate compounds. Over three years, roughly one in six locked-in homes would turn over even if rates never fell. That steady flow is a floor under transaction volume, and it is why sales at 3.75 million are low but not zero.

How BuyWise can use it. These same life events create mortgage demand: purchase loans for the next home, cash-out or buyout refis in divorce, reverse mortgages for downsizers, and non-QM for heirs or self-employed buyers. Estate attorneys, divorce attorneys and financial planners are strong referral relationships for an LO. It is also strong content for Buy Right, Borrow Smart: the market is slow, but people still move when life changes.
