2) Freddie Mac's 30-year falls to 7.24%, lowest this month; 15-year at 6.60%

So-what: Two straight weekly drops give you a clean talking point for buyers, but rates are still about 1 point above a year ago.

1-minute read

Freddie Mac's weekly survey moved lower again.

The 30-year fixed averaged 7.24% as of October 29, down from 7.32% the week before. Freddie called it the lowest level this month. The 15-year fixed averaged 6.60%, down from 6.68%.

For context, a year ago the 30-year was 6.17% and the 15-year was 5.41%. So rates have improved for two weeks, but they are still more than a full point higher than this time last year.

Freddie said the decline is "modestly boosting purchasing power for prospective buyers."

Keep in mind what this survey measures. Freddie averages rates from loan applications submitted Thursday through Wednesday. That means this week's number mostly reflects last week's market, before Thursday's tech-driven bond rally. Mortgage News Daily's daily index, which moves faster, was 7.29% Thursday.

So what: this is a headline buyers will see on the news. It is a good opening to call people who stalled out in September, when rates spiked toward 7.5%. Show them the payment at today's rate versus a month ago, rather than promising more drops.

Expand 3-minute read

Freddie Mac's Primary Mortgage Market Survey, the rate number most consumers hear about on the news, fell for a second straight week.

The numbers. The 30-year fixed averaged 7.24% as of October 29, 2026. That is down from 7.32% the week before. Freddie said it is the lowest level of the month. The 15-year fixed averaged 6.60%, down from 6.68%.

The year-ago comparison. A year ago, the 30-year averaged 6.17% and the 15-year averaged 5.41%. So even after two weeks of improvement, the 30-year is still about 1.07 points higher than last year. That gap matters when you talk with buyers who remember friends or family getting rates in the low 6s.

What Freddie said. Freddie's summary: "The average 30-year fixed-rate mortgage declined again this week, reaching its lowest level this month and modestly boosting purchasing power for prospective buyers."

How the survey works. This is worth knowing so you can explain why the numbers do not always match what borrowers see. Freddie builds the survey from loan applications submitted through its Loan Product Advisor system by credit unions, banks and mortgage companies. The average covers applications from the prior Thursday through Wednesday. It is released Thursdays at noon Eastern. That means this week's 7.24% mostly captures the market before Thursday's bond rally. It does not capture the latest pricing.

Comparing with daily data. Mortgage News Daily's index, which updates every weekday around 1 PM PT, had the 30-year at 7.29% on Thursday, down 0.06. MND's 52-week range runs from 5.99% to 7.61%, which shows how far rates climbed during the September spike. MND generally runs a bit higher than Freddie's number because the methods differ.

Why rates came down. The 10-year Treasury, the main benchmark for mortgage rates, spent most of September climbing from 4.75% to 5.3%, according to MBA data cited by Scotsman Guide. It has since come back to roughly 4.93% to 4.94%. Mortgage rates followed it down.

So what for originators. This is a good week to reach out to buyers who went quiet in September. Many of them watched rates jump toward 7.5% and decided to wait. Run a quick payment comparison. On a $500,000 loan, each quarter point is real money each month, and showing a borrower that number is more useful than talking about where rates might go. Avoid predicting further drops. The honest message is: rates have improved for two weeks, here is your payment today, and here is what we can do if you want to protect it.

For your content. If you post about rates this weekend, lead with the 7.24% figure and the "lowest this month" framing, then add the year-ago comparison so it stays honest. Remind viewers that your team can help buyers in every state you serve, not just California.

Expand 3-minute read

Friday brought one key data report and one important Fed speech. Together, they show a Fed that still leans toward tightening but is not in a hurry.

The Employment Cost Index. The ECI is a quarterly Labor Department report that measures what employers spend on wages and benefits. The Fed treats it as the most complete measure of wage pressure. In the third quarter it rose 0.8%, exactly as expected and slightly slower than the 0.9% second quarter. Wages and salaries rose 0.7%, down from 0.9%. Benefits rose 0.9%, down from 1.0%.

On a yearly basis, total compensation costs for civilian workers rose 3.4%. Wages were up 3.1% and benefits up 3.9%. The key detail, from investingLive's summary: after adjusting for inflation, private-sector wages and salaries fell 0.6% over the year. Paychecks are not keeping up with prices. That squares with the WSJ opinion headline this week asking if wages are keeping pace, and answering no.

Why that matters for rates. Wage costs are one of the main ways inflation can get stuck. A 0.8% quarter is not alarming, so this report did not push bond yields higher. MBS were nearly unchanged after it.

Barkin's comments. Richmond Fed President Barkin spoke Friday morning. His key lines, as reported by investingLive:

"Direction of travel is 'straightforward' for the Fed, the question is pace."

"A gradual approach is a good way to go right now."

He said businesses are seeing broad cost pressures and are planning to pass them on to customers. He said after years of high inflation, it is easier for companies to argue for price increases. He also said the AI investment surge has strained supply chains and is creating demand-side inflation, "the kind of thing the Fed can lean against." Consumer spending has been surprisingly strong, he said, and demand is solid "with only weakness in nonprofits and in the housing sector."

investingLive read the remarks as having a hawkish bias, but with a preference for moving slowly. He did not give a size or timing for the next move.

Thursday's inflation backdrop. Scotsman Guide's headline was "Fresh inflation data vindicates Fed hawks." The PCE price index jumped 0.5% in September and was 3.6% higher than a year earlier, up from 3.4%. Core PCE held at 3.1%. MBA's Mike Fratantoni noted that oil prices pushed up the headline number, but core inflation is still well above the Fed's 2% target.

The counterweight. Peter Boockvar wrote Friday that December rate hike odds have slipped to 74% from 84%, measured before and after Fed Chair Kevin Warsh spoke Wednesday. He argued, for the third time this week, that if tech stocks keep faltering, the Fed will not hike in December, because the economy and stock market lean so heavily on tech. Warsh himself said Wednesday that policymakers are not on a set course.

So what for your borrowers. The Fed is still leaning toward higher short-term rates, which mostly affects HELOCs and ARMs. Long-term mortgage rates follow the 10-year, which right now is reacting more to the stock market than to Fed talk. For clients weighing a HELOC versus a fixed second, this is a reason to show both and stress-test the variable payment.

The Mortgage Bankers Association's monthly affordability report shows how much September's rate spike hurt buyers, according to Scotsman Guide's coverage.

The headline number. The national median payment on purchase applications in September was $2,215. That is $53 more than in August and $148 more than a year earlier. The 2.5% month-over-month rise reversed three straight months of easing payments.

MBA's view. Edward Seiler, MBA's vice president of housing economics, said: "Compared to a year ago, mortgage payments are up more than 7%, outpacing earnings growth and adding to the affordability challenges facing prospective homebuyers." He added that "despite a slight decline in the median loan amount, higher mortgage rates drove monthly payments upward, with affordability deteriorating in 39 states."

The index. MBA's Purchase Applications Payment Index, or PAPI, tracks how new payments change relative to income. It was about 3.1% higher than a year earlier in September. A higher index means weaker affordability.

By loan type. Conventional applicants, with loans underwritten to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac guidelines, had a median payment of $2,244. That is 6.6% above a year earlier and 2.5% above August. FHA payments were $1,906, up 6.3% from a year earlier and 2.7% from August. New construction payments rose more than 4% to $2,307.

Where it is easiest. Louisiana, Connecticut, West Virginia, Alaska and Iowa had the lowest state PAPIs, meaning the best relative affordability. California was not on that list.

What caused it. MBA data showed average 30-year rates holding around 6.8% through August, then rising to 7.5% by the end of September. That tracked the 10-year Treasury, which opened September at 4.75% and closed the month at 5.3%. Inflation pressure drove the bond selloff. Purchasing power and mortgage demand fell together.

What has changed since. Scotsman noted that Treasury yields have come down from their peaks, with the 10-year spending the past week just under 5%. This morning it was about 4.94%. Freddie Mac's survey is down to 7.24%. So some of the September payment shock has already reversed.

Policy angle. Scotsman also noted the executive order President Trump signed last week targeting mortgage costs, which industry groups welcomed ahead of what is expected to be a busy legislative calendar in 2027.

So what for originators. This report gives you a concrete number for content and client conversations. A buyer who looked in late September saw the worst payments in months. Today, rates are meaningfully lower. A simple side-by-side, the payment at 7.5% versus the payment at today's rate on the same loan, can bring a stalled buyer back.

For FHA borrowers, the $1,906 median is a useful benchmark when you explain why FHA can still pencil for first-time buyers. And for anyone stretching on DTI, remember that payments rose faster than pay this year. Build in some cushion and talk about taxes and insurance, not just principal and interest.

For the show audience, this is a good data point: payments rose, then rates eased. Our team can run those numbers for buyers in every state we serve.

Two Calculated Risk posts give a quick health check on housing.

Bill McBride's Calculated Risk published two useful posts this week on credit and prices.

Delinquencies. Freddie Mac reported its single-family serious delinquency rate was 0.61% in September, up from 0.60% in July and 0.57% in September 2025. That is close to the pre-pandemic level of 0.60%. For perspective, Freddie's rate peaked at 4.20% in February 2010 after the housing bubble and at 3.17% in August 2020 during the pandemic.

Fannie Mae reported its serious delinquency rate was 0.62% in September, up from 0.60% in August and 0.54% a year ago. That remains below the pre-pandemic low of 0.65%. Fannie's rate peaked at 5.59% in February 2010 and 3.32% in August 2020.

Serious delinquency means three or more monthly payments past due or in foreclosure. Loans in forbearance count as delinquent in this report, though they are not reported to credit bureaus.

McBride's summary: "In general, single family delinquency rates are low but slowly increasing. Multi-family delinquency rates are high." This lines up with Freddie Mac's own earnings release, which put its rate at 0.61%, and with ICE data earlier this week showing the national delinquency rate at 3.66% in September.

Real prices. In a second post, McBride looked at prices adjusted for inflation. In nominal terms, the Case-Shiller national index and the 20-city index are both at all-time highs. But using CPI to adjust for inflation, the national index is 3.9% below its 2022 peak, and the 20-city index is 3.6% below. Both real indexes dipped slightly in August. It has now been 51 months since real prices peaked.

Even so, real national prices are about 8.3% above the 2006 bubble peak. McBride points out there is a natural upward slope to real prices over time, but they are still historically high. The price-to-rent ratio is 11.0% below its 2022 peak.

His outlook: with inventory picking up and higher mortgage rates reducing demand, nominal prices look mostly flat this year, and real prices look set to decline further. He has written before that after a sharp run-up, it often takes years for real prices to reach new highs.

What it means. Put these together and you get a market that is cooling slowly. Credit quality is still strong by historical standards, but drifting the wrong way. Prices are not falling on paper, but they are losing ground to inflation, which is a quiet form of improving affordability.

So what for originators. For buyers, the message is calm and factual. Prices have stalled while wages keep rising, so homes are slowly becoming a little more affordable in real terms, even before any change in rates. Avoid both doom and hype. For your existing clients, slowly rising delinquencies are a reminder to check in with borrowers who bought at high rates and tight budgets. Early outreach about a payment problem is far better than a surprise later. And for your show, the nominal versus real price comparison is a simple, honest chart that explains why buyers feel stuck even though prices are flat.
