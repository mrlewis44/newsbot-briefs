2) Freddie Mac: 30-year fixed hits 7.40%, the seventh straight weekly rise and the highest since November 2023

Wed show

So-what: Payments on a $500,000 loan are about $367 a month higher than a year ago, but rising listings and price cuts are giving prepared buyers more room to negotiate.

1-minute read

Freddie Mac's weekly survey had the 30-year fixed at 7.40% as of October 8. That is up from 7.28% last week and 6.30% a year ago. It is the highest reading since November 2023 and the seventh weekly increase in a row. The 15-year fixed rose to 6.73% from 6.60%.

Since August 20, when the 30-year averaged 6.65%, rates have climbed 75 basis points in seven weeks.

Here is what that means in dollars. On a $500,000 loan, principal and interest at 7.40% runs about $3,462 a month. At last year's 6.30%, it was about $3,095. That is roughly $367 more every month.

The other side of the story is leverage. The Wall Street Journal says the highest rates in three years are pushing sellers in some metros to cut prices. Realtor.com's Joel Berner says sellers are cutting prices at a pace not seen in four years. National prices are down 1.4% from a year ago and inventory is up 5.4%.

For buyers who are ready, that means more choices and more room to ask for credits or a rate buydown. Just make sure the payment still works at today's rate.

Expand 3-minute read

Freddie Mac released its Primary Mortgage Market Survey on Thursday at 9:00 AM PT. The 30-year fixed averaged 7.40% for the week ending October 8, up 12 basis points from 7.28%. A year ago it was 6.30%. The 15-year fixed averaged 6.73%, up from 6.60% last week and 5.53% a year ago.

Put that in context. The last time the 30-year was this high was November 16, 2023, when it was 7.44%. Freddie's data shows the rate has now risen for seven straight weeks. On August 20 it was 6.65%. Since then it has gone 6.66%, 6.71%, 6.76%, 6.95%, 7.03%, 7.28%, and now 7.40%. That is 75 basis points in seven weeks, and the rate only crossed back above 7% two weeks ago.

One timing note. PMMS averages rates from loan applications submitted to Freddie from the prior Thursday through Wednesday. That window included the 10-year's new long-term high near 5.36% on Wednesday morning. Thursday's bond rally came after the survey closed, so next week's number has a chance to look a bit better if the 10-year holds near 5.25%.

What drove it. Realtor.com senior economist Joel Berner said the 10-year Treasury averaged 5.28% this week, 9 basis points higher than the week before. He described "a wicked brew of inflation expectations, a broad bond market selloff, and rising fiscal deficits requiring new debt issuance." That lines up with the MBS Live list of forces pushing rates up.

The payment math. On a $500,000 loan, principal and interest is about $3,462 a month at 7.40%. At 7.28% last week it was about $3,421. At 6.65% in August it was about $3,210. At 6.30% a year ago it was about $3,095. A buyer who was preapproved in August at the same price has lost about $250 a month in room, before taxes and insurance.

The buyer leverage angle. The Wall Street Journal's Nicholas Miller wrote Thursday that the highest rates in nearly three years are lousy news for a stuck market, but they are also forcing some sellers to cut prices. Berner said pending home sales fell from a year earlier in both August and September, even before rates crossed 7%, and sellers are cutting prices at a rate not seen in four years. He said buyers who can pay cash are seeing very favorable conditions, with prices down 1.4% from a year ago and inventory up 5.4%.

How to use this with clients like Emily and Jake. The rate is worse, but the negotiation is better. In many markets buyers can now ask for seller credits, repairs, or a paid rate buydown. A seller credit used for a buydown often helps the payment more than a price cut of the same size. A temporary buydown can also soften the first year or two while leaving room to refinance if rates fall later.

Two cautions. Do not promise that rates will fall soon. And rerun preapprovals at today's rate so buyers do not fall in love with a house the payment cannot support. This is also a strong candidate for next Wednesday's show: 7.4% rates, and why that can be a better time to negotiate.

Peter Boockvar published two notes in the Boock Report on Thursday, October 8, and one on Friday morning. This summary uses the public portions of those posts plus Treasury's official auction results.

The 30-year auction. Treasury sold $22 billion of 30-year bonds on Thursday. The issue was a reopening of an existing bond, so its official term was 29 years and 10 months. The high yield was 5.618%. In the September 10 auction of the same size, the yield was 5.308%. So investors demanded about 31 basis points more to lend to the government for 30 years than they did a month ago.

Demand details. The bid-to-cover ratio was 2.54, compared with 2.61 in September. Indirect bidders, a group that includes foreign central banks and large fund managers, took about 72% of the bonds, down from about 79% in September. Direct bidders took about 21%. Primary dealers, the big banks that must bid at every auction and absorb what others do not buy, took about 6.8%, up from 2.2%. A higher dealer share usually means outside demand was a bit softer.

How the market read it. MBS Live's Matt Graham called the auction "ho-hum" but said it would have looked strong if not for the rally leading into it. In other words, yields had already dropped during the morning, so buyers got a lower yield than they might have liked. Bonds still finished Thursday at their best levels in four days.

The FT story. Boockvar said a much bigger story, potentially, was a Financial Times report that OpenAI's annualized revenue was about $20 billion less than previously signaled. According to the FT, the company recently told investors the figure was approaching $50 billion in September, far less than the $70 billion that had been widely reported. AI stocks responded by trading lower.

Boockvar's big picture point on Friday. Whatever the details of how OpenAI calculates revenue, he said the key point is that a massive chunk of the AI data center buildout relies on two companies, OpenAI and Anthropic. A large piece of the future cloud backlogs at Microsoft, Oracle, Amazon, and Google is tied to those two firms. Whether all that spending pays off depends on whether they become profitable.

Other notes from Friday. Container shipping rates eased. The Shanghai to New York rate fell 2% week over week but stayed above $10,000. Shanghai to Los Angeles fell 2.7%. Why this matters for mortgage rates. MBS Live lists resilient stocks as one of the forces keeping rates high, because stocks compete with bonds for investor money. If the AI trade cools in an orderly way, some money could rotate into bonds and help yields drift lower. A sharp stock selloff would likely do the same, but for worse reasons.

The auction itself is the caution. Long-term buyers are still there, but they want more than 5.6% to commit for 30 years. That keeps a floor under mortgage rates for now.

4) CBO: the federal deficit hit $2.0 trillion in fiscal 2026, $218 billion more than last year, with interest costs up $115 billion

So-what: More deficit means more Treasury bonds to sell, and that steady supply is one of the main reasons long-term rates, and mortgage rates, stay high.

1-minute read

The Congressional Budget Office estimates the federal deficit for fiscal 2026, which ended September 30, at $2.0 trillion. That is $218 billion more than the $1.8 trillion in fiscal 2025. It is also about $140 billion more than CBO projected in its latest baseline. The Wall Street Journal ran it on page one Friday.

The math: spending rose $386 billion, or 6%, to about $7.4 trillion. Revenue rose $169 billion, or 3%, to about $5.4 trillion. Interest on the national debt alone rose about $115 billion.

The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget called it among the highest deficits in U.S. history and the highest ever outside a war or recession. Its president said it is "no surprise that interest rates have climbed so much over the past few months."

Why this matters for mortgages: the government covers the gap by selling more Treasury bonds. More supply means investors can demand higher yields, and mortgage rates follow the 10-year. MBS Live lists heavy Treasury issuance as one of the ongoing forces keeping rates high.

This is not a reason rates must rise. It is a reason they have trouble falling quickly.

Expand 3-minute read

The Congressional Budget Office released its Monthly Budget Review for September on Thursday, October 8. It covers the full fiscal year 2026, which ran from October 1, 2025 through September 30, 2026. The Wall Street Journal led its Friday front page with the headline that the deficit jumped to nearly $2 trillion.

The headline numbers. CBO estimates the deficit at $2.0 trillion. That is $218 billion larger than fiscal 2025's $1.8 trillion, and about $140 billion more than CBO itself projected in its latest baseline. The government spent about $7.4 trillion and collected about $5.4 trillion.

Where the increase came from. Spending rose $386 billion, or 6%. Revenue rose $169 billion, or 3%, which offset part of it. According to the American Action Forum's breakdown of the CBO report, about $271 billion of the spending increase came from mandatory and discretionary programs, and about $115 billion came from higher interest payments on the national debt. Social Security spending rose $86 billion, or 5%, because of larger average benefits and more people enrolled. Medicare rose $77 billion, or 8%. Medicaid rose $55 billion, or 8%.

A timing wrinkle. Through August, the year-to-date deficit looked roughly flat with last year. That was because some payments due on September 1, 2025 fell on Labor Day and were moved into August 2025. CBO says that without those shifts, the deficit was already running $82 billion higher through August. September alone showed a deficit of about $28 billion, compared with a surplus last September that was inflated by the shifted payments.

The reaction. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget said the $2 trillion deficit ranks among the highest in U.S. history and is the highest ever outside a war or recession. CRFB president Maya MacGuineas said it is "no surprise that interest rates have climbed so much over the past few months." CRFB suggested a goal of cutting deficits to 3% of the economy, which it says is about half of today's level.

Why this matters for mortgage rates. When the government spends more than it collects, the Treasury borrows the difference by selling bonds. More bonds on the market means buyers can demand a higher yield. The 10-year Treasury yield is the main benchmark for 30-year mortgage rates. MBS Live keeps a list of forces pushing rates higher, and elevated Treasury issuance tied to fiscal imbalances is on it, along with war-driven inflation and strong economic data. Higher rates also make the problem worse, because interest costs on the debt keep growing. That is the $115 billion jump.

How to talk about it with clients. Keep it simple and avoid politics. The government is borrowing a lot of money, so it has to pay more to attract lenders. That raises the base cost of borrowing for everyone, including homebuyers. It does not mean rates will keep rising. Rates can still fall if inflation cools or the economy slows. But big deficits are a headwind that makes a fast drop in mortgage rates less likely.

What to watch: Treasury's own monthly statement for September is due October 13, and CBO plans more fiscal 2026 detail next month.

5) ResiClub: national active listings up 5.6% from a year ago in September, speeding up from 1.9% two months earlier

So-what: Rising rates have restarted the inventory build, which gives buyers more leverage, especially in Tampa, Denver, and Seattle, while Chicago and Hartford sellers still hold the edge.

1-minute read

Lance Lambert's monthly ResiClub inventory report, sent Thursday, says national active listings were up 5.6% from September 2025 to September 2026.

That is faster than the 1.9% growth two months earlier. Lambert ties the pickup to the recent jump in long-term yields and mortgage rates, which has renewed the market's softening.

For perspective, inventory growth a year ago was much hotter, at 16.9%. And the country is now just 5.2% below 2019 inventory levels, the last normal pre-pandemic year.

Where buyers have the most leverage: the South and Mountain West, especially former pandemic boomtowns. Among big markets, Lambert names Tampa, Denver, and Seattle. Where sellers still have the edge: much of the Midwest and Northeast, including Hartford and Chicago.

Florida is the exception to the trend. Many Florida markets now show slightly lower inventory than a year ago, as their post-boom correction loses steam.

Lambert also counts 17 of the 50 largest metros with more active listings per household than the 2018 to 2019 average. That is up from 15 a year ago.

So-what for buyers: more homes on the market means more choices and more room to negotiate, but it varies a lot by city.

Expand 3-minute read

Lance Lambert's ResiClub PRO monthly inventory report landed Thursday afternoon, October 8. It covers active listings data through the end of September 2026, drawn from Realtor.com, across more than 800 metros, 3,000 counties, and 25,000 ZIP codes.

The national number. Active listings rose 5.6% from September 30, 2025 to September 30, 2026. Two months earlier, the year-over-year growth rate was just 1.9%. So after slowing for most of the past year, inventory growth has picked back up over the last three months. Lambert links that directly to the recent surge in long-term yields and mortgage rates, which has renewed the market's softening and shifted more power to buyers in many places.

The longer view. A year ago, inventory growth was running at 16.9%, so today's pace is still far calmer than the 2025 surge. Nationally, active inventory is now just 5.2% below September 2019 levels. ResiClub describes the nationally aggregated market as soft, with a lot of nuance by region and price tier.

Where buyers are gaining leverage. Many of the softest markets are in the South and Mountain West. Those were the top pandemic boomtowns, where prices ran past local incomes. Once remote work migration slowed and mortgage rates spiked, places like Punta Gorda, Florida and Austin, Texas had to rely on local incomes. Heavy new construction in the Sun Belt added pressure. Builders often cut prices or offer incentives such as rate buydowns, which pulls buyers away from resale homes and cools that market too. Among major metros, Lambert names Tampa, Denver, and Seattle as markets where buyers have gained more power.

Where sellers still have the edge. Much of the Northeast and Midwest was less tied to pandemic migration and has less new construction. Inventory there remains relatively tight. Lambert names Hartford and Chicago as major markets where sellers have kept more leverage.

The Florida exception. Over the past year, Florida's post-boom correction has lost some momentum. Many Florida markets now show active inventory slightly down from a year ago.

A better yardstick. Comparing to 2019 gets less useful over time because some cities have grown. So ResiClub now also measures active listings per household and compares that to the 2018 to 2019 average. In September, 17 of the 50 largest metro areas had more inventory per household than that pre-pandemic average, up from 15 a year earlier. Lambert says the population adjustment changed the overall picture very little.

Why inventory matters for prices. ResiClub's research shows markets where inventory has climbed back to 2019 levels have generally had weaker price growth, or outright declines, over the past four years. Markets still well below 2019 levels have held up better.

What this means for buyers like Emily and Jake. In much of Southern California, inventory is still on the tight side by historical standards, so expect fewer bargains than in Tampa or Austin. But the national direction is clear: more listings and longer market times. That gives prepared buyers room to ask for credits and repairs. Pair this with the 7.40% Freddie Mac rate to show why negotiating power and payment planning go together right now.

7) MBA: mortgage credit availability slips 0.2% in September as lenders tighten documentation, cash-out, and investor loans

So-what: Agency lenders are trimming the edges of their menus, which pushes more self-employed, cash-out, and investor borrowers toward non-QM and DSCR programs.

1-minute read

The Mortgage Bankers Association's Mortgage Credit Availability Index fell 0.2% in September to 107.1. A lower reading means lending standards are tightening. The index was set to 100 in March 2012.

The tightening was on the conventional side. The Conventional index fell 0.4%. The Government index, which covers FHA, VA, and USDA, was unchanged. Within conventional, the Jumbo index fell 0.3%, its second monthly drop in a row. The Conforming index fell 0.2%.

MBA's Joel Kan said lenders tightened documentation requirements on conventional loans. They also cut back on loans that allow cash-out refinances and investor home purchases.

One bright spot: Kan said recent growth in non-agency loan programs continued to support the jumbo part of the market.

Why this matters for brokers: when conventional lenders pull back on cash-out and investor loans, those borrowers do not disappear. They move to non-QM, bank statement, and DSCR lenders, where pricing is higher but approvals are more flexible.

The change is small. But it fits a pattern of lenders getting more careful as rates rise and volume shrinks. Expect more conditions on files with complex income or investment properties.

Expand 3-minute read

The Mortgage Bankers Association released its Mortgage Credit Availability Index for September on Thursday, October 8. The index uses underwriting and eligibility data from more than 95 lenders and investors, provided by ICE Mortgage Technology. It blends factors like credit scores, loan types, and loan-to-value ratios into one number. A falling index means credit is getting harder to get. A rising one means it is getting easier. The baseline is 100 in March 2012.

The headline. The overall index fell 0.2% to 107.1. For context, that is still above the 2012 baseline, but far below the loose credit of the mid-2000s, when the index ran several times higher. The story is not a credit crunch. It is a quiet trimming of the menu.

Where the tightening happened. The Conventional MCAI fell 0.4%. The Government MCAI, covering FHA, VA, and USDA loans, was unchanged. Inside the conventional bucket, the Jumbo MCAI fell 0.3%, its second straight monthly decline, and the Conforming MCAI fell 0.2%.

What lenders changed. Joel Kan, MBA's vice president and deputy chief economist, said lenders tightened documentation requirements on conventional loans. They also reduced offerings of loans that allow cash-out refinances and investor home purchases. He said the tightening also affected jumbo programs, but that recent growth in non-agency loan programs continued to support the jumbo segment.

The demand backdrop. MBA's weekly survey, released Wednesday, showed applications down 4.2% in the week ending October 2. Refinance applications fell 8% and purchase applications fell 2%. The adjustable-rate share held at 10.3%. When volume drops, lenders often protect themselves by cutting riskier products first. Cash-out refis and investor loans are usually at the top of that list.

Why this matters for a broker shop. Three groups feel it most. First, self-employed borrowers with complex returns. Tighter documentation on conventional loans means more requests for extra statements and explanations. Second, homeowners who want to pull cash out to pay off higher-rate debt. Fewer conventional programs offer that, and the ones that do may price it higher. Third, investors buying rentals. Agency investor loans already carry extra pricing, and fewer lenders are offering them.

All three groups are natural fits for non-QM. Bank statement loans can qualify self-employed borrowers on deposits instead of tax returns. DSCR loans qualify investors on the property's rent instead of personal income. Non-QM cash-out can work for borrowers who do not fit agency boxes. Optimal Blue data earlier this week showed non-QM above 11% of locks, so that channel is already growing.

A practical step. Check overlays with your top conventional investors this month. Some changes in documentation and cash-out rules do not get loud announcements. Find out before a file gets suspended in underwriting.

What not to read into it. One month of a 0.2% move is small. It is a direction, not a dramatic shift, and government lending has not tightened at all. FHA remains open for first-time buyers like Emily and Jake who have modest down payments and decent credit.

8) ICE data: non-permanent residents' share of FHA purchase loans fell from 5.8% to about 0.1% after HUD's May 2025 rule change

So-what: Borrowers on work visas and similar status now need conventional or non-QM financing, which means bigger down payments and better credit for many of them.

1-minute read
