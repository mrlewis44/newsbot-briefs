1) MBS Live: Tuesday's bounce was a trap; UMBS 6.0 gives back 0.26 and the 10-year tests 5.32%

Wed show

So-what: Tuesday's small rally is already gone and the 10-year sits just under the 5.34% level MBS Live treats as a lock trigger, so floating into today's auction and Fed minutes is a real risk.

1-minute read

Tuesday was a rare good day for bonds. Wednesday took it back before most of the West Coast was awake.

Matt Graham's MBS Morning, sent at 7:07 AM PT, had UMBS 6.0 at 97.80, down 0.26. The 10-year Treasury was at 5.317%, up 3.3 basis points. The premarket 10-year alert at 4:30 AM PT read 5.338%.

Tuesday's MBS Recap closed UMBS 6.0 at 98.06, up 0.21, with the 10-year at 5.285%. That rally tracked a drop in oil prices. Graham called it "nice," but said it was not enough to signal a real change in direction.

Today's headline is blunt: "No Surprise: It Was a Trap." For two months, every isolated rally day has been followed by a return to higher rates. Graham says there is no single cause, and he pokes fun at anyone blaming "auction concerns" or France.

What it means for locks: MBS Live lists 5.34% and then 5.41% as ceiling levels that can act as lock triggers. We are a hair under the first one. Two events hit today: the 10-year Treasury auction at 10:00 AM PT and the FOMC minutes at 11:00 AM PT.

Expand 3-minute read

Start with the numbers. At 7:07 AM PT, UMBS 6.0 was 97.80, down 0.26. UMBS 6.5 was 100.51, down only 0.06. GNMA 6.0 was 98.02, down 0.28. The 15-year UMBS 5.0 was 96.88, down 0.08. On the Treasury side, the 10-year was 5.317%, up 3.3 basis points, and the 30-year was 5.698%, up 4.0. The 2-year actually fell 1.3 basis points to 4.785%.

That split matters. Short-term yields follow the Fed. Long-term yields follow inflation worries, supply, and investor appetite. Today the selling is in the long end, which is exactly where mortgage rates take their cue. So this is not a "Fed hike is coming" move. It is a "nobody wants long bonds" move, and that is harder to talk your way out of.

Tuesday's close was the opposite picture. The MBS Recap had UMBS 6.0 at 98.06, up 0.21, and the 10-year at 5.285%, down 2.3 basis points. Near the best levels of the day, MBS were up more than a quarter point and the 10-year was down 3.8 basis points at 5.269%. Graham said the move traced a drop in oil prices fairly clearly. He also noted yields ran into resistance at 5.26%, while buyers seemed to show up whenever yields crested 5.3%.

His lock and float note from Tuesday is the key line: modest rally days like that "should be viewed as the exception until we can confirm a reversal." Today proved the point within one overnight session.

Graham's list of what keeps pushing rates higher, in no particular order: the war's implied impact on inflation and Treasury issuance; heavy Treasury issuance from fiscal imbalances; heavy corporate bond issuance competing for the same investors; resilient stocks; gradually lower foreign demand, partly tied to tariffs and weaker trade ties; genuine strength in some economic data; and a Fed willing to use the fed funds rate to fight inflation.

He does not want anyone pinning today on one story. He lists oil, Europe, auctions, war headlines, corporate issuance, fiscal worries, a strong economy, and foreign demand, and says it is not any one of them. His joke target today is "Treasuries are worried about France." He says all four combinations of higher or lower French yields being good or bad for U.S. yields have been argued in the past week. The WSJ print edition does lead with France's "debt bomb," and a separate story says the French government is willing to bypass parliament to pass billions in cuts. Treat that as background noise, not a trading signal.

Technical levels from MBS Live: ceiling levels at 5.34% and 5.41%, which can be used as lock triggers. Floor levels at 5.29%, 5.14%, 5.00%, 4.93%, and 4.83%. We opened today right between 5.29% and 5.34%.

Today's calendar: MBA applications came out at 4:00 AM PT. The 10-year note auction is at 10:00 AM PT. FOMC minutes are at 11:00 AM PT. The WSJ also ran a piece on Fed communication. Per the digest summary only, Kevin Warsh wants markets to focus on the economy rather than Fed talk, and last week his top lieutenants stepped in to reset bets on an October rate increase. The minutes could move that debate either way.

Bottom line for clients: if a borrower is inside 15 days and happy with the payment, the trend still favors locking. A quarter-point drop in MBS before 7 AM is the kind of move that can trigger negative reprices if it holds or worsens during the day.

2) MBA: applications fall 4.2% as the 30-year contract rate jumps to 7.49%, highest in almost three years

Wed show

So-what: Refis are close to shut off at 7.5%, and FHA purchase apps fell the most, which says payment shock is hitting first-time buyers first.

1-minute read

The MBA's weekly survey for the week ending Oct. 2 shows demand cracking under higher rates.

Total applications fell 4.2% from the prior week, seasonally adjusted. The refinance index dropped 8% and is 56% below the same week a year ago. The seasonally adjusted purchase index fell 2%, and unadjusted purchase apps are 15% lower than a year ago.

The average 30-year fixed contract rate on conforming loans ($832,750 or less) rose to 7.49% from 7.30%. Jumbo rose to 7.39% from 7.27%. FHA rose to 7.14% from 6.97%. The 15-year rose to 6.71% from 6.56%. The one bright spot: the 5/1 ARM fell to 6.43% from 6.47%.

Joel Kan said rates hit their highest level in almost three years as Treasury yields rose and spreads widened with volatility. Refi apps were at their lowest level since 2025. FHA purchase apps fell 6%, the most of any loan type. The ARM share held at 10.3%.

Plain math: on a $500,000 loan, the move from 7.30% to 7.49% adds about $65 a month in principal and interest. That is one week of rate movement.

Expand 3-minute read

This is the first primary data on how borrowers reacted to the late-September bond selloff, and it lines up with what brokers are seeing on the phones.

The headline numbers, all for the week ending Oct. 2: the composite index fell 4.2% seasonally adjusted and 4% unadjusted. The refinance index fell 8% week over week and is 56% below the same week in 2025. The seasonally adjusted purchase index fell 2%. The unadjusted purchase index also fell 2% and is 15% lower than a year ago.

Rates by product, per the MBA survey: 30-year conforming: 7.49%, up from 7.30%. 30-year jumbo (above $832,750): 7.39%, up from 7.27%. 30-year FHA: 7.14%, up from 6.97%. 15-year fixed: 6.71%, up from 6.56%. 5/1 ARM: 6.43%, down from 6.47%.

Two things jump out. First, jumbo is still pricing 10 basis points below conforming in this survey. That matters in Orange County, where many purchases sit above the conforming line. Second, the 5/1 ARM moved the other way. The gap between the 30-year conforming rate and the 5/1 ARM is now 106 basis points.

Kan's quote explains why: "Mortgage rates moved to their highest level in almost three years last week, with the 30-year fixed rate reaching 7.49% as both Treasury rates increased and spreads widened with the increase in rate volatility." Spreads widening means mortgage rates rose even more than Treasuries. When bond investors get nervous about volatility, they demand extra yield on mortgage bonds because borrowers can refinance away when rates fall.

On refis, Kan said "very few homeowners have an incentive to refinance at these rates." With rates roughly a full point above a year ago, refi apps were at their lowest since 2025 and less than half of last year's pace. The refi share of all applications slipped to 37.0% from 38.3%.

On purchase, the decline hit every loan type, but FHA purchase apps fell the most, down 6%. FHA's share of total applications slipped to 16.4% from 16.7%. VA's share slipped to 11.8% from 11.9%. USDA held at 0.5%. FHA is where first-time buyers with smaller down payments live, so that 6% drop is the clearest sign of affordability stress in this report.

The ARM share held at 10.3%. Kan noted a higher share of borrowers have been choosing ARMs to lower the starting payment. On a $500,000 loan, the 30-year at 7.49% runs about $3,493 a month in principal and interest. A 5/1 ARM at 6.43% runs about $3,137. That is roughly $356 a month less for the first five years, with the risk that the rate adjusts after that.

Credit pulls tell the same story. The Xactus Mortgage Intent Index fell about 5.7% week over week to 102.7. Xactus's Thomas Lloyd said that is roughly 21% below the same week last year, and with fourth-quarter seasonal slowing starting, "a meaningful near-term rebound in mortgage intent is unlikely."

For the show: this is a good week to explain why the rate quote a buyer saw two weeks ago is gone, how much a 0.19-point jump costs per month, and when an ARM or a buydown is worth a look. Freddie's PMMS lands Thursday; last week it was 7.28%.

This HousingWire roundup ties together three threads: where daily rates are, what that is doing to lenders, and what it does to buyers.

Rates first. HousingWire's Mortgage Rates Center showed the 30-year conforming loan averaging 7.63% on Tuesday, up 31 basis points over two weeks. The 30-year FHA averaged 7.59%, up a startling 59 basis points in two weeks. The 30-year jumbo averaged 7.85%, up 45 basis points. These daily index numbers run higher than the weekly MBA and Freddie surveys because they capture this week's selloff in real time. MBA's Bob Broeksmit said last week that rates had risen for the sixth straight week to a nearly three-year high.

Lenders next. Cohn's read is that some banks are "prophylactically raising rates, even if they don't have to, just to get out of the fray." In plain terms, some lenders are pricing defensively so they do not get stuck holding loans if bonds keep falling. That widens the gap between the best and worst quotes on any given day, which helps brokers who shop.

The cuts HousingWire lists are real and recent. Benchmark Mortgage closed its wholesale and correspondent division and will focus on retail. New American Funding cut 160 jobs in its consumer-direct channel. Pennymac cut some lending and fulfillment roles and closed an office in Tennessee. Cohn expects more: "We're going back to a period like in 2022, when rates went up precipitously, and we saw some lenders going out of the market." For a broker, that means checking the health of wholesale partners and watching for lenders that quietly tighten or slow turn times.

Hector Amendola of SimplyPMG said the conversation has moved from "When do we get back to 5%?" to 8%, and that he heard 9% floated this week. His advice does not change: "Nobody knows where rates are headed going into next year, and anyone who tells you they do is selling something."

Mat Ishbia used his monthly "3 Points" video to push two affordability levers. One is VantageScore 4.0 on loans sold to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, which he says can mean lower LLPAs and cheaper mortgage insurance. The other is FHFA aligning Fannie and Freddie rules on mortgage insurance cancellation. He was candid that the MI change will not be massive. He also pointed to home equity: with more than half of consumers rolling over a credit card balance each month, he sees demand for cash-out refis and HELOCs to combine payments.

Buyers last. First American's Real House Price Index showed affordability in July was 3.5% better than a year earlier, when rates averaged 6.5%. Now that rates are above 7%, that has trimmed about $19,000 from the typical borrower's purchasing power. Chief economist Mark Fleming put it simply: "The affordability tug-of-war has shifted." Slower price growth and rising incomes were winning. Now higher rates are pulling the other way.

The Neighbors Bank survey of roughly 1,000 adults in September is a useful show topic. Sixty percent said they would rather own half a home today than keep renting until they could afford one alone. That rose to nearly 70% when the co-owner was family. People earning under $50,000 were twice as likely to consider buying with a close friend as those earning $100,000 or more. Co-buying works, but only with clear title, a written agreement, and an exit plan.

4) UWM drops its minimum credit score overlay on agency loans, a day after going dual-score FICO plus VantageScore

Wed show

So-what: At UWM, an agency file with an AUS approval no longer dies on a score floor, and every pull now prices off the better of FICO or VantageScore 4.0.

1-minute read

UWM made two credit moves in two days.

On Tuesday, it said it will pull both a FICO score and a VantageScore 4.0 on every credit report. Its system will automatically use whichever qualifying score gives the borrower the best eligible pricing. Brokers no longer have to pick a model.

On Wednesday morning, UWM went further. It removed its minimum credit score overlay on agency loans, effective immediately. That covers Fannie and Freddie conventional loans plus FHA, VA, and USDA. Eligibility will now be set by the automated underwriting decision, not a hard score cutoff.

Scores still drive pricing, including agency LLPAs. And minimum scores stay in place for UWM's jumbo, Bank Statement, and Investor Flex products.

Mat Ishbia said UWM spent a year studying scores, approvals, and loan performance. His conclusion: "A credit score is definitely part of the story, but it doesn't tell the whole story."

This follows Fannie Mae's November 2025 Selling Guide, which dropped minimum scores for DU loans. It also follows Rocket making VantageScore 4.0 its default. For brokers, it means some files that were dead on score alone may now be worth running.

Expand 3-minute read

These are two separate announcements, and together they change how credit works on UWM agency files.

Move one, Tuesday: dual scoring. UWM will now obtain both a FICO score and a VantageScore 4.0 on every credit pull. Its system compares them behind the scenes and applies the higher qualifying score that meets program requirements. UWM says the goal is the lowest payment, cheaper mortgage insurance, and lower all-in fees. Its early internal research says using all available scores "could save many borrowers thousands" over the life of the loan. HousingWire did not report a specific average dollar figure from UWM.

Move two, Wednesday: no minimum score overlay on agency loans. An overlay is a lender rule stricter than the agency's own guidelines. UWM is dropping its credit score overlay, effective immediately, for conventional loans backed by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac and for FHA, VA, and USDA loans backed by Ginnie Mae. Instead of a score floor, UWM will rely on the automated underwriting system decision to decide eligibility.

Three caveats. First, scores still matter for pricing, including agency loan-level price adjustments. A lower score will still cost more. Second, the change is agency only. UWM confirmed minimum scores remain for its jumbo, Bank Statement, and Investor Flex products. Third, AUS approvals are not automatic for thin or damaged credit. The file still has to earn the approve on its full profile.

Ishbia's reasoning: after a deep dive into scores, approvals, and performance, UWM found scores are an important indicator but "not the strongest predictor" of whether a borrower qualifies. He added that AUS gives "a much more complete assessment by evaluating numerous factors across a borrower's financial profile, which is why Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac and Ginnie Mae rely on AUS to assess creditworthiness."

The backdrop is a fast-moving credit score market. In early September, FHFA said all Fannie and Freddie approved lenders could use either Classic FICO or VantageScore 4.0. At the end of September, FHFA Director Bill Pulte said the GSEs would use a single LLPA pricing matrix for loans scored with either model. FHFA has also pointed to future acceptance of FICO 10T and an eventual move to two-bureau credit reports. And Fannie Mae's November 2025 Selling Guide removed minimum score requirements for loans run through Desktop Underwriter, letting DU's own risk analysis decide eligibility.

Competitors are moving too. Rocket said last week VantageScore 4.0 will be its default model for eligible conforming and VA loans starting in the fourth quarter of 2026. In a four-month test using 1.4 million credit reports scored both ways, Rocket found some additional borrowers qualified, and those who saved money using VantageScore saved an average of $1,600 at closing. Pennymac rolled out VantageScore 4.0 across all channels earlier this week.

What to do with this: pull the files you declined or parked over the last six months for score alone. If the borrower had stable income, reserves, and reasonable debt ratios, they may now get an AUS approve and a UWM path. Also re-check pricing on active files, since a better VantageScore could improve LLPAs or MI. For the show, the simple message is that a single three-digit number is no longer the hard gate it was, but it still sets your price.

5) ARM resets crest in 2027 at about 186,000 loans; 2020 seven-year ARMs face a median jump of $1,066 a month

Wed show

So-what: ARMs are back above 11% of locks, so buyers taking one now should see what 2027's reset borrowers are facing before they sign.

1-minute read

New ICE and Optimal Blue data map the coming wave of adjustable-rate resets.

There are now 3.1 million active first-lien ARMs, the most in five and a half years. That is still only 5.6% of all active mortgages. About a third have started adjusting.

ICE counts 84,000 resets through July 2026 and 64,000 more by year end, for 148,000 this year. Resets rise to about 186,020 in 2027, then ease to 154,710 in 2028.

The pain is concentrated. About 74,000 seven-year ARMs from 2020 face the biggest hit: a median payment increase of about $1,066 a month, or 36%. Another 65,000 five-year ARMs from 2022 face a median increase of $430, or 22%.

ICE does not see a systemic risk. Today's ARM borrowers generally had to qualify at the fully indexed rate, not a teaser rate.

ARMs now make up more than 11% of rate locks, the largest share in nearly four years. MBA's survey this morning had the ARM share at 10.3% of applications. For buyers using an ARM to make today's payment work, the 2027 reset group is the cautionary tale.

Expand 3-minute read

ARMs are back because fixed rates are high. That makes this data worth walking through slowly.

The stock of ARMs: ICE counts 3.1 million active first-lien ARMs, the highest in five and a half years. They are still just 5.6% of all active mortgages. Andy Walden of ICE said only about a third of active ARMs have begun adjusting because most newer ones are still in their fixed intro period. More than 90% of ARMs originated since 2022 are still in that intro period.

The reset calendar: 84,000 ARMs reset through July 2026. Another 64,000 are scheduled by the end of the year, bringing 2026 to 148,000. ICE projects 186,020 resets in 2027 and 154,710 in 2028. Those counts will shrink as borrowers refinance, sell, or pay off before their first adjustment.

Why 2027: ARM usage hit about 1% of rate locks in December 2020, when fixed rates were near record lows, according to Optimal Blue, whose data covers about 40% of the market across roughly 900 lenders. As rates rose in 2022, ARMs came back, led by 5/1 and 7/1 products, and peaked at 12.6% of locks in October 2022. Optimal Blue data shows average ARM rates of 3.84% at the end of March 2022 and 4.28% at the end of April 2022. Brennan O'Connell of Optimal Blue said the first batch from that spring is about six months from resetting, and many 5/1 ARMs in 2027 "could easily be 200 basis points higher than what they're currently paying."

Who gets hit hardest: about 74,000 seven-year ARMs originated in 2020. ICE expects the largest median payment increase there, about $1,066 a month or 36%. That reflects very low starting rates, larger balances, and higher periodic caps. Another 65,000 five-year ARMs originated in 2022 face a median increase of $430, or 22%.

Why it is not 2008: post-crisis rules generally require borrowers to qualify at the fully indexed rate, not just the teaser. So most of these households were tested against a higher payment from the start. ICE and Optimal Blue expect 2027 reset counts to stay limited in the context of the broader market, even if individual borrowers see real payment changes.

The Fed angle: ICE estimated that the Fed's most recent hike, assuming a full 25-basis-point pass-through to ARM indexes, raises the median affected borrower's payment by about $14 a month. Newer ARMs, which tend to have bigger balances, would see a median increase of about $53.

Today's ARM demand: ICE says ARMs recently topped 11% of rate locks, their largest share in nearly four years. ICE's conforming 30-year fixed index hit 7.2% on Sept. 24. This morning's MBA survey had the ARM share of applications at 10.3%, with the 5/1 ARM at 6.43% against 7.49% for the 30-year fixed.

What to tell buyers: an ARM can be a smart bridge if you have a real plan, such as expected income growth, a likely move, or a refinance if rates fall. It is a bad bet if the only plan is hoping. Show the fully indexed payment and the worst-case payment at each cap, not just the start rate. For refinance outreach, look for clients with 2020 seven-year ARMs and 2022 five-year ARMs in your past book. They are the ones facing 2027 resets.

6) Non-QM should be the first conversation, not the rescue: Optimal Blue puts it above 11% of August locks

So-what: Self-employed and investor files that start in agency and fail at week four cost time and trust; a better discovery call routes them right on day one.

1-minute read

Marc Halpern, CEO and founder of Foundation Mortgage, argues in a HousingWire column that most "non-QM fallout" is really a broker workflow problem.

The pattern: a broker starts a self-employed borrower on a conventional loan, pulls two years of tax returns, and watches the underwriter cut income for legitimate deductions. DTI fails. Conditions pile up. By week four or five, the file stalls and the broker calls a non-QM lender to save it.

Halpern's fix is a better first conversation. Ask how the borrower earns, what documents exist, what the property is, and the goal. Then pick bank statements, P&L, 1099, DSCR, asset qualifier, or agency from the start.

He cites Optimal Blue: non-QM was more than 11% of total lock volume in August, up 3 percentage points from a year earlier. Investor and DSCR loans were more than 35% of non-QM production, and bank statement loans nearly 30%. He adds that recent non-QM securitizations show average FICOs in the mid-700s and LTVs below 60%.

His line: "This is not a subprime 2.0 story." Non-QM is a regulatory classification, not a shortcut around underwriting.

Expand 3-minute read

This is an opinion column, but it is written by a wholesale non-QM lender and it lines up with BuyWise's self-employed focus.

The problem Halpern describes: a self-employed borrower is started on a conventional loan by default. The broker requests two years of tax returns. The underwriter recalculates qualifying income downward for legitimate business write-offs. Debt-to-income fails on the lower figure, new conditions get added, and the file stalls by week four or five. Only then does the broker call a non-QM lender. By that point, Halpern says, "the borrower has lost weeks, the closing timeline is at risk and the file is being restructured under pressure."

His reframe: what the industry calls non-QM fallout "is really something else. It is a broker workflow that started with the wrong documentation method from day one."

Who these borrowers are: business owners using real deductions, 1099 professionals with steady deposits but no W-2, real estate investors whose properties should carry the debt, retirees drawing from portfolios, foreign nationals without U.S. income history, and buyers of non-warrantable condos or small multifamily. He notes California and Florida drive most of his company's volume, which tracks with where entrepreneurs and international buyers concentrate.

The product set is far deeper than a decade ago: bank statement loans, P&L programs, 1099-only, DSCR investor loans, asset qualifiers, foreign national loans, and closed-end seconds. He also notes jumbo loans are now largely classified as non-QM because they are not agency.

None of it skips underwriting. A bank statement loan needs two years of verified deposits and a documented cash flow analysis. DSCR needs the property's income to support the debt. Asset qualifiers need documented liquid reserves measured against the loan amount and term. Foreign national loans need verified international income and big down payments. Every file goes through an ability-to-repay analysis and investor review.

The market data he cites: Optimal Blue's latest report has non-QM at more than 11% of total lock volume in August, up 3 percentage points year over year. Investor and DSCR loans made up more than 35% of non-QM production. Bank statement loans made up nearly 30%. Recent non-QM securitizations, he says, show pools with average FICO scores in the mid-700s and LTVs below 60%, and institutional investors are buying at market pricing.

Why brokers keep starting in the wrong lane: training and habit. Agency guidelines are familiar. Non-QM still carries a "lesser product" label inherited from before 2008.

His practical fix is a different opening question. Before quoting a rate or requesting a document, learn how the borrower earns income, what documentation is actually available, what the property is, and what they want to accomplish. A self-employed borrower can describe how deposits come in, so you can go straight to bank statements. An investor can tell you which units have leases, so you know if DSCR fits.

For BuyWise and the @joshlewisCMC relaunch, this is ready-made content: why your tax return is not your income story, how bank statement and DSCR loans are underwritten, and why starting in the right lane saves weeks. One caution worth adding for viewers: non-QM rates and fees usually run higher than agency, so the trade is real and should be priced side by side.

His group then won a consent solicitation that removed five directors: Daniel Lewis, Arnaud Massenet, Bhaskar Menon, Prabhu Narasimhan, and Harit Talwar. The math: operating expenses are about $65 million a quarter, with a target near $50 million. Quarterly revenue is in the $50 million range, with a target above $65 million. Garg said getting halfway on either side gets Better to break-even.
