3) Realtor.com: 65.6% of mortgages are still below 5% and 19.2% below 3%, but the 6%-plus share is 22.5% and could reach 28.5% by mid-2027

Wed show

So-what: Lock-in is easing slowly, which means more move-up sellers each year, but most owners still have a big reason to stay put.

1-minute read

Realtor.com's latest look at outstanding mortgages shows the rate lock-in effect is still strong. HousingWire covered the analysis Friday.

As of the second quarter of 2026, 19.2% of outstanding mortgages carried a rate below 3%. Another 29.9% were between 3% and 4%, and 16.5% were between 4% and 5%. Add those up and 65.6% of borrowers have a rate under 5%.

On the other end, 12% sit between 5% and 6%, and 22.5% are at 6% or higher.

Loans made from July 2019 through June 2022, the low-rate era, still make up 42.8% of all outstanding mortgages, up from 40.8%. Loans made since July 2022 are 27.8%.

If rates stay above 6%, Realtor.com projects the 6%-plus share could reach 27% to 28.5% by mid-2027, the highest in more than a decade.

Realtor.com's Hannah Jones put it simply: "A mortgage rate below 3% was an extraordinary exception, not a new normal."

So what: each year more owners hold today's rates, which slowly frees up move-up sellers. But two-thirds still sit under 5%, so inventory will loosen gradually, not suddenly.

Expand 3-minute read

The lock-in effect is the main reason so few existing homes have come up for sale over the past few years. Owners with very low mortgage rates do not want to trade them for a new loan at today's rates. A new Realtor.com analysis, covered by HousingWire on Friday, measures how much of that grip is left.

Here is the rate breakdown for outstanding mortgages as of the second quarter of 2026. 19.2% carry a rate below 3%. 29.9% are between 3% and 4%. 16.5% are between 4% and 5%. Together, 65.6% of borrowers have a rate under 5%. Another 12% are between 5% and 6%. The remaining 22.5% are at 6% or higher.

One number stands out. Loans originated from July 2019 through June 2022, the period of record-low rates, now make up 42.8% of outstanding mortgages. That is up from 40.8%. You might expect that share to shrink as people move and pay off loans. Instead it grew. One likely reading is the newer, higher-rate loans are being paid off or refinanced faster, while low-rate owners hang on. Loans made since July 2022 account for 27.8% of the total.

Realtor.com also projected what happens next. If rates stay above 6%, the share of mortgages at 6% or higher could climb to between 27% and 28.5% by mid-2027. That would be the highest share in more than a decade. The projection assumes about 3.1 million new mortgages a year.

Hannah Jones of Realtor.com summed up the big picture: "A mortgage rate below 3% was an extraordinary exception, not a new normal."

Why does this matter for buyers and for us? There are three takeaways.

First, inventory will improve slowly. Every year, life events like new jobs, growing families, divorce and retirement force some low-rate owners to sell anyway. As the 6%-plus group grows, more owners will not lose anything by moving. That is a gradual release, not a flood.

Second, move-up buyers have a real decision to make. A client with a 3% loan who wants a bigger home is weighing a much higher payment. The conversation should focus on total monthly cost, how long they plan to stay, and whether options like keeping the current home as a rental make sense.

Third, the 22.5% of owners at 6% or higher are a refinance pool to watch. Wednesday's Fed meeting and this week's data could move rates either way. If rates fall meaningfully, those borrowers are the first who benefit.

For first-time buyers, the lesson is that waiting for a flood of listings may be a long wait. Buying when the payment works, with a plan to refinance if rates drop, is often more realistic than timing the market.

This also connects to Pulte's comments today about missing first-time buyers. Tight resale supply keeps pushing buyers toward new construction, where builders are paying heavy incentives.

6) The signed housing order is narrower than the draft: mortgage insurance removal by AVM becomes a Fannie and Freddie pilot, and KBW calls it 'fairly benign'

Wed show

So-what: The MI cancellation help is a pilot, not a blanket rule, so do not promise clients automatic PMI removal yet.

1-minute read

Friday's brief covered the signing of the housing cost executive order. HousingWire's follow-up adds the part that matters for client conversations: the final order is narrower than the draft that leaked earlier.

The order directs Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac to pilot mortgage insurance cancellation based on an automated valuation model, or AVM, once equity and other requirements are met. An AVM is a computer estimate of a home's value, so a borrower might not need a full appraisal to drop MI.

The draft version would have applied to all GSE loans. HousingWire noted that about 40% of borrowers with a mark-to-market loan-to-value under 80% might have qualified under that broader approach. The signed version is a pilot instead.

The order also launches or expands GSE pilots for title insurance alternatives and for lower-cost limited cash-out refinances.

KBW called the order "more positive than some expectations, especially for mortgage insurers" and "fairly benign for both mortgage and title insurers."

So what: this is a step, not a switch. Tell clients PMI relief may get easier, but the details and eligibility are still to come.

Expand 3-minute read

Thursday's executive order on housing costs got a lot of attention when it was signed, and Friday's brief covered the basic list. HousingWire's Flávia Furlan Nunes followed up Friday afternoon with reaction from housing leaders. Most of the article sits behind HousingWire's member wall, so this summary covers only the visible portion.

The key point is that the final order is narrower than the earlier draft. That changes how we should talk about it with clients.

Start with mortgage insurance. The order directs Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac to pilot mortgage insurance cancellation using an automated valuation model, once equity and other requirements are met. An AVM is a computer-generated estimate of a home's value based on sales data. Today, a borrower who wants to drop private mortgage insurance early often needs an appraisal to prove the loan balance has fallen below a set share of the home's value. Using an AVM could make that process faster and cheaper for the borrowers who qualify.

The draft would have gone further. It would have applied to all GSE loans. HousingWire said roughly 40% of borrowers with a mark-to-market loan-to-value below 80% might have qualified under that version. Mark-to-market means the loan balance compared with what the home is worth today, not what it was worth at purchase. In a market where many owners have gained equity over the past several years, that would have been a big pool. The signed order makes it a pilot instead, so the size and timing are not yet clear.

The order also launches or expands GSE pilots in two other areas. One is title insurance alternatives. The other is lower-cost limited cash-out refinances. A limited cash-out refi is the type most homeowners use to change their rate or term without pulling out much equity. Cheaper versions could help when rates eventually fall, but again, these are pilots.

How did Wall Street read it? KBW said the order was "more positive than some expectations, especially for mortgage insurers." The firm also called it "fairly benign for both mortgage and title insurers." In plain English, the industries that might have lost revenue under the draft came out better than feared.

What does this mean for our business? First, avoid overselling. A client who heard "the President is getting rid of PMI" may expect automatic removal. That is not what the order does. The honest answer is that Fannie and Freddie will test easier MI removal for some borrowers, and we will watch for the rules.

Second, it gives us a useful reason to reach out. Past clients who bought with less than 20% down and have gained equity may already be able to request MI removal under current rules. A quick review could save them real money each month, pilot or not.

Third, the refinance and title pilots are worth tracking for the next rate drop. Lower fees on a limited cash-out refi make the math work at a smaller rate improvement.

Credit report costs have been one of the most annoying line items in mortgage lending for the past few years. On Friday, FICO said it will not raise its mortgage score price for lenders in its Preferred Program.

Here are the facts from FICO's press release, issued October 23 at 4:15 PM ET, which is 1:15 PM PT. FICO will hold its wholesale mortgage price at $10 through its tri-merge resellers. The price applies to lenders and brokers enrolled in the Preferred Program. It is flat year over year.

FICO calls it the most predictive score available to the mortgage industry. It uses trended credit bureau data and rental payment information.

Who wins? Lenders and brokers who enroll avoid another price hike for the coming year. That helps keep closing costs from creeping up. In a market where builders are paying tens of thousands in incentives to close deals, every cost we can control matters.

What should a broker do? First, ask your credit vendor if it is a participating reseller. Second, find out what the Preferred Program commitment actually requires before signing anything.
