2) September starts drop 5.2% to 1.221 million vs. 1.3 million expected, but single-family is up 2.6% from a year ago while apartments fall 24.9%

Wed show

So-what: The miss is mostly an apartment story. Single-family building is still a bit ahead of last year, but a 4.0% drop in single-family permits points to a thinner new-home pipeline next year.

1-minute read

Builders started fewer homes in September than economists expected, but the details are less grim than the headline.

Total housing starts ran at a 1.221 million annual pace, down 5.2% from August's revised 1.288 million and down 7.4% from a year ago, per Calculated Risk's write-up of the Census report. Economists surveyed by The Wall Street Journal expected 1.3 million.

Most of the drop came from apartments. Multifamily starts were down 24.9% from a year ago. Single-family starts, the kind most of our buyers want, fell 4.6% for the month to 859,000 but are still up 2.6% from September 2025.

Bill McBride also pointed out that starts for the prior two months were revised up, which softens the miss.

The warning sign is permits, the step before a shovel goes in the ground. Total permits were steady at 1.400 million, just above the 1.39 million forecast. Single-family permits fell 4.0% to 845,000.

Homes under construction held at 1.280 million, which is 434,000 below the October 2022 record. For buyers, today's builder deals are real. A big wave of new supply later is not likely.

Expand 3-minute read

The government's monthly construction report came out this morning. The headline is a miss, but Calculated Risk's breakdown shows where the weakness really is.

Start with the totals. Housing starts, meaning homes where construction actually began, ran at a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 1,221,000 in September. That is down 5.2% from August's revised 1,288,000 and down 7.4% from 1,319,000 a year ago. The Wall Street Journal's survey of economists expected 1.3 million, which would have been a 2% gain. So builders came in well short.

Now the detail that matters most for our buyers. Single-family starts were 859,000, down 4.6% from 900,000 in August. But they are up 2.6% from a year ago. Buildings with five or more units started at a 349,000 pace, and multifamily starts overall were down 24.9% from a year ago. In plain terms, the yearly decline is mostly an apartment pullback, not a collapse in house building.

Bill McBride added two helpful notes. First, "Total housing starts in September were well below expectations, however starts for the previous two months were revised up." Upward revisions mean the summer was stronger than first reported, so the two-month picture is better than the headline. Second, starts for the year to date are down only 0.5% from last year on an unadjusted basis.

Permits are the forward-looking piece, since a permit comes before construction. Total permits were 1,400,000, down 0.2% from a revised 1,403,000 and down 3.0% from 1,444,000 a year ago. The WSJ survey expected 1.39 million, so total permits beat slightly. The catch is single-family. Single-family permits fell 4.0% to 845,000 from 880,000. Five-plus unit permits were 503,000. When single-family permits fall, it usually means fewer house starts a few months from now.

How much weight should one month get? Not too much. The Census starts figure carries a wide margin of error, plus or minus 11.8% on the monthly change, per Truflation's Truman newsletter. That is why trends over several months matter more than one print.

The pipeline view is useful too. Homes under construction were 587,000 single-family and 693,000 multifamily, both unchanged from August. The total of 1.280 million is 434,000 below the record 1.714 million set in October 2022. Fewer homes in the pipeline means fewer finished homes coming to market over the next year.

This fits Monday's builder survey. NAHB chief economist Robert Dietz expects single-family construction could decline again in 2027, since he sees mortgage rates above 7% through the end of next year, per Scotsman Guide.

For the show, here is the simple version. Builders are still building houses at about last year's pace, and they are cutting prices to move them. But falling single-family permits suggest they are getting more cautious. Buyers waiting for a flood of new supply to push prices down should not count on it. Buyers who want new construction should shop builder incentives now.

Expand 3-minute read

Monday's builder survey was the first housing report of the week, and it was rough for builders. For buyers, it is a sign of leverage.

The NAHB/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index fell 3 points to 29 in October, from 32 in September. HousingWire calls it "the lowest reading since June 2012." Economists expected 31, per the Boock Report. The index runs from 0 to 100, and 50 is the breakeven line between more builders seeing good conditions and more seeing bad ones. Per Scotsman Guide, it has not been above 50 since April 2024.

Every part of the index fell. Traffic of prospective buyers, meaning how many people are walking through model homes, dropped 5 points to 18. Dietz described it as the "lowest level since April 2012, excluding the pandemic's onset." Expected sales over the next six months fell to 32, and current sales fell to 33.

NAHB was direct about why: "Mortgage rates have climbed well above 7% in recent weeks, and higher gas prices and inflation have further weakened buyer traffic across much of the country." That ties the builder slump to the same oil and inflation story that is moving bond yields.

The buyer angle is in the pricing data. About 43% of builders cut prices in October, the highest share in the 2020 to 2026 period, up from 38% in September and 35% in August. The average cut was 6% for the seventh straight month. About 65% used sales incentives, the 19th straight month at 60% or higher. HousingWire adds that the median new-home price is at a "nearly five-year low" and that discounting is "now a core part of the sales playbook."

Margins are where builders feel it. HousingWire says how well a builder holds up depends on its land costs, how many homes it builds before having a buyer, and lender rate buydowns. Buydowns are expensive for builders, but for a buyer they can lower the monthly payment more than a similar price cut.

Costs are rising on several fronts. More than one-third of builders reported immigration enforcement activity in their markets, per the Boock Report, which tightens labor. Scotsman Guide also lists high energy costs, tariffs and competition for workers from data center projects. Dietz expects single-family construction could decline again in 2027 with mortgage rates above 7% through the end of next year.

Peter Boockvar summed it up: "the housing recession is getting even more difficult with the rise in mortgage rates. Unfortunately I see time as the only cure."

What it means in practice. Buyers looking at new construction have more negotiating room than they have had in years. Ask builders directly about price reductions, closing-cost help and rate buydowns, and compare the after-incentive payment with resale homes. A builder's preferred lender offer is worth checking against an outside quote. This morning's starts report also showed single-family permits down 4.0%, so fewer homes being started now could mean less choice later.

This is a clean Wednesday show topic: one number, 29, and one action step, which is to shop builder incentives with a lender who can compare the true monthly cost.

Housing data got the attention this morning, but oil may matter more for where mortgage rates go next.

Start with the numbers. Peter Boockvar's Boock Report this morning said November WTI crude futures hit a new cycle high, "up $5 today to $109." He also noted that the May 2027 WTI contract traded above $90 for the first time. The two figures came at different times this morning, and both point the same direction.

Why the 2027 contract matters. A spike in the front month can be a short-term scare that fades. When oil for delivery next May also trades above $90, it means traders expect high prices to last. That is the kind of move that shows up in inflation forecasts, and bond investors want higher yields when they expect more inflation.

Here is the rate channel in plain terms. Oil feeds into gasoline, shipping and many other costs. Higher costs show up in inflation reports. Hotter inflation makes the Fed less likely to cut and bond buyers less willing to accept low yields. Mortgage rates follow the bond market, so they stay higher.

MBS Live spells this out in its standing list. Among the forces keeping rates high, it lists the war and inflation, along with heavy Treasury and corporate bond issuance. Among the things that would help rates fall the most, it lists the war ending and oil falling, along with a recession and inflation returning to 2%. Right now, oil is moving the wrong way on that list.

There are signs of the conflict spreading into business plans. The Wall Street Journal's front page today says Saudi Arabia postponed its big investment conference until 2027 after Houthi attacks on the kingdom's airports. We have seen only the headline, so treat it as context.

Higher prices are showing up in other materials too. Cleveland-Cliffs' CEO said, per the Boock Report, "we are currently selling steel under the best price environment in three years." Steel is a building cost, so that is one more pressure on builders already squeezed on margins.

It is already hurting housing demand. In Monday's builder survey, NAHB said: "Mortgage rates have climbed well above 7% in recent weeks, and higher gas prices and inflation have further weakened buyer traffic across much of the country." Buyer traffic fell to 18, the weakest reading since 2012 outside the pandemic. Gas prices hit buyers twice: once in their monthly budget and again through higher mortgage rates.

What to tell clients. Oil is one of the main reasons rates have not come down despite weak housing data. Nobody can predict when the conflict ends or when oil turns. Until it does, borrowers should plan around today's rate range and not count on a quick drop. For anyone closing soon, that supports MBS Live's defensive lock stance. For buyers still shopping, the better lever right now is builder incentives and price negotiation, not waiting on the oil market.

This is a strong show topic because it connects something everyone sees at the gas pump to the rate on their loan.

Two of the biggest hidden costs in a conventional loan could be on the table before the year is out.

FHFA Director Bill Pulte said in posts on X on Saturday that the agency is studying "the usage of just one or two credit reports" to bring "even more savings" to consumers, per HousingWire. Today most loans use all three bureaus, called a tri-merge. He also said he expects to decide "whether there will be any changes to LLPAs before year end."

LLPAs are loan-level price adjustments. They are the add-on costs Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac charge based on things like credit score and down payment. They show up as a higher rate or higher points.

Pulte did not say which LLPAs he is reviewing or which loans would be affected. He skipped the MBA convention in Chicago last week, where he was reportedly expected to announce that lenders could deliver loans with two credit reports instead of three.

Expand 3-minute read

Fannie and Freddie pricing could change before the end of 2026. Here is what was said and what it could mean.

FHFA Director Bill Pulte posted a series of messages on X on Saturday, per HousingWire's write-up on Monday. The key quote: "we are studying the usage of just one or two credit reports to bring even more savings than we already have to American consumers." He also said he expects to decide "whether there will be any changes to LLPAs before year end."

Start with credit reports. For decades, conforming loans have used a tri-merge, meaning a report from each of the three credit bureaus. Credit report fees have climbed, and originators have complained loudly. Moving to two reports, called a bi-merge, would cut that cost. FHFA explored this during the Biden administration, but parts of the industry pushed back over concerns about scoring differences and gaps in data. Pulte was reportedly expected to announce a two-report option at the MBA Annual Convention in Chicago last week. He canceled his appearance instead.

Now LLPAs. Loan-level price adjustments are the fees Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac charge based on risk factors like credit score, loan-to-value and loan purpose. Borrowers usually do not see them as a line item. They show up as a higher rate or more points. Pulte did not say which LLPAs he is looking at or which loan channels could be affected. He framed the review as part of a push to "make housing more accessible." FHFA recently merged the pricing grids for loans delivered with FICO Classic or VantageScore 4.0 into one grid.

There is more possibly coming. HousingWire says Pulte's absence from the MBA convention was also tied to rumors of an executive order on affordability, including moves to reduce mortgage insurance and closing costs on some refinances. That is a rumor, not a policy. Pulte said in a statement that his job is traveling to states and delivering housing resources, and he denied media reports that he wants to be White House chief of staff.

Why lenders are paying attention. MBA expects origination volume to fall 5% in 2027 to less than $2 trillion, with rates forecast above 7%. In a market that tight, any cut to fees that borrowers pay at closing can help a deal pencil. It also fits MBA's own affordability roadmap from last week, which asked for lower FHA premiums and LLPAs and a single-file credit option for borrowers with scores above 700.

What to tell clients right now. Nothing has changed on today's loans. Do not quote future savings or advise anyone to wait for a pricing change that may never come or may be small. If LLPA cuts arrive, they would likely help borrowers with lower scores or smaller down payments the most, since those loans carry the biggest add-ons today. That makes it a good topic to explain on the show: what LLPAs are, why two people get different rates on the same day, and what to watch for by year end.

6) Renting wins on monthly cost: buying runs $771 a month more than renting as Zillow rental demand jumps 19.2% from a year ago

Wed show

So-what: The rent-versus-buy math is tough at 7%-plus rates, and would-be buyers are renting single-family homes instead. The case for buying now has to rest on long-term goals, not month-one savings.

1-minute read

Higher rates are pushing would-be buyers into rentals, and the monthly numbers explain why.

Zillow's rental demand index rose 19.2% in September from a year ago, per Scotsman Guide. Demand for single-family rentals rose 21.6%, more than double the 10.1% rise for apartments. People still want a house. Many are just renting one.

Here is the cost gap. The typical monthly cost of buying a home rose 5% from a year ago to $3,037. The typical cost of renting a home rose 3.1% to $2,266. That makes buying $771 a month more expensive than renting. Typical rent across all rentals was $1,932, up 2.7%.

California stands out. Rental demand jumped 82.5% in San Jose and 66.9% in San Francisco. Bay Area rents are rising fastest too, up 11.8% in San Francisco and 8.3% in San Jose.

Rents rose year over year in 49 of the 50 largest metros. So renters are not escaping rising costs. They are trading a bigger payment now for a smaller one that still climbs.

For buyers like Emily and Jake, this is the honest framing: buying usually costs more per month today, and the payoff is a fixed payment and equity over time.

Expand 3-minute read

This one is useful for the rent-versus-buy conversation that comes up with almost every first-time buyer.

Scotsman Guide summarized Zillow's September rental report on Monday. The headline number is Zillow's Observed Renter Demand Index, which tracks how much interest there is in rentals. It rose 19.2% from a year ago. Zillow's explanation is simple: higher borrowing costs are pushing people who might have bought into renting instead.

The preference for houses has not gone away. Demand for single-family rentals rose 21.6% from a year ago, more than double the 10.1% increase for apartment rentals. In plain terms, many of the people who would have been buyers are renting the same kind of home they wanted to buy.

Now the monthly math. Zillow found the typical cost of buying a home rose 5% from a year ago to $3,037 a month. The typical cost of renting a home, as opposed to an apartment, rose 3.1% to $2,266. That gap is $771 a month in favor of renting. Across all rental types, typical rent was $1,932, up 2.7%. So buying costs are rising almost twice as fast as rents.

California is at the center of this. San Jose had the biggest jump in rental demand of any large metro, up 82.5%. San Francisco was second at 66.9%, followed by Austin at 49.3%, Miami at 48.9% and Phoenix at 43%. Bay Area rents are also rising fastest, up 11.8% in San Francisco and 8.3% in San Jose. For single-family homes specifically, San Jose rents rose 7.4% and San Francisco 6.7%.

Not every market is hot. Rental demand fell 6.2% in Louisville, 6.1% in Baltimore and 5.6% in Washington, D.C. But rents still rose from a year ago in 49 of the 50 largest metros.

How to use this with clients. First, be honest. At today's rates, buying usually costs more per month than renting a similar home, and Zillow puts that gap at $771. Pretending otherwise costs trust. Second, the real comparison is over time. A fixed-rate mortgage payment stays put, apart from taxes and insurance, while rent has been rising almost everywhere. Part of each mortgage payment also builds equity. Third, the rental crowd is the pipeline. Renters who wanted a house are exactly the audience for a plan that gets them ready to buy, whether that is fixing credit, building a down payment or understanding what a refinance could do if rates fall later.

One caution on tone. We should not promise lower rates ahead. The point is that the decision should rest on how long someone plans to stay, their budget and their goals, not on beating the rent bill in the first month.

This is a strong show segment because it uses a clear number, $771, and speaks directly to the renter who is wondering whether to keep waiting.

A third major forecast for 2027 is out, and it adds to the higher-for-longer picture that lenders have been bracing for.

Here is what HousingWire reported from Bank of America's research note, released Friday, Oct. 16. BofA expects $1.719 trillion in mortgage originations in 2027. That would be down 15% from the $2.030 trillion it expects this year. Refinances would make up only about $344 billion, roughly one dollar in five. The rest would be mostly purchase loans.

The forecast rests on a few key assumptions. BofA expects the 10-year Treasury yield to average 5.25% in 2027. It expects existing-home sales to fall 2.5%, new-home sales to rise only 0.5%, and home prices to rise 1%. HousingWire's headline says the bank warns of a 2023-style housing replay, which was a year of high rates, very low home sales and slim refinance volume.

The rate assumption is the part to watch. The 10-year was 5.103% this morning per MBS Live, and it closed Monday at 5.143%. So BofA is assuming rates average a bit higher than today for all of next year. If oil stays high and inflation stays sticky, that assumption looks reasonable. Nobody knows which path wins, which is why it helps to compare several forecasts.

Other signs point the same way. Scotsman Guide ran a piece this week titled "2027 refinance market could be worse," quoting Optimal Blue's Mike Vough, though the full article is paywalled. Practical steps: stay close to agents, keep a pipeline of renters who want to buy, and keep a list of past clients with higher rates in case rates do drop. Those clients would be first in line if the refi window opens even briefly.

For borrowers, the forecast is not a reason to wait. It suggests rates may not drop much next year. Buyers who can afford a home now should plan around today's rates, with a refinance as a possible bonus later, not a plan.

So-what: Affordability is slowly improving because pay is rising while prices stall, not because prices are falling. That is a long, flat road, so buyers should not wait for a crash.

1-minute read

Homes are slowly getting more affordable compared with paychecks, but very slowly.

Bill McBride at Calculated Risk updated his house prices to income charts on Monday evening. The Social Security national average wage index for 2025 was $72,800.33, up 4.2% from $69,846.57 in 2024. Wages are up almost 31% over the last five years.

Compared with that wage index, the Case-Shiller home price index is about 7.5% below its 2022 peak. Compared with median household income, it is about 11% below the peak.

In plain terms, prices have mostly gone flat while incomes kept rising. That is how affordability improves without a crash.

McBride's forecast is memorable: "I expect house prices to spend 7 years in purgatory." He means prices may move sideways for years while incomes catch up.

Two cautions. This is about prices and pay only. It does not include mortgage rates, which are above 7% and still the biggest hurdle for most buyers. And it is a national picture, so local markets differ.

For Emily and Jake, the message is steady. Waiting for prices to collapse may mean waiting years for a drop that never comes.

This is one of the most useful long-run affordability measures, and Calculated Risk's update on Monday evening adds fresh wage data.

Bill McBride compares home prices with incomes in two ways. The first uses the Social Security Administration's national average wage index. For 2025, that index was $72,800.33, up 4.2% from $69,846.57 in 2024. Over the last five years, wages are up almost 31%. That is a big rise in pay, and it is the main reason affordability has started to improve.

Next he divides the Case-Shiller home price index by that wage index. By that measure, house prices are about 7.5% below their 2022 peak. In other words, compared with what the average worker earns, homes cost about 7.5% less than they did at the peak.

The second comparison uses median household income instead of the average wage. By that measure, prices are about 11% below the peak. The two numbers differ because household income and individual wages grow at different speeds, but both point the same direction.

Here is the key point. Home prices have not fallen much nationally. What changed is that pay kept rising while prices mostly went flat. That is a slow way for affordability to improve, but it is real.

McBride's outlook is the quote for the show: "I expect house prices to spend 7 years in purgatory." Purgatory here means a long stretch of flat or slow prices while incomes catch up. It is not a crash forecast. It is a forecast of a long, boring market.

This fits other recent data. Monday's brief noted ResiClub's report that home prices slipped in September and that some large metros are down from a year ago. Builders are cutting prices, with 43% doing so in October per NAHB. Those are signs of a flat market, not a collapse.

There are two big limits to this measure. First, it ignores mortgage rates. A home that is 7.5% cheaper relative to wages can still cost more each month if rates are much higher than they were. With Freddie Mac's 30-year average at 7.40% as of Oct. 15, the monthly payment is still the main hurdle for most buyers. Second, it is a national average. Coastal California markets can look very different from the country as a whole.

How to use this with clients. For buyers waiting for prices to drop a lot, the honest answer is that this data and McBride's outlook suggest a long flat stretch is more likely than a crash. Waiting could mean paying rent for years while prices stay roughly the same. For buyers who can afford today's payment, rising incomes over time make a fixed mortgage payment easier to carry. And if rates come down later, a refinance could help, though nobody should count on it.

This is a strong Wednesday segment because it gives viewers one simple idea: affordability is improving through pay raises, not price cuts, and it may take years.

This is an opinion piece in HousingWire, not a news report, but it raises a question that comes up with many of our clients.

The headline is "The borrowers traditional underwriting wasn't built for." The argument is that standard mortgage underwriting was designed around steady W-2 paychecks, and a growing share of Americans do not earn that way.

The author cites Upwork's 2026 Future Workforce Index, which says more than 76 million Americans freelance. Their income can be steady over a year but lumpy month to month, and it often looks smaller on tax returns because of business deductions.

The piece also points to a gap in first-time buyer data. NAR's annual survey says the typical first-time buyer is a record age 40. MBA's loan-level data puts the typical first-time buyer near age 32. Those are very different pictures. One way to read it is that surveys and actual loan records are capturing different groups of people. Either way, it is a reminder to be careful with headline statistics about who is buying.

Why this matters for underwriting. On a standard conforming loan, self-employed income is usually documented with tax returns, and the qualifying number is what is left after write-offs. A freelancer who earns well but deducts a lot can end up qualifying for far less than their real cash flow suggests. That is where many self-employed buyers get stuck.

There are alternatives. Non-QM loans, meaning loans outside the standard Fannie, Freddie and government rules, include bank statement programs that look at deposits instead of tax returns, and 1099 programs that look at gross pay reported by clients. These can open doors, but they usually come with higher rates, larger down payments or both. The right choice depends on the borrower's numbers, so it is worth running both paths side by side.

There is also a planning angle. A self-employed buyer who is a year or two away from buying can work with a tax preparer on how write-offs affect qualifying income. Sometimes a conventional loan is possible with better planning. Sometimes a non-QM loan now and a refinance later makes more sense.

A caution on tone. The piece is advocacy, and its numbers are survey-based. It does not show default data or prove that alternative underwriting is safer or riskier. We should present it as a perspective, not as settled fact.

For BuyWise and the show, this is a natural fit. Many viewers who think they cannot qualify are self-employed or have side income. A short segment on how lenders count that income, and what options exist when tax returns understate it, could help a group that banks often turn away.

10) Opendoor tests a 2% down shared-equity 'Own' program in North Carolina: fixed payment for five years, no mortgage at move-in

So-what: Low down payment alternatives are getting creative as buyers thin out. For most clients who can qualify, a 3% down conventional or 3.5% FHA loan keeps all the appreciation.

1-minute read

Opendoor is trying a new way to sell homes to buyers who do not have much cash.

The company is piloting a program called Own in North Carolina with Ownify, a co-investment platform, per HousingWire. Buyers put 2% down, skip the traditional mortgage at move-in and pay one fixed monthly amount for five years.

Part of that payment buys more equity each month. The rest is an occupancy fee that covers maintenance, taxes, insurance and HOA dues. A capital partner funds the rest of the purchase. Buyers can buy the remaining share any time at fair market value, set by averaging three appraisals.

To qualify, buyers generally need a 660 credit score and $60,000 in household income. Homes are limited to Opendoor-owned houses from $200,000 to $750,000 in Charlotte, Raleigh-Durham and the Piedmont Triad. The waitlist is closed for now.

Industry voices are skeptical. A Stratmor managing director called it "basically a rent-to-own model with some skin in the game." A consultant asked why someone who qualifies for 3% down FHA would give up part of their appreciation.

Opendoor also bought far more homes than it sold in Q2: 4,378 bought versus 2,339 sold.

Expand 3-minute read

Shared-equity and rent-to-own ideas keep popping up as buyers get harder to find. Opendoor's new pilot is the latest, and it is worth understanding before a client asks about it.

Here is how it works, per HousingWire's reporting on Monday. Opendoor is testing a program called Own in North Carolina with Ownify, a fractional homeownership platform founded in 2022. Qualified buyers put 2% down and get an equity stake on day one. There is no traditional mortgage at move-in. Instead, buyers make one fixed monthly payment for five years.

That payment has two parts. One part buys a little more equity each month. The other is an occupancy fee that covers maintenance, property taxes, insurance and HOA fees. A capital partner pays the rest of the price and typical closing costs. Ownify says its fund co-invests to bring the down payment and monthly costs 10% to 15% below a mortgage, and it targets a 10% to 14% return for its investors.

Buyers can purchase the rest of the home at any point in the five years at fair market value, which is the average of three independent appraisals. If they move before buying out, Opendoor cashes out their equity at fair market value minus a relisting fee. That fee is highest in the first 30 months and drops after that. After five years, buyers can renew, but Opendoor has not said what pricing or underwriting looks like after that.

Eligibility is narrow. Applicants generally need a 660 credit score and at least $60,000 in household income. Only Opendoor-owned single-family homes and townhomes priced from $200,000 to $750,000 in Charlotte, Raleigh-Durham and the Piedmont Triad qualify. The waitlist is currently closed, and Opendoor declined to comment on a product in beta.

The skeptics have good points. Coby Hakalir of Stratmor called it "basically a rent-to-own model with some skin in the game for the buyer, similar to a land contract," and said it is not scalable "without a ton of capital." Consultant Craig McClelland noted it lowers the floor to 2% from 3% for FHA, and asked: "if you can do 3% and qualify for FHA, then why would you do a program like this?" The buyer gives up part of the appreciation, and he doubts many people will accept that unless the payment is meaningfully lower.

Why Opendoor is doing it. In the second quarter it bought 4,378 homes and sold only 2,339, ending with 5,459 homes in inventory. McClelland does not see distress. He sees a way to drive more inbound inquiries and lower the cost of finding buyers.

For our clients in California, this program does not apply. But the questions are the ones to ask about any shared-equity offer. What is the payment after year five? How much equity do you really keep? What does it cost to leave early? Compare that with a 3% down conventional loan or 3.5% down FHA, where the buyer keeps all the appreciation.

This is the bigger picture behind the day-to-day rate moves, and it explains why bonds have been so jumpy.

Start with MBS Live. Matt Graham's Tuesday Morning note says the bond market is deciding "who it wants to be" in the fourth quarter. One version worries about inflation, debt issuance and growth, which pushes yields up. The other is a safe haven for cash freed up as companies cut back on AI spending, which pushes yields down. This morning, with the Nasdaq lower, the second version is winning slightly and UMBS 6.0 is up 0.15. On Monday, Graham said that whenever tech worries are "anything less than dire," yields have cautiously drifted higher. The 10-year rose about 6 basis points Monday to 5.143%.

The newest data point is in credit. Per the Boock Report, CoreWeave's five-year credit default swaps closed Monday at 1,089 basis points, down 38 on the day. In plain terms, it costs almost $1.1 million a year for five years to insure $10 million of CoreWeave bonds against default. Boockvar says that "implies about a 55% chance of default." Meanwhile, the stock market still values the company at about $35 billion. His quote: "the stand off between credit and equity participants is quite fascinating." Last week our brief had those swaps at 1,117, so the fear eased slightly but remains extreme.

There was another crack overseas. Elice, a South Korean AI cloud company, priced its IPO at 90,500 won and closed its first day at 61,500 won, a 32% drop. Weak demand for new AI deals is the kind of signal that can send money back toward bonds.

Banks are paying attention to rates, too. Fifth Third reported net charge-offs of $139 million, or 31 basis points, and said: "We are focused on limiting exposures most sensitive to higher rates." That is a lender quietly planning for higher-for-longer.

The longer view comes from two directions. Mauldin's Over My Shoulder on Monday featured money manager Vitaliy Katsenelson, who says an AI hangover is coming. His comparison is COVID, when companies like Peloton invested as if temporary demand were permanent. He believes AI buyers are building more capacity than will be needed, and he points to the decade-long telecom slump after 1999. Economist Guy Berger at MacroMostly is calmer on jobs. He is "reasonably confident (not 100% sure)" AI will not cause persistent mass unemployment, though he expects "significant gross labor market disruption." So far, he says, AI's labor impact "has on average been very small." That matters because a sudden AI-driven jobs shock is one of the things that could pull rates down fast, and Berger does not see one yet.

MBS Live's list of what would help rates fall the most includes "AI/tech credit/earnings bubble concerns playing out in markets," alongside a recession and lower oil. WSJ headlines fit the theme, such as "The Weak Points in AI Bonds Are Becoming Clearer," though we have not read those stories.

The other force has not gone away. November crude hit a new cycle high near $109 this morning, per the Boock Report, and higher energy costs feed inflation worries that push yields up.

What this means for locks. The quick rate improvements lately have come from fear about AI and tech credit. That can happen again, but nobody can time it, and on calm days yields have drifted higher. For a borrower closing soon, hoping for a tech scare is not a strategy. MBS Live's lock stance remains defensive.
